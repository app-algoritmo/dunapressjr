## Belgium vs. Brussels: De Wever Rejects Russian Assets Loan Over €140B Debt Risk

In the labyrinthine corridors of European power, where national interests often collide with collective imperatives, Belgium finds itself at a precarious crossroads. As of late 2025, the Belgian government is locked in a tense standoff with the European Union's institutions in Brussels—literally on its own doorstep—over the fate of approximately €210 billion in frozen Russian central bank assets. These funds, immobilized since Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, have become a financial weapon in the West's arsenal against Moscow. Yet, for Belgium, they represent not just a moral quandary but a potential fiscal catastrophe: a looming €140 billion debt bomb if legal challenges from Russia succeed. Flemish nationalist leader Bart De Wever, a pivotal figure in Belgium's fractious coalition politics, has drawn a line in the sand, declaring unequivocally that using these assets to back a massive loan for Ukraine "will never happen." His words, echoed in a recent interview with the Belga News Agency, underscore a deeper rift: the tension between EU-wide solidarity and the raw calculus of national liability.

To grasp the stakes, one must rewind to the war's early days. As Russian tanks rolled into Ukraine, Western nations swiftly froze around €300 billion in assets belonging to Russia's central bank, primarily held in European depositories. The bulk—some €210 billion—sits in the vaults of Euroclear, a Brussels-based clearing house that processes international securities transactions. Euroclear, technically a Belgian entity under national jurisdiction, became an unwitting custodian of this frozen fortune. The EU, in coordination with G7 partners, moved quickly to harness the windfall. By mid-2024, a mechanism was devised to skim profits from these immobilized reserves—estimated at €3-5 billion annually—to fund Ukraine's defense and reconstruction. This was a clever sidestep: touching only the interest, not the principal, to skirt international law on sovereign immunity.

But ambition escalated. At the G7 summit in June 2024, leaders pledged a €50 billion loan to Kyiv, extraordinary in its scope and backed by the anticipated revenue stream from those frozen assets. The European Commission, headquartered in Brussels, championed the plan as a "solidarity loan," framing it as an ethical imperative in the face of Russian aggression. For Ukraine, battered by over three years of war, the infusion promised critical breathing room: bolstering energy infrastructure, arming frontline troops, and rebuilding shattered cities like Kharkiv and Mariupol. EU foreign policy chief Josep Borrell hailed it as "history's largest sanctions-based financing," a testament to Europe's resolve.

Enter Belgium's hesitation, a microcosm of broader fissures within the bloc. Euroclear's holdings make Belgium the single largest host of these assets, exposing it to outsized risk. If Russia mounts a successful legal challenge—through bodies like the International Court of Justice or even domestic Belgian courts—the host nation could be held liable for restitution. Russian officials, including Finance Minister Anton Siluanov, have vowed retaliation, labeling the scheme "theft" and promising countermeasures that could include asset seizures abroad or disrupted energy flows. Legal experts, such as those at the European Council on Foreign Relations, warn that while profit-skimming enjoys broad legal cover under the EU's June 2024 assets directive, dipping into the principal for a loan crosses a red line. Sovereign assets, they argue, are protected by customary international law, and any breach could invite chaos in global finance.

This is where the €140 billion figure crystallizes into a nightmare scenario. It's not the full €210 billion—Belgium's exposure is calibrated to its share—but a conservative estimate from Belgian Treasury analyses, factoring in potential multipliers from compound interest, legal fees, and retaliatory sanctions. In a worst-case ruling, Belgium might shoulder not just repayment but punitive damages, crippling its €600 billion national debt (already hovering at 105% of GDP in 2025). Finance Minister Vincent Van Peteghem, from the centrist Open VLD party, has repeatedly flagged this in EU Council meetings, insisting on ironclad indemnification from fellow member states. "We cannot be the EU's sacrificial lamb," he stated in a September 2025 parliamentary hearing, his voice laced with the quiet fury of a nation wedged between economic giants.

Bart De Wever, leader of the New Flemish Alliance (N-VA) and de facto kingmaker in Belgium's hung parliament since the June 2024 elections, has amplified this defiance. Now serving as deputy prime minister in a fragile seven-party "Arizona" coalition, De Wever's influence looms large. His Belga interview, published on October 28, 2025, was a masterclass in populist restraint: "Using frozen Russian assets for a Ukraine loan 'will never happen' under my watch. It's fiscal suicide dressed as solidarity." De Wever, a historian by training with a penchant for dissecting EU overreach, frames the dispute as emblematic of Brussels' disdain for smaller states. "Belgium isn't a colony of the Commission," he quipped, invoking the Flemish-Walloon divide that has long paralyzed national governance. His N-VA, euroskeptic yet pro-Ukraine, polls at 25% in Flanders, positioning him as a potential prime minister in the next reshuffle.

The clash isn't abstract; it's visceral. Brussels, the EU's de facto capital, exerts gravitational pull on Belgium's politics, yet the two are uneasy bedfellows. The city's 1.2 million residents, a polyglot mix of Eurocrats, expats, and locals, embody the bloc's contradictions: gleaming glass towers of the Berlaymont host dreamers of "ever-closer union," while nearby Molenbeek neighborhoods grapple with radicalization and inequality. Belgium's federal structure—six governments juggling competencies—mirrors this schizophrenia. The frozen assets saga has inflamed linguistic fault lines: Flemish parties like N-VA decry Walloon socialists' perceived softness on Russia, while French-speaking liberals push for EU alignment to secure jobs in the capital.

Economically, the pressure is mounting. Euroclear, employing 1,500 in Brussels, warns of reputational hemorrhage if perceived as a political pawn. CEO Philippe Morel penned an open letter to Commission President Ursula von der Leyen in August 2025, pleading for neutrality: "We safeguard assets, not wage wars." Meanwhile, Belgium's export-driven economy—chocolate, diamonds, and chemicals—relies on stable global finance. A Russian backlash could spike energy prices (Belgium imports 80% of its gas via Dutch hubs) or trigger capital flight. The National Bank of Belgium's 2025 stress tests project a 2-3% GDP hit in a full-blown legal dispute, exacerbating inflation at 2.8%.

Yet, Brussels isn't backing down. Von der Leyen's team, eyeing the 2026 EU budget, views the loan as non-negotiable. Diplomatic cables leaked to Politico in October 2025 reveal arm-twisting: threats to withhold structural funds or fast-track infringement proceedings against Belgium for "insufficient sanctions enforcement." Foreign Minister Hadja Lahbib, a vocal Ukraine advocate, has shuttled between the Schuman roundabout and the Palais de la Nation, brokering compromises like a shared EU guarantee fund. But De Wever dismisses it as "smoke and mirrors," demanding veto rights over any principal use—a non-starter in the Commission's supranational playbook.

This impasse echoes historical precedents. Recall the 2015 Greek debt crisis, where Athens buckled under Troika pressure, or Hungary's 2022 veto on Ukraine aid amid Orbán's Moscow flirtations. Belgium, however, punches differently: as home to NATO and EU HQs, it wields soft power. Prime Minister Alexander De Croo, balancing coalitions, has leveraged this, hosting a mini-summit in Bruges last month where Dutch and Luxembourgish counterparts voiced similar qualms. The Benelux union, dormant since Brexit, stirs anew as a bulwark against "Brussels know-it-alls."

For Ukraine, the delay stings. President Volodymyr Zelenskyy's October 2025 videoconference with De Croo pleaded for speed: "Every day without funds costs lives." Aid fatigue grips Europe—polls show 55% of Belgians favor de-escalation with Russia—yet moral suasion persists. NGOs like the International Crisis Group argue the loan's risks are overstated; precedents from Iran's frozen assets in 2016 yielded no blowback.

As winter looms, with Russian drones hammering Kyiv's grid, the "who wins" question hangs heavy. De Wever's intransigence could fracture the coalition, forcing snap elections where N-VA surges. Or Brussels blinks, diluting the loan to pure profits, preserving unity at half-measure. Legal scholars like Yanis Varoufakis, now advising Flemish think tanks, predict a hybrid: ring-fenced assets under a new EU vehicle, diluting Belgian exposure.

Belgium's bind illuminates the EU's original sin: ambition outpacing accountability. Born from coal and steel to bind Franco-German foes, the Union now grapples with hybrid threats—sanctions as swords, assets as shields. De Wever's stand, pragmatic or parochial, revives Gaullist ghosts: Europe of fatherlands, not faceless federation. In a multipolar world, where Trump's shadow lengthens and China's yuan challenges the euro, such national pushback may prove prescient. If Belgium holds firm, it won't just avert debt; it'll recalibrate the bloc's center of gravity, reminding that sovereignty, even in the heart of Europe, dies hard.

The saga's human toll underscores the abstraction's bite. In Antwerp's docks, where Russian oil once flowed, workers eye layoffs from disrupted trade. In Ukrainian exile communities in Ghent, families parse headlines with dread. De Wever, ever the tactician, knows the optics: a patriot shielding taxpayers, not a Russophile. But victory's pyrrhic—alienating allies, emboldening Putin.

As November 2025 unfolds, with COP30 in Belém casting green shadows on fossil feuds, Belgium's fight endures. It's a reminder: in the EU's grand design, small states aren't footnotes. They're fulcrums. And in this tug-of-war over frozen fortunes, the winner may redefine not just aid, but alliance.
