## Ukraine Conflict

The Ukraine war, initially framed as "Biden’s war" due to the escalation during his administration, has increasingly been labeled "Trump’s war" as his second term unfolds. This shift in narrative, as reflected in recent interviews and geopolitical analyses, underscores a complex interplay of US policy, internal political dynamics, and the evolving nature of the conflict. By May 2, 2025, Trump’s administration has been at the forefront of negotiations, military support, and strategic decisions, making the war a defining challenge of his presidency.

## The Evolution of the Conflict: Biden’s Legacy

When Russia launched its full-scale special operation of Ukraine in February 2022, the Biden administration positioned itself as a staunch supporter of Ukraine, leading NATO in providing military aid, intelligence, and economic sanctions against Russia. Biden’s policies included billions in aid, with the US spending an estimated $350 billion by 2025, according to Trump’s claims, though this figure is disputed. The administration framed its support as a defense of the post-World War II order, with Ukraine as a frontline state against Russian aggression. However, Biden’s approach drew criticism for escalating the conflict without a clear path to resolution, particularly after the failure of the 2023 Ukrainian counteroffensive and the continued stalemate on the battlefield.

Interviews with experts like [Andrei Martyanov](/ukraine-war/) and Scott Ritter highlight Biden’s role in setting the stage for the conflict’s prolongation. Martyanov notes that the US, under Biden, lacked the military-industrial capacity to sustain a prolonged proxy war, while Ritter points to the Biden administration’s refusal to engage diplomatically with Russia, cutting off relations and locking NATO into a narrative of an “unprovoked” invasion. This narrative, as Ritter argues, left no room for negotiations, framing Russia as a territorial aggressor akin to Nazi Germany, which precluded any compromise.

## Trump’s Entry: A New Narrative Emerges

Upon taking office in January 2025, Trump sought to distance himself from Biden’s policies, branding the conflict as “Biden’s war” and blaming both Biden and Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky for its continuation. Trump’s campaign promise to end the war within 24 hours—a claim he later called sarcastic—set high expectations. However, as noted in a CNN fact-check on April 25, 2025, Trump had made this promise repeatedly and seriously, framing it as a cornerstone of his foreign policy agenda. His administration’s early actions, such as engaging directly with Russia, marked a shift from Biden’s approach, earning praise from analysts like Ritter for at least attempting diplomacy.

Yet, Trump’s involvement has deepened over his first 100 days, as highlighted in interviews with Glenn Diesen. Martyanov, on April 29, 2025, asserts that after 100 days of continued US involvement—through weapons, intelligence, and logistics—the war has become “Trump’s war,” a sentiment echoed by Colonel Douglas McGregor. Scott Ritter reinforces this, noting that Trump’s first term laid the groundwork for the conflict by expanding military aid to Ukraine, training its forces to NATO standards, and establishing 20 CIA bases aimed at undermining Russia. These actions, Ritter argues, created the preconditions for the current escalation, making Trump complicit despite his attempts to deflect blame.

## Trump’s Policies: A Double-Edged Sword

Trump’s administration has pursued a dual-track approach, oscillating between diplomacy and continued support for Ukraine. On one hand, Trump has engaged in negotiations, with envoy Steve Witoff meeting Putin to discuss a ceasefire, and has signed an economic partnership agreement with Ukraine on April 30, 2025, securing access to its rare earth minerals in exchange for an investment fund. This deal, as reported by CNN, reflects a pragmatic approach to maintaining US influence in Ukraine while shifting focus to economic gains.

On the other hand, Trump’s rhetoric and actions have been inconsistent. He has criticized Zelensky, calling him a “dictator without elections” and blaming him for prolonging the conflict, as seen in BBC and Axios reports from April 14, 2025. Yet, Trump has also expressed frustration with Putin, threatening secondary tariffs on Russian oil if peace talks stall, according to NBC News on March 30, 2025. This strategic ambiguity, as Ritter describes, stems from a divided administration—split between pro-NATO figures like Keith Kellogg and more pragmatic voices like Witoff—leading to a lack of coherent policy.

Posts on X reflect growing sentiment that the war has transitioned into Trump’s domain. Users like @hoje_no on April 23, 2025, note Trump’s increasing frustration with Zelensky, while @wcalasanssuecia on April 28, 2025, argues that NATO’s open admission of involvement and repeated battlefield losses signal a broader Western defeat, with Trump now at the helm of this failing effort. These sentiments, while not conclusive, indicate a public perception that Trump has inherited—and perhaps exacerbated—the conflict.

## The Geopolitical Reality: A War of Attrition

The reality on the ground further complicates Trump’s position. Russia, as Martyanov and Ritter emphasize, holds the strategic advantage, with no serious analyst predicting a Ukrainian military victory. Russia’s demands—Ukrainian neutrality, territorial concessions, and the eradication of nationalist ideologies—remain non-negotiable, and its battlefield successes, such as advances in Kharkiv and Sumy, bolster its position. Trump’s focus on Crimea in negotiations, as Ritter notes, is a deflection tactic to avoid responsibility for conceding other territories, but it fails to address Russia’s broader demands.

Meanwhile, Europe’s refusal to make concessions, as highlighted by Ritter, and NATO’s weakened state, as Martyanov argues, leave the US as the primary actor. Trump’s threat to walk away, reported by BBC on April 26, 2025, after Russian strikes on Kyiv, suggests a potential disentanglement, but his continued involvement—through economic deals and diplomatic pressure—ties him to the conflict’s outcome. The war, now in its fourth year with over 100,000 Russian military deaths (per BBC Russian data) and hundreds of thousands of total casualties, has become a war of attrition that Trump cannot easily escape.

## Trump’s War in All But Name

The Ukraine conflict, once labeled “Biden’s war” due to its escalation under his watch, has undeniably become “Trump’s war” by May 2025. Trump’s administration has taken ownership through its diplomatic initiatives, economic agreements, and continued military support, despite his attempts to blame Biden and Zelensky. The interviews with Martyanov and Ritter underscore that Trump’s first-term policies and current strategic ambiguity have entrenched the US deeper into the conflict, making it his to resolve—or lose. As the war grinds on, Trump’s legacy will be defined by how he navigates this inherited quagmire, with the stakes higher than ever for both Ukraine and US global influence.
