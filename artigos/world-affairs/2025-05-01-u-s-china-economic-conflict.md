## **Understanding China’s Economic Rise and the U.S.-China Economic Conflict

Glenn Diesen Interview Einar Tangen about Geopolitical Consequences of the US-China Economic War

China’s economic transformation over the past few decades is one of the most remarkable stories in modern history. From a resource-scarce nation with limited financial infrastructure to a global economic powerhouse, China’s journey has been shaped by meticulous planning, hard work, and strategic reinvestment. However, as China’s rise continues, it faces increasing economic tensions with the United States, which perceives its dominance as under threat. This article explores China’s economic development, the evolving U.S.-China economic conflict, and the broader implications for global stability, drawing on insights from Aar Tangan, a senior fellow at the Thai Institute and founder of Asia Narratives.

## China’s Economic Development: From Poverty to Power

China’s economic ascent began in earnest after its accession to the World Trade Organization (WTO) in 2001, which Tangan describes as a period of “expansion on steroids.” Double-digit growth became the norm, fueled by a combination of strategic planning and an abundant supply of labor. The Chinese government’s five-year plans provided a clear roadmap, prioritizing infrastructure development and industrialization. Projects like highways were financed through innovative models such as “build-own-transfer,” ensuring efficient allocation of resources without the cronyism often seen elsewhere.

The backbone of China’s growth was its workforce, particularly migrant workers who left rural areas for urban centers. These workers endured grueling conditions, often working 12- to 14-hour days, but their efforts translated into tangible improvements. Tangan recounts meeting workers who celebrated being able to eat meat several times a week, a stark contrast to their earlier deprivation. This perspective underscores the resilience and determination that drove China’s progress.

Education also played a pivotal role. Unlike the U.S., where literacy rates and educational attainment have stagnated (with 60% of the population reading at a sixth-grade level or below), China boasts near-universal literacy among those under 45 and a strong emphasis on higher education. This investment in human capital has enabled China to transition from low-cost manufacturing to innovation-driven industries, challenging the outdated cliché that “the U.S. innovates, China replicates.”

However, China’s development has not been without challenges. Despite its economic strides, social progress has lagged, and disparities between urban and rural areas persist. Additionally, China’s resource scarcity—ranking 100th globally in per capita resources—has forced it to rely heavily on manufacturing and trade, making it vulnerable to external disruptions.

## The U.S.-China Economic Conflict: A Clash of Trajectories

The U.S.-China economic relationship is increasingly defined by friction, driven by divergent trajectories and competing visions. The U.S., grappling with a shrinking middle class (from 61% in 1971 to 40% in 2023) and growing economic inequality, feels it has “lost a step.” Populist sentiments, exemplified by former President Donald Trump, resonate with those disillusioned by stagnant opportunities and a ballooning deficit. In contrast, China’s disciplined planning and reinvestment have propelled it forward, fostering a sense of earned progress.

A key flashpoint is the U.S.’s attempt to curb China’s technological advancement, particularly in semiconductors. Tangan notes that U.S. restrictions on technology transfers have backfired, spurring China to develop its own capabilities at a rapid pace. This “boomerang effect” highlights a critical miscalculation: underestimating China’s ability to innovate independently. Tangan attributes this partly to cultural biases, recalling conversations with U.S. executives who dismissed China’s potential, revealing a mix of arrogance and prejudice.

The weaponization of financial systems, such as the SWIFT banking network, adds another layer of tension. While primarily used against nations like Russia, Iran, and Venezuela, China fears it could be next. To hedge against this, China has reduced its holdings of U.S. Treasury bills, engaged in currency swaps, and issued bonds backed by U.S. assets in markets like Saudi Arabia. These moves aim to insulate China from financial coercion while maintaining global trade stability, reflecting its preference for a “steady, stable world.”

China’s dominance in rare earth minerals further complicates the conflict. Controlling over 90% of global processing, China produces higher-purity materials at lower costs, giving it a near-unassailable edge. Tangan emphasizes that replicating this capacity elsewhere would take a decade or more, leaving the U.S. and its allies vulnerable in the interim. Recent Chinese restrictions on exports to South Korea, a conduit for rare earths, underscore China’s ability to retaliate strategically.

## The Risks of Escalation: From Economic to Military Conflict

The U.S.-China economic war carries significant risks of escalation, particularly in militarized contexts like Taiwan or Xinjiang. Tangan dismisses narratives of genocide in Xinjiang, noting its status as China’s second-largest tourist destination and the lack of credible evidence supporting such claims. Instead, he points to U.S.-funded disinformation campaigns, citing the National Endowment for Democracy’s history of supporting regime change in vulnerable nations.

A potential flashpoint is Taiwan, where miscalculations could trigger a broader conflict. Tangan warns of a scenario akin to World War I, where unintended escalations spiral out of control. Beijing’s concern is heightened by the U.S.’s reliance on its military as a “last card” to assert dominance, especially as economic and financial leverage wanes. The recent U.S. declaration of economic measures against 180 nations, including sanctions on Antarctica’s penguins, illustrates a lack of strategic coherence that alarms global observers.

## Toward a Positive-Sum Future

Despite these tensions, Tangan sees potential for de-escalation through pragmatic diplomacy. China’s recent white paper on the failed Phase One trade deal and unilateral tariff reductions on circuitry signal a willingness to negotiate a Phase Two agreement. Such a deal could allow the U.S. to save face while reducing global tariffs, enhancing China’s stature as a responsible global actor. Unlike the U.S.’s zero-sum approach, China advocates for a multipolar world where nations grow together, a philosophy reflected in its partnerships with Russia and BRICS nations.

Tangan contrasts China’s collaborative approach with the U.S.’s “America First” nationalism, which he argues has shifted from optimism to grievance. This inward turn, coupled with a refusal to learn from others, weakens the U.S.’s global standing. Meanwhile, China’s emphasis on mutual respect and shared development, as seen in its restraint in Central Asia, fosters trust among partners.

## A Call for Trust and Cooperation

The U.S.-China economic conflict is a symptom of broader global shifts, with China’s rise challenging the U.S.’s unipolar dominance. While the U.S. struggles with internal decline and external overreach, China’s disciplined planning and strategic investments position it as a formidable competitor. However, the path forward lies not in zero-sum confrontation but in positive-sum cooperation. Tangan’s insights underscore the need for trust, mutual understanding, and a rejection of the “law of the jungle” mentality. Without a U.S. leadership willing to prioritize global stability over dominance, the risk of economic conflict spiraling into something far worse remains a sobering possibility.
