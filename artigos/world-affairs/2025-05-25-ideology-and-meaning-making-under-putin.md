## Ideology and Meaning-Making under Putin: Russia’s Conservative Pivot and Global South Alliance

In a thought-provoking interview with Professor Glenn Diesen, Marlene Laruelle, a research professor at George Washington University and director of Illiberal Studies, discusses her new book, Ideology and Meaning-Making under the Putin Regime. Drawing on over two decades of studying Russia’s ideological landscape, Laruelle offers a comprehensive analysis of how Russia has crafted a national narrative since Vladimir Putin’s rise in 2000. From the ashes of Soviet collapse and Yeltsin’s liberal experiment, Russia has sought to redefine itself as a civilization state, balancing domestic cohesion with global positioning. This article explores Laruelle’s insights into Russia’s ideological evolution, its rejection of Western universalism, and its pivot toward the Global South.

## The Need for Ideology: Nation-Building Post-Soviet Russia

Laruelle frames ideology as a universal necessity, akin to a roadmap shared by a nation’s majority to define identity, history, and purpose. In Russia, this process is both a response to its vast, multi-ethnic complexity and a reaction to the 1990s’ turmoil, marked by economic collapse, political fragmentation, and fears of civil war. The Putin regime, consolidating power in the late 1990s, prioritized a minimal consensus on Russian identity to prevent disintegration. Unlike Soviet Marxism or Yeltsin’s Westernizing liberalism, which both faltered, the new ideology emphasizes state-led stability and moral renewal, addressing the public’s demand for order and social justice post-1990s chaos.

Laruelle highlights a 2021 survey showing 79% of Russians support a state ideology, reflecting a grassroots desire for direction, norms, and moral signaling from the state. This demand, rooted in the perceived amorality of 1990s liberalism, seeks to restore predictability and stability, even if citizens critique state corruption. The regime’s narrative, evolving from flexible experimentation in the early 2000s to a more codified conservatism, serves both domestic cohesion and Russia’s global branding as a distinct civilization.

## Crafting a Civilization State: Navigating Russia’s Complex History

Russia’s ideological challenge lies in reconciling its diverse historical paths—Kievan Rus, Muscovy, Peter the Great’s Europeanization, Slavophile backlash, Soviet universalism, and Yeltsin’s liberalism. Laruelle argues that the concept of a “civilization state” encapsulates this multiplicity, presenting Russia as a unique blend of Slavic, Turkic, and Mongol influences across a transcontinental space. This flexible framework, while an “empty signifier,” allows citizens to project personal meanings onto a shared narrative, unified by state leadership and continuity.

The civilization state narrative bypasses historical ruptures by embracing all eras—tsarist, Soviet, and post-Soviet—as part of Russia’s identity. For example, Putin’s celebration of both Red and White Russians in the Civil War integrates conflicting legacies into a cohesive national story. This approach, Laruelle notes, mirrors other nations’ strategies, like France’s blending of monarchy and revolution, but is uniquely ambitious given Russia’s scale and diversity.

## Rejecting Western Universalism: A Counterrevolutionary Stance

Laruelle identifies Russia’s rejection of Western universalism as a counterrevolutionary response to the 1990s, when political, economic, and cultural liberalism flooded Russia, disrupting its social fabric. Drawing from 19th-century Slavophile and German Romantic traditions, Russia’s civilizationist narrative champions diversity over a homogenizing global liberalism. This stance, articulated by thinkers like Fyodor Dostoevsky and echoed in Putin’s 2013 Valdai speech decrying the West’s rejection of Christian roots, posits Russia as the “real Europe,” preserving traditional values against a decadent, progressive West.

This moral revival, Laruelle argues, is authentic, resonating with Russians who view the 1990s as immoral. It also aligns Russia with Global South nations like China and India, which resist Western normative pressure. By framing itself as an anti-colonial power, Russia taps into a shared sovereignty narrative, advocating for a multipolar world where civilizations follow distinct paths. This rhetoric, rooted in Soviet decolonization and Slavophile anti-imperialism, strengthens Russia’s appeal in Asia, Africa, and Latin America, shielding it from Western sanctions and isolation.

## Conservatism as Russia’s Answer to Liberalism

Russian conservatism, Laruelle explains, counters liberalism’s universalism by emphasizing cultural particularism and stability. Unlike U.S. conservatism, which preserves liberal individualism, Russia’s version seeks to conserve Putin’s post-2000 synthesis of tsarist, Soviet, and post-Soviet identities. Initially a moderate “liberal conservatism” with economic liberalism, it has grown more reactionary, prioritizing regime security and traditional values over progressive change. This shift reflects public trauma from the 1990s, craving predictability over upheaval.

The conservative narrative rejects Western models as inauthentic, building instead on Russia’s historical roots. It positions the state as the moral arbiter, delivering norms even if citizens bypass them, balancing rebellion with order. Laruelle notes tensions within this ecosystem, with moderate conservatives favoring stability and reactionaries pushing for a pre-liberal past, yet both serve the regime’s goal of preserving Putin’s Russia.

## Eurasianism and the Global South: Russia’s New Imaginary

Laruelle’s exploration of Eurasianism highlights its role in redefining Russia’s identity. Traditionally, Eurasianism celebrated Russia’s internal diversity—Slavic, Turkic, and Mongol heritages—valorizing the Mongol period as formative. Today, it emphasizes a transcontinental identity, aligning with China via initiatives like BRICS and pivoting to the Global South. This shift, driven by declining influence in post-Soviet states like Ukraine and Central Asia, rebrands Russia as a bridge between East and West, leveraging its Muslim minorities to engage Islamic nations.

The Global South narrative, framed as anti-colonial, positions Russia as a partner in dismantling Western dominance. Laruelle sees this as a long-term strategy, not mere opportunism, reflecting a genuine belief that the West’s global relevance is waning. By advocating for civilizational diversity and sovereignty, Russia aligns with nations like Brazil and South Africa, fostering a multipolar order where it can maintain great power status despite economic challenges.

## Russia’s Appeal in the West: A Fluid Ideological Marketplace

Laruelle notes Russia’s growing appeal among Western conservatives, who see it as a bulwark against liberal excesses. This convergence, evident in the U.S. with Trump’s illiberalism and Europe’s far-right movements, is not solely Russian influence but a mutual attraction. Russia’s anti-liberal brand—rooted in thinkers like Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, whose 1978 Harvard speech critiqued both communism and capitalism’s spiritual voids—resonates with post-liberal audiences. However, alliances remain tenuous, as seen with figures like Italy’s Giorgia Meloni, who combine illiberalism with pro-NATO stances.

The global ideological marketplace, Laruelle argues, is increasingly fluid, blurring old dichotomies like democracy versus authoritarianism. Russia navigates this postmodern landscape adeptly, engaging far-left, far-right, Muslim, and Christian audiences, unlike the West’s rigid narratives. This fluidity, coupled with Russia’s civilizational rhetoric, positions it as a precursor in redefining global ideological alignments.

Marlene Laruelle’s Ideology and Meaning-Making under the Putin Regime reveals Russia’s ideological journey as a quest for cohesion and global relevance. By crafting a civilization state narrative, Russia reconciles its diverse history, rejects Western universalism, and pivots to the Global South. Its conservative ideology, balancing moral revival with regime stability, counters liberalism’s upheavals, while Eurasianism rebrands Russia as a transcontinental power. As the West grapples with its own cultural divides, Russia’s fluid navigation of a multipolar world offers lessons and challenges. Laruelle’s work underscores that Russia’s search for meaning is both authentic and strategic, shaping its future in a rapidly changing global order.
