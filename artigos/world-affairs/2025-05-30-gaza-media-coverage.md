## NZ Journalists Expose Gaza Genocide Cover-Up by Mainstream Media

In a compelling panel discussion hosted by Pascal Lottaz on Neutrality Studies, New Zealand-based journalists Eugene Doyle, Jeremy Rose, and Ramon Das critique the mainstream media’s coverage of the Gaza genocide, focusing on Radio New Zealand (RNZ). Their article, a response to RNZ’s self-assessment claiming high journalistic standards, exposes systemic bias, omission, and reliance on Western “trusted sources” that obscure Israel’s atrocities. The panel highlights how cultural proximity, selective framing, and lack of Palestinian voices distort public understanding, implicating New Zealand in genocide through military involvement and media silence. This article analyzes their critique, exploring the failures of journalism and potential paths for reform.

## RNZ’s Flawed Self-Assessment

RNZ commissioned an independent review by a former journalist to evaluate its Gaza coverage, prompted by complaints. The report, described by Doyle as having a “nothing to see here” attitude, examined individual grievances already vetted by RNZ, the Media Council, and the Broadcasting Standards Authority, ensuring a foregone conclusion of compliance. The panelists—Doyle, Rose, and Das—disagreed vehemently, arguing it missed the genocide’s enormity. Rose’s analysis revealed RNZ’s database from 2008 to 2025 contained 6,000 Ukraine-related items versus under 3,000 for Gaza, despite Gaza’s four wars and ongoing slaughter since October 2023. This disparity, they contend, reflects a deliberate downplaying of Palestinian suffering.

The report’s mention of “cultural proximity”—positing Israel’s alignment with New Zealand via tourism, trade, and language—shocked the panel. Das called it implicit racism, noting the report’s failure to confront this bias. Rose cited its origins in a 1965 study by Johan Galtung, which linked coverage to power dynamics, not culture, underscoring the report’s misapplication. The panel argues RNZ’s reliance on Western sources like the BBC, Reuters, and The New York Times perpetuates a narrative favoring Israel, sidelining Palestinian perspectives.

## Framing and Omission: A Pernicious Narrative

The panel identifies two critical flaws in RNZ’s coverage: selective framing and omission. Rose highlighted the absence of interviews with Palestinians or Israeli critics, contrasting with pre-2022 coverage. A notable example is RNZ’s three-minute segment with UN Special Rapporteur Francesca Albanese in 2023, where over two minutes featured a BBC clip of an Israeli spokesperson, leaving Albanese under a minute to respond. This, Rose argues, exemplifies a pattern of granting Israel an implicit right of reply, as seen in reports framing hospital bombings as targeting “Hamas bases” without noting discredited claims, like those about Al-Shifa Hospital.

Doyle pointed to RNZ’s use of “retaliatory war” for Israel’s actions, ignoring the 80-year context of Palestinian dispossession since Zionist plans under David Ben-Gurion. Das criticized the false symmetry framing Gaza as a two-way conflict, obscuring Israel’s long-term ethnic cleansing agenda. The panel also noted the stark contrast in terminology: Israeli “hostages” versus Palestinian “prisoners,” and “war” implying symmetry when Gaza faces one-sided devastation. These choices, they argue, dehumanize Palestinians, echoing Western narratives demonizing them as terrorists while sanitizing Israeli actions.

## New Zealand’s Complicity and Media Silence

The panel connects media failures to New Zealand’s complicity in the genocide. Doyle revealed New Zealand’s military involvement, with troops in the Red Sea aiding U.S. strikes on Yemen’s Houthis, often launched from Australian bases. This participation, alongside allies like the U.S., UK, Canada, and Australia—settler colonies like Israel—receives scant coverage, leaving most New Zealanders unaware. The panelists lament RNZ’s omission of Israel’s targeted killings of over 200 journalists in Gaza, a figure surpassing combined journalist deaths in World War II, Korea, Vietnam, and Afghanistan. Reporters Without Borders confirms at least 35 were deliberately targeted, a story RNZ ignores.

Das highlighted Israeli public attitudes, with 82% of Jewish Israelis supporting Gaza’s ethnic cleansing and 56% favoring expelling Israeli Arabs, per a Geocartography poll. These shocking views, absent from RNZ, contrast with Western leaders’ divergence from public sentiment, underscoring Israel’s unique societal support for genocide. The panel argues RNZ’s failure to report such stories violates its public interest mandate, shielding New Zealanders from their government’s role in atrocities.

## The Psychology of Journalistic Failure

The panelists explore why journalists perpetuate this bias. Rose attributes it to cowardice, noting RNZ’s reliance on Western wire services like Reuters and the BBC, which supply pre-framed stories. Individual reports may not be overtly false, but their selective use—favoring Israeli voices like Benjamin Netanyahu over Palestinians—creates a distorted narrative. Doyle cites C.S. Lewis, suggesting journalists rationalize their complicity to maintain a self-image of moral goodness, unwilling to challenge Western moral superiority. Das references Noam Chomsky, arguing rationalization, not deliberate malice, drives coverage, with journalists avoiding stories like the deaths of 200 journalists or World Central Kitchen workers due to their disruptive implications.

The panel contrasts RNZ’s silence with hypothetical outrage if Russia killed similar numbers, highlighting a double standard rooted in power dynamics. Eugene’s anecdote about a USS Blue Ridge visit to Wellington, covered as a “bucket list” trip despite protests against U.S. genocide support, exemplifies media’s normalization of complicity, reducing geopolitics to fluff.

## Reforming Journalism: A Path Forward

The panel proposes reforms to counter these failures. Doyle sees hope in alternative media and public pressure, citing cracks in Western narratives, like the Financial Times labeling Israel’s actions genocidal and Spain’s prime minister condemning Israel. Grassroots movements, historically pivotal in shifting narratives on Vietnam or apartheid, could force mainstream media to adapt as public awareness grows. Rose praises Democracy Now! for its reader-funded model, enabling extensive Palestinian voices, suggesting independent funding could free journalists from state or corporate constraints. Das urges RNZ to adhere to its public interest principles, prioritizing stories about government complicity in genocide over sanitized narratives.

All agree that diversifying voices—Palestinian, Israeli critics, and even genocidal proponents like General Giora Eiland—would enrich coverage, making it less “boring” and more truthful. Das emphasizes moral sensitivity, noting even U.S. Republican senators’ recent objections to Palestinian civilian deaths as a sign of potential change. The panelists advocate for a contested media landscape, amplifying marginalized perspectives to challenge the “guardian” of acceptable narratives.

Eugene Doyle, Jeremy Rose, and Ramon Das expose RNZ’s complicity in obscuring the Gaza genocide through biased framing, omission, and reliance on Western sources. New Zealand’s media, mirroring global counterparts, sanitizes Israel’s atrocities while ignoring the country’s military role, leaving the public uninformed. The panel’s critique reveals a journalism failing its public interest mandate, rationalizing genocide through selective storytelling. Reforming this requires independent funding, diverse voices, and public pressure to break the West’s moral superiority myth. As genocide persists, their call for courageous, truth-driven journalism is a vital step toward accountability and change.
