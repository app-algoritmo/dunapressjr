## Trump’s Trade War Folds: Peter Schiff on China, Debt Crisis, and Smart Investing

In a candid interview with Professor Glenn Diesen, economist and CEO of Euro Pacific Asset Management Peter Schiff dissects President Donald Trump’s recent trade war maneuvers, labeling them a theatrical surrender masquerading as victory. After imposing steep tariffs on China and other nations, only to swiftly remove them, Trump claimed a win, leaving observers puzzled. Schiff argues that the trade war was fundamentally misguided, rooted in a misunderstanding of trade deficits and driven by Trump’s branding prowess rather than economic strategy. This article explores Schiff’s analysis of Trump’s trade war, its implications for U.S.-China relations, and the looming sovereign debt crisis threatening the U.S. economy.

## Trump’s Trade War: A Publicity Stunt, Not a Strategy

Schiff dismisses Trump’s trade war as a “bunch of BS,” arguing that the tariffs—hiked to 145% on China before being dropped—were never about winning but about projecting strength. Trump’s declaration of victory, Schiff contends, mirrors his branding tactics: “declare war, surrender, and call it a victory.” The tariffs, based on flawed calculations tied to trade deficits rather than reciprocal duties, lacked substance. Schiff notes that no significant trade deals emerged, not even with allies like the UK, where the U.S. holds a trade surplus. The episode, he argues, was pure “Trump show”—form over substance, leveraging his marketing skills honed on The Apprentice and his real estate empire.

The trade war’s failure stems from a misdiagnosis of trade deficits, which Schiff attributes to U.S. fiscal and monetary policies—decades of overspending, low interest rates, and excessive borrowing—not foreign cheating or bad deals. By focusing on symptoms rather than the disease, Trump’s approach was doomed, exacerbating economic vulnerabilities while achieving nothing tangible.

## Lessons for China: A Strengthened Resolve

The trade war’s collapse has strained U.S.-China relations, with Schiff highlighting the antagonism of labeling China an “enemy” despite its role as America’s largest lender and supplier. China’s refusal to negotiate under tariff pressure, followed by reciprocal tariffs, forced Trump’s retreat, proving Beijing’s strategic patience. Schiff sees China learning to diversify exports to Europe, Southeast Asia, and Latin America, reduce dollar holdings, and boost domestic consumption to lessen reliance on the U.S. market. This shift, he warns, threatens the U.S., which has long benefited from China’s enabling of its debt-fueled consumption.

China’s vulnerability—holding vast U.S. treasuries—could prompt further divestment, especially given precedents like the seizure of Russian assets in Europe. Schiff suggests China will accelerate de-dollarization to shield itself, a move that could destabilize the U.S. economy, which relies on foreign lending to sustain its deficits.

## The Dollar Dilemma: Strong or Weak?

Schiff critiques Trump’s contradictory stance on the dollar. A strong dollar, necessary to attract foreign lending, fuels trade deficits by making imports cheap and exports uncompetitive. Conversely, a weak dollar could shrink deficits but would spike inflation and interest rates, deterring lenders wary of devalued repayments. Trump’s simultaneous push for a strong dollar and criticism of Federal Reserve rate hikes—calling for cuts that weaken the dollar—reveals a lack of coherence. Schiff argues that the real issue—excessive government spending and low savings—remains unaddressed, as politicians prioritize re-election over painful reforms.

## A Looming Sovereign Debt Crisis

Schiff foresees a “dollar crisis and sovereign debt crisis” as the U.S.’s $37 trillion debt spirals unchecked. With legitimate repayment impossible, the U.S. faces two default paths: an honest restructuring, converting short-term treasuries into low-yield, long-term bonds worth pennies on the dollar, or printing money, triggering hyperinflation that renders repayments worthless. Both scenarios spell disaster for creditors, particularly China. Schiff warns that de-dollarization, already underway, will accelerate, with the crisis unfolding “slowly at first, then all at once,” though the exact timing remains uncertain.

## Where Smart Money Goes: Gold, Silver, and Global Stocks

To navigate the coming crisis, Schiff advises avoiding U.S. government bonds and cryptocurrencies, which he deems speculative “fake money” propped up by hype. Instead, he recommends gold and silver—real assets immune to inflation—and foreign stocks in Europe, Southeast Asia, and South America, which he believes will outperform U.S. markets. At Euro Pacific Asset Management, Schiff’s strategies focus on companies poised to thrive in a post-dollar world, offering mutual funds and managed accounts to capitalize on global economic reordering. His Schiff Gold company provides affordable precious metals, steering clear of high-margin collectibles.

Peter Schiff’s analysis exposes Trump’s trade war as a hollow spectacle, driven by branding rather than economic logic. By folding on tariffs and claiming victory, Trump has worsened U.S.-China tensions while ignoring the root causes of trade deficits—profligate spending and monetary mismanagement. As China diversifies and de-dollarizes, the U.S. edges toward a sovereign debt crisis, with default or inflation as inevitable outcomes. Schiff’s call to invest in gold, silver, and global stocks offers a pragmatic hedge against this turmoil. Without addressing its fiscal recklessness, the U.S. risks economic collapse, leaving Trump’s “victory” a fleeting illusion in a crumbling financial order.
