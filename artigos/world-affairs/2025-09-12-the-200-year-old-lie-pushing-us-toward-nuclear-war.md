## Hypocrisy Unveiled: How a 200-Year-Old Doctrine Risks Nuclear War and Why Neutral Zones Are the Answer

In a interview on *Neutrality Studies*, hosted by Pascal Lottaz, Professor Jeffrey Sachs, a renowned Columbia University economist and global policy advisor, delivers a stark warning: the hypocrisy embedded in Western foreign policy, rooted in a 200-year-old doctrine, is driving the world toward nuclear catastrophe. Titled "The 200-Year-Old Lie Pushing Us Towards Nuclear War," their discussion dissects the flawed logic of "spheres of influence," the dangerous "with us or against us" mentality, and Sachs’ innovative proposal for "spheres of security" as a path to de-escalation.

### The Hypocrisy of Spheres of Influence

Sachs begins by addressing the Western narrative that condemns Russia’s demand for a "sphere of influence" in Eastern Europe, particularly Ukraine, as illegitimate. He argues this critique is steeped in hypocrisy, given the United States’ adherence to the Monroe Doctrine since 1823. President James Monroe declared that European interference in the Western Hemisphere would be seen as a direct threat to US security, while promising non-interference in European affairs—a reciprocal arrangement. Sachs notes that this doctrine, reaffirmed by leaders like Donald Trump, is effectively a claim to a US "sphere of security." Yet, the US denies Russia the same right, dismissing its concerns about NATO’s encroachment as mere imperial ambition.

"The United States is run fundamentally by hypocrites and dunderheads," Sachs asserts, criticizing leaders who ignore Russia’s legitimate fears of US missile systems, military bases, and CIA operations near its borders. He references the 1962 Cuban Missile Crisis, where the US nearly went to nuclear war over Soviet bases in Cuba, to underscore that great powers universally seek security buffers. Denying Russia this, while the US subverts governments across the Western Hemisphere (e.g., Venezuela, Panama), exposes a double standard that fuels global tensions.

### Spheres of Security: A Path to Peace

Sachs proposes redefining the contentious "sphere of influence" as a "sphere of security"—a geographic buffer where great powers agree not to deploy threatening military or covert assets. This is not about dominating smaller nations but ensuring mutual safety. For Russia, this means a neutral Ukraine, free from US or NATO military presence, to prevent first-strike capabilities or regime-change operations. Sachs emphasizes that Russia has consistently demanded this since 1991, not to control Ukraine’s internal affairs but to safeguard its own borders.

He draws on historical precedent: the 1955 Austrian State Treaty, where the Soviet Union agreed to withdraw occupation forces in exchange for Austria’s neutrality. Austria remained unthreatened thereafter, proving the efficacy of neutral zones. Similarly, Soviet diplomat Maxim Litvinov in the 1920s–40s envisioned a neutral belt from Sweden to the Mediterranean, protecting both the Soviet Union and the West from mutual fears of invasion. Sachs laments that such ideas were rejected, notably by Zbigniew Brzezinski, who saw post-Soviet Eastern Europe as a "security void" for NATO to fill, rather than a neutral zone benefiting all.

### The "With Us or Against Us" Mentality

A core obstacle to peace, Sachs argues, is the US’s binary worldview: nations are either allies or enemies, with neutrality deemed a threat. He cites the ancient Melian Dialogue from Thucydides’ *Peloponnesian War*, where Athens massacred the neutral island of Melos for refusing allegiance. This "with us or against us" logic persists in US policy, evident in the 2014 Maidan coup that ousted Ukraine’s neutralist President Yanukovych. Sachs reveals that in 2022, when Zelensky was poised to sign a neutrality-based peace deal with Russia, the US and UK’s Boris Johnson intervened, urging him to fight on—a decision linked to an estimated million deaths.

This mentality also targets neutral states like Qatar, recently bombed by Israel for hosting Hamas negotiators during ceasefire talks. Sachs sees this as part of a pattern: when negotiations threaten US or allied dominance (e.g., Iran’s nuclear talks), military strikes disrupt progress. The US’s complicity, driven by domestic lobbies, undermines its own diplomatic efforts, perpetuating conflict.

### The Nuclear Age Demands Clarity

Sachs critiques the renaming of the US Department of Defense to the Department of War under Trump, seeing it as a rare moment of semantic honesty but a terrifying signal. "The American political system delivers war," he states, pointing to US involvement in Gaza’s crisis, supported by American weapons and tech giants like Microsoft and Amazon. This war-making, unchecked by a muted Congress, operates on whims, not rational strategy, in a nuclear age where missteps could be catastrophic.

The Bulletin of Atomic Scientists’ Doomsday Clock, Sachs notes, has ticked closer to midnight with each post-Cold War president, from Clinton’s NATO expansion to Biden’s escalation in Ukraine. US policies—overthrowing governments, denying Russia’s security concerns, and rejecting neutrality—have diminished American safety, not enhanced it. Sachs argues that embracing neutral zones could reverse this, creating space for great powers to coexist without provocation.

### Overcoming the Tragedy of Great Power Politics

Sachs acknowledges skepticism from realists like John Mearsheimer, who view great power clashes as inevitable, rendering neutrality a "vacuum" to be filled. Yet, Sachs insists that in the nuclear age, tragedy is not destiny. He calls for public awareness, especially in the US and Europe, to reject the neoconservative push for hegemony since 1991. Neutral states must bolster defensive capabilities, as Qatar should to protect its diplomatic role, while advocating for reciprocal security.

The US Constitution’s vesting of war powers in Congress, not the president, offers a structural fix, but Sachs sees little hope in a system driven by secrecy and special interests. He urges citizens to demand clarity and accountability, recognizing that wars of choice, like Ukraine’s, do not serve national security but endanger it.

### A Call for Neutrality

Sachs’ vision of "spheres of security" is a pragmatic plea for survival. By legitimizing great powers’ need for safe borders—without endorsing their misdeeds—the world can avoid direct confrontations. Europe, once interdependent with Russia, could revive neutrality as a stabilizing force, as could regions like the Gulf or Asia-Pacific. The alternative—escalating proxy wars and rejecting dialogue—risks nuclear annihilation.

This interview, blending historical insight with urgent policy proposals, underscores the need to break free from hypocritical doctrines and embrace mutual security. Sachs’ call is clear: neutrality is not weakness but a shield against catastrophe.
