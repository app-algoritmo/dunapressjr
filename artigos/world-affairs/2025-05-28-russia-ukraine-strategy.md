## Russia’s Military Strategy in Ukraine: Martyanov on Demilitarization and NATO’s Decline

In a sobering interview with Professor Glenn Diesen, military analyst and author Andrei Martyanov dissects Russia’s unyielding military strategy in Ukraine, framing it as a calculated campaign to achieve political objectives through battlefield dominance. As diplomatic efforts falter—evidenced by stalled Istanbul talks and U.S. Vice President-elect JD Vance’s declaration that negotiations have reached a “dead end”—Russia presses forward with significant territorial gains. Martyanov argues that Russia, undeterred by Western posturing, is demilitarizing not just Ukraine but NATO itself, leveraging superior industrial and technological capabilities. This article explores Martyanov’s insights into Russia’s strategy, the battlefield’s evolution, and the diminishing prospects for compromise.

Stalled Diplomacy: Root Causes Unaddressed

Martyanov dismisses recent diplomatic overtures, including a two-hour call between Presidents Donald Trump and Vladimir Putin, as theatrical posturing lacking substance. Putin, he notes, reiterated Russia’s stance: no ceasefire or halt to hostilities until “root causes”—NATO’s expansion, U.S. co-belligerence, and Ukraine’s militarization—are addressed. Russia views the U.S. as a primary instigator, with Trump’s first term enabling Ukraine’s near-NATO integration through arms deliveries like Javelins.

Martyanov sees Trump’s self-proclaimed peacemaker role as narcissistic grandstanding, undermined by his reliance on neoconservative advisors like Keith Kellogg, whom he deems militarily incompetent. The Kremlin’s rejection of a Trump-Putin meeting, signaled by Dmitry Peskov, underscores Russia’s dismissal of U.S. mediation, viewing it as a “non-agreement-capable” partner due to past betrayals like the sabotaged 2022 Istanbul talks.

The U.S.’s dual-track diplomacy—Witkov’s conciliatory approach versus Kellogg’s hawkish stance—is, Martyanov argues, less a deliberate good cop-bad cop tactic than evidence of Trump’s loss of control. Russia, informed by its intelligence services like the FSB and SVR, perceives Trump as an ignorant narcissist surrounded by advisors incapable of grasping modern warfare or Russia’s red lines. Martyanov believes Moscow sees no viable compromise until NATO’s influence is dismantled, reflecting a broader goal of demilitarizing the alliance itself.

Battlefield Dynamics: A Grinding Slaughter

On the battlefield, Russia’s strategy is a methodical grind, exploiting Ukraine’s collapsing front lines. Martyanov highlights breakthroughs south of Kostyantynivka, where Russian forces have found operational gaps, encircling Pokrovsk from the north while leaving a tactical western exit for Ukrainian troops to flee under fire. This “classic split” exhausts Ukrainian defenses, rendering their lines untenable. Advances in Sumy, with 202 settlements forcibly evacuated, and potential moves toward Slovyansk signal Russia’s operational flexibility across a broadening front.

Martyanov emphasizes Russia’s industrial warfare approach, prioritizing artillery and multiple launch rocket systems, which account for over 80% of casualties. Drones, while visually striking, are secondary, countered by Russian anti-drone technologies. Ukraine’s depleted tank and armored vehicle stocks—evidenced by losses of Leopard 2s and Bradleys—limit its counteroffensive potential, leaving infantry and artillery to bear the brunt. Russia’s 700,000-strong reserve, equivalent to multiple army groups, underscores its capacity for escalation, potentially targeting NATO facilities in Poland or Finland if provoked. Martyanov cites the reinstatement of the Leningrad Military District as a strategic signal of readiness.

Strategic Objectives: Beyond Territorial Gains

Russia’s goals, Martyanov clarifies, are political: demilitarization and denazification of Ukraine, now extended to NATO’s broader demilitarization. The 2022 sabotage of talks by Boris Johnson, acting for the Biden administration, hardened Russia’s resolve, making regime change in Kyiv a priority. Territorial conquest is secondary; Russia seeks a buffer zone but avoids governing Ukraine’s hostile, anti-Russian population. Martyanov notes Putin’s abandonment of “brotherly people” rhetoric, reflecting a shift from integration to containment. Ukraine, he predicts, will revert to an agrarian state, its industry destroyed, akin to a modern Morgenthau Plan.

Russia’s refusal to annex beyond the four claimed regions (Donetsk, Luhansk, Zaporizhzhia, Kherson) stems from pragmatic concerns: administering a resentful population is costly and counterproductive. However, Martyanov sees potential for further westward advances, possibly past Kherson, to secure strategic depth, especially if diplomacy fails. Russia’s slow, deliberate pace, untethered from a timeline as per Putin’s orders, minimizes casualties while maximizing Ukrainian losses, with 200,000 potential prisoners of war posing a logistical challenge.

Technological and Industrial Superiority

Martyanov underscores Russia’s technological and industrial edge, outpacing NATO by one to two generations in weapons systems. The Russian General Staff, led by Valery Gerasimov, is shaping a military designed for dominance beyond 2050, integrating advanced reconnaissance, precision firepower, and air defense. NATO’s incompetence, rooted in outdated doctrines and reliance on fabricated histories like those of Nazi generals, leaves it unprepared for modern, net-centric warfare. Russia’s overwhelming artillery and long-range fires dominate, rendering Western hopes of Ukrainian resilience delusional.

The U.S. and NATO’s inability to sustain a real war, Martyanov argues, stems from depleted resources and industrial capacity. Europe, a “paper tiger,” lacks the energy and economic base to compete, while U.S. threats, like those from NORAD’s chief, ring hollow. Russia’s strategy leverages this asymmetry, maintaining a reserve capable of decisive action while NATO flounders.

Implications for the West and Future Prospects

Martyanov sees little room for compromise, given the West’s non-agreement-capable record—evidenced by violations of treaties like INF and ABM. Russia’s pivot to Asia, driven by Europe’s economic decline and energy shortages, signals a permanent reorientation. The U.S.’s planned troop withdrawal from Europe by 2028, driven by incapacity rather than goodwill, aligns with Russia’s goal of NATO’s collapse or retreat to pre-expansion borders. Bilateral relations with individual states, not supranational entities, are Russia’s preferred future.

The West’s desperation, reflected in European calls for a 30-day unconditional ceasefire, stems from battlefield realities. Martyanov suggests Russia might show leniency on administrative borders of the four regions, not controlling all claimed territories, but neutrality and denazification are non-negotiable. Demilitarization offers some flexibility, but only within a framework ensuring NATO’s retreat. Without addressing root causes, Ukraine risks ceasing to exist, ground down by Russia’s relentless strategy.

Conclusion

Andrei Martyanov’s analysis reveals Russia’s military strategy in Ukraine as a masterclass in industrial warfare, blending overwhelming firepower with strategic patience. Dismissing Trump’s diplomatic overtures as hollow, Russia pursues demilitarization and denazification, extending its sights to NATO’s broader weakening. The battlefield’s evolution, driven by artillery and precision systems, underscores Russia’s technological superiority, while the West’s delusions hasten its irrelevance. As Russia shapes a post-2050 military, the Global South watches, and the U.S. empire falters. Martyanov’s stark warning—address root causes or face Ukraine’s erasure—portends a grim future unless the West rethinks its hubris.
