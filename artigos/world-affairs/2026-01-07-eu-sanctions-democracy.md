## The EU's Assault on Democracy: Sanctions Over Justice | Patrik Baab Interview - EU sanctions democracy - By Pascal Lottaz - Neutrality Studies

### An In-Depth Look at Patrik Baab's Revelations on European Political Persecution

In the heart of Europe, where democratic ideals are supposed to reign supreme, a disturbing trend is emerging. The European Union, once hailed as a beacon of peace and unity, is increasingly using tools of foreign policy to target its own citizens. This isn't about punishing rogue states or international aggressors; it's about silencing dissent within its borders. In a recent interview on the Neutrality Studies channel hosted by Pascal Lottaz, renowned German journalist and author Patrik Baab delves into this phenomenon, coining it as a form of "anti-democracy." He argues that the EU is creating a system where individuals can be harassed, financially ruined, and socially ostracized without due process, all under the guise of upholding democratic values.

Baab, with his extensive background in war reporting and investigative journalism, draws from personal experiences and broader observations to paint a grim picture. Having reported from conflict zones like Ukraine's Donbass region, he has faced backlash for challenging mainstream narratives. In the interview, he describes how his own trip to Eastern Ukraine in 2022 led to professional repercussions. Upon returning to Germany, Baab was dismissed from his teaching position at a university, accused of spreading Russian propaganda simply because he sought to report on both sides of the conflict. This, he says, is emblematic of a larger issue: the EU's shift from judicial processes to administrative sanctions as a means of control.

The conversation begins with Lottaz posing a provocative question: What do you call a political system where foreign policy is used to persecute domestic citizens, even to the point of human rights violations, all while allowing free elections for representatives? Baab's response is stark: We don't have a word for it, but "anti-democracy" fits well. He explains that in traditional dictatorships, oppression is overt—no elections, no pretense of freedom. But in the EU, the facade of democracy remains intact. Citizens vote, parliaments convene, yet behind the scenes, unelected bureaucracies wield immense power to punish those who deviate from the official line, particularly on issues like the Ukraine-Russia conflict.

One of the key mechanisms Baab highlights is the EU's sanctions regime. Originally designed for international relations, these sanctions are now being applied internally. For instance, the EU has expanded its blacklist related to the Ukraine war to include not just Russians, but Europeans and even citizens of third countries who are deemed to support "Russian propaganda." Baab points to cases like that of Jacques Baud, a retired Swiss colonel and former UN and NATO official, who was sanctioned for his critical analyses of the war. Baud's "crime"? Writing books and articles that question the Western narrative. No trial, no evidence presented in court—just an administrative decision that freezes assets, restricts travel, and tarnishes reputations.

Baab argues that this bypasses fundamental principles of the rule of law. In a true democracy, accusations require evidence, a fair trial, and the right to defense. Sanctions, however, operate in secrecy. Decisions are made by the EU Council, often based on intelligence reports that are not disclosed. Appeals exist in theory, but in practice, they drag on for years, leaving individuals in limbo. "It's like medieval outlawry," Baab says, referencing historical practices where people were declared outside the law's protection without trial. This modern version, he notes, financially eradicates people, preventing them from working, banking, or even receiving social benefits in some cases.

Drawing from his book "On Both Sides of the Front," Baab shares firsthand accounts from Ukraine. He describes the war not as a simple invasion but as a multifaceted conflict involving civil war elements, geopolitical rivalries, and economic interests. In Donbass, he witnessed communities divided by language and allegiance, with many ethnic Russians feeling oppressed by Kyiv's policies since 2014. Yet, reporting these nuances in Germany led to accusations of bias. Baab recounts how media outlets and academic institutions, influenced by transatlantic alliances, enforced a narrative aligned with NATO and US interests. Dissenters like him are labeled as threats, justifying sanctions or cancellations.

The interview also touches on the broader implications for European society. Baab warns that this "anti-democracy" is eroding trust in institutions. When journalists, analysts, and ordinary citizens fear reprisal for questioning foreign policy, free speech suffers. He cites examples from Germany, where universities and media houses have purged critical voices. In one instance, a professor was investigated for attending a conference in Russia. This climate of fear, Baab asserts, mirrors McCarthyism in the US during the Cold War, but with a European twist—using EU-wide mechanisms to enforce conformity.

Lottaz and Baab discuss how this ties into Europe's self-destructive path. Sanctions against Russia, intended to weaken Moscow, have boomeranged. Europe's economy is reeling from energy shortages, deindustrialization, and inflation. Baab points out that Germany's chemical and automotive sectors are relocating to Asia and the US, where energy is cheaper. "We're sacrificing our prosperity for ideology," he says. Yet, elites remain committed, blinded by allegiance to Washington. Baab traces this to the 2003 Iraq War, when European leaders began aligning more closely with US neoconservative policies, corrupting domestic politics.

Human rights violations are central to Baab's critique. The EU's Charter of Fundamental Rights guarantees fair trials and freedom of expression, yet sanctions violate these. Baab references the European Court of Justice, which has upheld some appeals but often too late to mitigate damage. He mentions Nathalie Yamb, an Ivorian activist sanctioned for her pro-Russian stance, showing how the EU extends its reach beyond borders. This extraterritorial application, Baab argues, undermines international law and neutrality principles.

Throughout the interview, Baab emphasizes empirical evidence over speculation. His reporting is grounded in on-the-ground observations, interviews with locals, and analysis of official documents. He criticizes mainstream media for relying on unverified sources, like Ukrainian government reports, while ignoring Russian perspectives. This one-sidedness, he says, fuels propaganda wars that distort reality and justify internal repression.

As the discussion winds down, Baab calls for resistance. He urges Europeans to demand transparency in sanction processes and protect journalistic freedom. "Democracy isn't just voting; it's about accountability and justice," he states. Without these, the EU risks becoming a bureaucratic authoritarianism, where power concentrates in Brussels, detached from citizens.

This interview sheds light on a critical juncture for Europe. As geopolitical tensions rise, the temptation to sacrifice liberties for "security" grows. Baab's insights remind us that true democracy requires vigilance against such erosions. By replacing justice with sanctions, the EU not only weakens itself economically but also morally, paving the way for a future where dissent is equated with disloyalty.

Patrik Baab's conversation with Pascal Lottaz is a wake-up call. It reveals how foreign policy tools are being turned inward, creating a chilling effect on free discourse. As Europe grapples with its identity, acknowledging these flaws is the first step toward reclaiming democratic integrity.
