## Economic Decline & Death of the American Empire: Trump’s Trade War and Global Shifts

In a penetrating interview with Professor Glenn Diesen, Einar Tangen, a senior fellow at the Taihe Institute and chairman of Asia Narratives, dissects the faltering U.S. economic strategy under President Donald Trump’s trade war and its broader implications for America’s global dominance. Trump’s tariffs, briefly imposed on nearly every nation before being paused or reversed, were declared a victory despite achieving little. Tangen argues that this erratic approach, coupled with a $38 trillion debt and domestic economic woes, signals the decline of the American empire. This article explores Tangen’s analysis of the U.S.-China trade dynamics, America’s unsustainable policies, and the global shift toward a multipolar order.

## Trump’s Trade War: A Bluff Called by China

Tangen frames Trump’s tariffs—targeting 180 countries, including allies like Canada and adversaries like China—as a reckless “broadside” lacking an endgame. Intended to project power and force unequal bilateral deals, the tariffs backfired when China, unfazed, refused to negotiate under pressure. Tangen sees Trump’s retreat, followed by a claimed victory, as a face-saving tactic after China called his bluff. The 90-day reset, with talks now in Geneva at the World Trade Organization (WTO), reflects China’s strategic push for neutral ground and multilateral order, contrasting Trump’s unilateral bullying.

The tariffs, Tangen notes, were based on flawed logic, targeting trade deficits without addressing their root cause: a strong U.S. dollar and domestic policy failures. Importers, not foreign exporters, bear tariff costs, passing them to U.S. consumers, who face rising prices. This contradicts Trump’s mandate to reduce costs, as 70% of Americans live paycheck to paycheck, grappling with credit card defaults and bankruptcies. China’s proposed “Phase Two” deal—reviving a failed Phase One agreement for U.S. agricultural and energy purchases—aims to lock Trump into a transactional pact, ensuring stability until his term ends.

## The Roots of Decline: A Debt Bomb and Hollowed Middle Class

Tangen traces America’s economic woes to a strong dollar, which fuels cheap imports but stifles exports, and a failure to reinvest corporate profits into workers or infrastructure. Since 2001, U.S. corporations reaped trillions by outsourcing to low-wage countries like India ($3/day labor) and China ($20/day), but wages stagnated, shrinking the middle class from 61% in 1971 to 40% today. This impoverishment, ignored by politicians touting a rosy economy, fueled Trump’s rise as voters sought change, despite his lack of a coherent plan.

The U.S.’s $38 trillion debt, Tangen warns, is a “debt bomb” akin to a Ponzi scheme, with $2 trillion borrowed annually to cover a $1 trillion interest bill. Credit downgrades by S&P (2011), Fitch (2023), and Moody’s (2025) signal eroding confidence, raising borrowing costs. Trump’s $4 trillion unfunded tax cut, facing Republican skepticism, exacerbates the crisis. Tangen critiques Trump’s team—TV pundits and loyalists like Pam Bondi, who exaggerated fentanyl seizure impacts—as ill-equipped, reflecting his preference for sycophants over experts.

## A Misguided Quest for Manufacturing Glory

Trump’s push to re-industrialize America, Tangen argues, is futile. Global supply chains, driven by cost efficiencies, favor low-wage nations. U.S. labor costs, even with a devalued dollar, cannot compete, especially with only 40% of Americans reading above a sixth-grade level, unprepared for a digital economy. The Manufacturers Association’s reported 100,000 job vacancies underscore this skills gap. Trump’s dismantling of the Department of Education and failure to invest in STEM further undermines competitiveness.

Tariffs, meant to protect U.S. industries, raise costs for components in supply chains, making American products like cars less competitive. Recent layoffs, such as FedEx’s 20,000 job cuts, signal broader economic strain, with logistics and auto industries facing further losses. Tangen dismisses Trump’s nostalgia for a manufacturing golden age as economically unsound, squeezing margins without addressing structural deficiencies.

## China’s Strategy: Patience and Diversification

China, Tangen explains, plays the “adult in the room,” avoiding confrontation to preserve global stability. Rather than crashing the dollar by dumping treasuries, Beijing seeks a Phase Two deal to manage Trump’s volatility, tying him to commitments that penalize reneging. Simultaneously, China diversifies, redirecting exports to Europe, Southeast Asia, and Latin America, and strengthening BRICS institutions like the New Development Bank, which Colombia recently sought to join. Initiatives like grain corridors and commodity exchanges with Russia reduce reliance on U.S.-dominated systems, reflecting a broader Global South push for sovereignty.

The U.S.’s unreliability, intensified by Biden’s semiconductor bans and Trump’s erratic tariffs, has alienated allies. Canada’s election of a nationalist leader and Europe’s hesitation signal a global pivot away from U.S. hegemony. Tangen sees BRICS nations—China, Russia, Brazil—as inheritors of a broken Western-led order, tasked with stabilizing global trade and finance.

## The Death of the American Empire

Tangen views America’s isolation as self-inflicted, driven by Trump’s rejection of international norms, from seizing Greenland to undermining the WTO. The U.S., once a beacon of rule of law, now demands vassal-like loyalty without reciprocity, alienating even allies. Its $38 trillion debt, coupled with a shrinking middle class and educational deficits, portends collapse. Tangen warns of a potential military escalation as Trump’s last card to unify a fracturing nation, risking global catastrophe.

The American public, facing rising prices and job losses, may soon split Trump’s 30% cult-like base, as empty shelves and unaffordable goods erode faith. The bond market’s looming hammer—higher interest rates—could trigger bankruptcy, with no plan to repay or restructure debt. Tangen sees no easy fix, dismissing impeachment or reliance on ideologues like JD Vance, tied to figures like Peter Thiel, as solutions.

Einar Tangen’s analysis paints a dire portrait of America’s economic decline, accelerated by Trump’s incoherent trade war and systemic failures. China’s strategic patience and global diversification contrast with the U.S.’s isolation, signaling the death of its empire. As debt mounts and the middle class erodes, America faces a reckoning, with consumers and bond markets poised to deliver painful blows. The Global South, led by BRICS, emerges as a stabilizing force, but the U.S.’s path—lacking leadership or vision—risks chaos.
