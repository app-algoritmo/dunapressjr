## EU and EEA 2025 Press Freedom Ranking: Top 10 Worst for Free Speech

Europe has long positioned itself as the global vanguard of democratic values, where the free flow of information underpins everything from the bustling cafes of Paris to the fjords of Norway. Yet, beneath this polished facade, a quieter battle rages—one where words are weaponized, journalists are muzzled, and the very mechanisms meant to protect liberty instead erode it. In 2025, the Reporters Without Borders (RSF) World Press Freedom Index reveals a sobering truth: even within the European Union (EU) and the European Economic Area (EEA)—encompassing 30 nations committed to shared principles of openness—the commitment to free expression is fraying. While Nordic powerhouses like Norway and Estonia top the global charts, southern and eastern members lag perilously, their media landscapes scarred by conflicts of interest, economic strangulation, and legal chokepoints.

[Vídeo](https://datawrapper.dwcdn.net/kumCs/1/)

This isn't an anomaly of distant borders; it's a crisis at the heart of the world's most integrated economic bloc. The RSF's 2025 edition, evaluating 180 countries across political, legal, economic, sociocultural, and safety indicators, underscores how economic fragility has surged as the primary threat to journalism. Ad revenues evaporate into the coffers of tech behemoths, public broadcasters face budget slashes, and the delayed European Media Freedom Act (EMFA) leaves independent voices dangling. In the EU/EEA, press freedom scores average a respectable 77.5, but the variance is stark: from Norway's near-perfect 92.31 to Greece's dismal 55.37. Over half the world's population now resides in "red zones" of severe restrictions, and while Europe bucks this trend overall, its internal disparities signal deeper rot.

To illuminate this, we've zeroed in on the EU/EEA's underperformers. Drawing exclusively from the RSF 2025 data, this ranking spotlights the 10 worst-ranked countries—those with the highest global positions (indicating greater suppression)—focusing on systemic pressures like strategic lawsuits against public participation (SLAPPs), opaque state advertising, and ownership concentration that turns newsrooms into echo chambers. These aren't isolated failings; they reflect a continental drift where populist surges and fiscal austerity collide with democratic norms.

Leading the pack in infamy is Greece, plummeting to 89th globally with a score of 55.37—the bloc's absolute nadir. Once the birthplace of democracy, Athens now contends with a media ecosystem riddled by oligarchic entanglements. Shipping magnates and political allies dominate outlets, dictating coverage on everything from the migrant crisis to economic reforms. RSF highlights pervasive conflicts of interest, where journalists face SLAPPs that bankrupt investigations into corruption. In 2025, the government's handling of wildfire reporting drew accusations of censorship, with reporters detained at hotspots. Economic scars from the 2010s debt saga persist: underfunded public broadcaster ERT limps along, while private media shuns dissent to secure ad dollars. This isn't mere inefficiency; it's a calculated chill, where self-censorship thrives amid threats of libel suits. Greece's slide, down from 88th last year, underscores how EU funds—poured into recovery—bypass media pluralism, fostering a landscape where truth bends to power.

Cyprus follows closely at 77th (59.04), an island paradise masking a press plagued by favoritism. State advertising flows disproportionately to compliant outlets, per RSF, turning journalism into a patronage game. The divided isle amplifies this: in the Republic, tycoons with banking ties quash exposés on financial scandals, while the north's Turkish-occupied zone enforces dual censorship. Journalists endure harassment, and the 2025 banking probe into laundered funds saw key stories spiked. As an EU member since 2004, Cyprus's opacity clashes with bloc standards, yet EMFA's slow rollout offers little remedy. Economic reliance on tourism and finance starves independents, leaving citizens with sanitized narratives that evade accountability.

Bulgaria ranks 70th (60.78), a Balkan entrant whose 11-spot tumble since 2024 signals alarm. Post-communist hopes have curdled into oligarchic capture: a handful of media barons, often ex-intelligence figures, control 80% of the market. RSF decries SLAPPs as a weapon of choice, draining resources from anti-corruption watchdogs like Bivol.bg. The 2025 protests against judicial meddling highlighted this, with state TV blacking out coverage while loyalists peddle propaganda. EU accession pressures wane as funds flow unchecked, exacerbating economic woes—ad markets concentrate, independents fold. Bulgaria's story is a cautionary tale: without vigilant enforcement, integration breeds complacency.

At 68th (62.82), Hungary embodies Viktor Orbán's "illiberal democracy" blueprint. The Central European University’s 2019 exodus was a harbinger; now, state ads—90% of media revenue—reward allies, crippling foes like Klubrádió. RSF notes political instrumentalization at its peak, with laws branding critical NGOs "foreign agents." In 2025, coverage of EU fund graft faced shutdowns, journalists exiled or sued into silence. Economic fragility bites: inflation erodes salaries, forcing self-censorship. Hungary's EU peers decry this as a sovereignty facade for suppression, yet Orbán's veto power stalls reforms.

Malta, 67th (62.96), shatters the Mediterranean idyll. The 2017 assassination of Daphne Caruana Galizia lingers as a scar; her murder exposed golden passport rackets, but probes stall amid SLAPP barrages. RSF flags ownership concentration: casino moguls and developers own outlets, skewing reports on money laundering. 2025 saw heightened tensions over EU audits, with public broadcaster PBS accused of bias. Tiny Malta's insularity amplifies risks—journalists self-isolate, economic dependence on opaque sectors stifles scrutiny. As the bloc's densest nation, its press woes ripple, challenging the EU's anti-corruption pledges.

Croatia edges in at 60th (64.20), where Adriatic charm conceals HDZ party dominance. State funds flow to friendly media, per RSF, while independents like Novosti battle ethnic minority coverage suits. The 2025 earthquake recovery scandals ignited SLAPPs, draining outlets amid ad droughts. Post-2013 accession, Croatia's media law lags, leaving reporters vulnerable to raids. Economic tourism reliance mutes environmental critiques, fostering a compliant fourth estate.

Romania, 55th (66.42), grapples with post-Ceaușescu ghosts. Oligarchs like Dan Voiculescu control swaths of TV, turning airwaves into political arenas. RSF highlights SLAPPs against Panama Papers-style digs, with 2025 elections marred by disinformation. EU funds combat this unevenly—public TVR underperforms, economic informality starves locals. Bucharest's vibrancy contrasts rural silence, where access dwindles.

Italy ranks 49th (68.01), a boot-shaped paradox. Silvio Berlusconi's legacy endures in media tycoon sway, with Rai politicized under Meloni's rightward tilt. RSF notes defamation decriminalization delays, SLAPPs proliferating against mafia exposés. 2025 migration coverage drew censorship cries, economic divides—north vs. south—mirror press fragmentation. As the EU's third economy, Italy's laggard status emboldens authoritarians.

Slovakia, 38th (71.93), reels from 2023's Kočner trial echoes. Public RTVS faces defunding threats, RSF warns, amid populist pushes. SLAPPs target investigative duo Aktuality.sk, post-2018 murder. 2025's coalition shifts amplified biases, economic ad concentration hurts. Bratislava's EU heart hosts irony: vibrant NGOs, but media gasps.

Slovenia rounds the top 10 at 33rd (74.06), where Janša-era scars fade unevenly. Ownership battles persist, RSF says, with state ads tilting scales. 2025's green transition reporting faced suits, economic SME fragility looms. Ljubljana's alpine calm belies tensions, as EMFA promises dangle.

These rankings expose a pattern: southern and eastern EU/EEA states bear the brunt, their scores dipping below 70 amid intertwined threats. Economic indicators tanked bloc-wide, with RSF attributing 40% of declines to funding shortfalls. The EMFA, adopted in 2024 but implementation-skewed by 2025, aims to curb SLAPPs and boost transparency—yet delays in transposition leave vulnerabilities. Council of Europe reports echo this, urging defamation decriminalization to safeguard discourse. Meanwhile, the Digital Services Act (DSA) walks a tightrope: lauded for hate speech curbs, critiqued for overreach that could chill expression.

Contrast these shadows with beacons: Norway (1st, 92.31) sustains pluralism via arm's-length funding; Estonia (2nd, 89.46) leverages e-governance for resilience; Netherlands (3rd, 88.64), Sweden (4th, 87.13), Finland (5th, 87.18) exemplify trust-based models. Liechtenstein (12th, 83.42) and Luxembourg (13th, 83.04) punch above weight, their micro-scales fostering diversity. These successes hinge on investment: diversified revenues, robust safety nets, cultural veneration of truth.

Broader trends? The EU/EEA's average masks polarization—western cores thrive, peripheries strain under migration, inequality, and populism. RSF's 2025 analysis ties this to global headwinds: AI deepfakes erode trust, climate reporting invites reprisals. Yet, hope flickers: Poland's 31st (74.79) rebound post-PiS, via withdrawn abuses; Czechia's 10th (84. something, wait from list) via budget bolsters. Civil society—from Malta's Daphne Project to Bulgaria's hackathons—resists, amplifying via digital veins.

Reversing course demands urgency. Expedite EMFA, diversify funding (e.g., EU-wide media funds), train against SLAPPs. Policymakers must audit ad flows, shield public media from whims. Citizens, too: demand literacy, support indies via subscriptions. Europe's EU/EEA pact isn't ironclad; it's a living covenant, tested by these fissures. As 2025 wanes, the index isn't destiny—it's a mirror, urging recommitment to the Enlightenment's flame.

In this refocused lens, the truth emerges: suppression thrives in complacency's soil, but uprooting it fortifies the union. The pen remains mightier, if wielded collectively.
