## Europe Must Relearn Diplomacy: Evarist Bartolo’s Vision for a Multipolar Future

In a compelling interview with Professor Glenn Diesen, Evarist Bartolo, Malta’s former Minister of Foreign and European Affairs, critiques Europe’s outdated mindset of dominance, rooted in 500 years of colonial history, and its failure to adapt to a multipolar world. Bartolo argues that Europe’s dismissal of diplomacy, obsession with military dominance, and subordination to Washington undermine its global relevance. Drawing from his experience in the EU Foreign Affairs Council, he highlights a colonial legacy—epitomized by the “garden vs. jungle” rhetoric—that hinders equitable partnerships with the Global South, Russia, and China. This article explores Bartolo’s insights, urging Europe to embrace diplomacy, humility, and coexistence to navigate a shifting global order.

## A Colonial Mindset Persists

Bartolo traces Europe’s worldview to the 15th-century Doctrine of Discovery, a papal edict sanctioning colonial domination of non-Christian lands. This “doctrine of domination,” only rescinded by Pope Francis in recent years, framed Europe as a civilizing force against “barbarous” others—initially Islam and Orthodox Christianity, later Russia and China. From Malta, a historical frontier of Catholic Europe, Bartolo sees this legacy morphing into NATO’s role as a bulwark against the East, perpetuating a Manichean narrative of good versus evil.

This mindset, evident in former EU foreign policy chief Josep Borrell’s “garden vs. jungle” metaphor, portrays Europe as civilized and the Global South as needing taming. Bartolo criticizes this as neocolonial, noting its persistence in EU policies like the Global Gateway, which extracts African resources without African input. Such arrogance, he argues, alienates the Global South, which rejects domination, and fuels resentment against Europe’s perceived superiority.

## Diplomacy Dismissed as Naïve

As Malta’s foreign minister, Bartolo found diplomacy sidelined in the EU Foreign Affairs Council, deemed “naïve” compared to arms sales and war mobilization. He recounts proposing dialogue to resolve Eastern Mediterranean tensions between Turkey, Cyprus, Greece, and Egypt, only to be rebuffed. This disdain for diplomacy, he suggests, reflects the influence of arms industries in nations like France, where conflict is more profitable than peace.

Bartolo laments the EU’s failure to heed the 1975 Helsinki Accords’ vision of peaceful coexistence, which aimed to bridge Cold War divides. Post-1989, instead of building a common European security architecture, as pledged in Malta by Bush and Gorbachev, the West pursued NATO expansion, ignoring Russia’s security concerns. This domination-driven approach, Bartolo argues, provoked the Ukraine war, a tragedy of “three years of no diplomacy,” fueled by ultimatums and punishment rather than dialogue.

## Europe’s Strategic Blindness

Bartolo warns of Europe’s “strategic blindness” in clinging to U.S. coattails amid its decline. The EU’s demographic crisis—losing 57 million working-age people by 2050—economic stagnation, and technological lag (China leads in 57 of 64 critical technologies, per the Australian Strategic Policy Institute) erode its global clout. Yet, leaders refuse to admit this, doubling down on militarization over welfare, with plans to raise defense spending to 3.5–5% of GDP.

This shortsightedness extends to neglecting Africa, where a youthful population outnumbers aging Europeans. Bartolo criticizes the EU’s toxic migration debate and failure to forge equitable partnerships, as seen in Libya’s instability since NATO’s 2011 intervention. The EU’s fragmented approach—France and Italy backing rival Libyan factions—undermines collective policy, further isolating Europe.

## Relearning Diplomacy: A Path Forward

Bartolo advocates relearning diplomacy, inspired by figures like Enrico Mattei, who in the 1950s challenged Western oil monopolies with 50/50 deals in the Middle East and Africa. Italy’s “Mattei Plan” for Africa signals this partnership model, but Bartolo urges broader EU adoption. Diplomacy, he argues, requires humility, listening, and treating nations as equals—qualities absent in the EU’s vertical, neocolonial relationships.

Quoting military historian Basil Liddell Hart, Bartolo emphasizes keeping cool, showing patience, and avoiding self-righteousness to engage enemies constructively. He recalls John F. Kennedy’s 1963 call to see the Soviet Union as human, not evil, a lesson lost on today’s leaders. The EU’s McCarthy-like smear of dissenters as “Putin’s puppets” stifles debate, fostering groupthink and democratic disenchantment.

## A Multipolar Imperative

In a multipolar world, Bartolo sees Europe’s survival hinging on diversifying partnerships with China, India, Brazil, and Russia, rejecting vassalage to Washington. The EU’s strategic laziness, relying on U.S. dominance, is unsustainable as global power shifts. Bartolo cites JD Vance’s call for partners, not vassals, as a wake-up call, yet notes Europe’s inaction. The Global South’s rise, paralleled in Catholicism’s shift to non-Western voices, demands a horizontal, coexistence-based approach.

Bartolo warns that without this shift, Europe risks irrelevance, economic decline, and political instability. Citing Viktor Frankl’s “tragic optimism,” he urges persistent effort to prevent humanity’s destruction, advocating diplomacy as the antidote to domination’s curse.

Evarist Bartolo’s critique exposes Europe’s failure to shed its colonial mindset, dismissing diplomacy for a doomed quest for dominance. The EU’s subordination to Washington, neglect of the Global South, and provocation of Russia threaten its stability in a multipolar world. By relearning diplomacy—embracing humility, listening, and partnership—Europe can forge equitable ties and reclaim relevance. Bartolo’s call, grounded in historical lessons and urgent realities, is a clarion for a Europe that coexists, not conquers, ensuring a future of peace over self-destruction.
