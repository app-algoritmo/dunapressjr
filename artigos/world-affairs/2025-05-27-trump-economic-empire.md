## Trump’s Quest for Empire Profit: Michael Hudson on U.S. Decline and BRICS’s Rise

In a revealing interview with Professor Glenn Diesen, renowned economist Michael Hudson dissects President Donald Trump’s economic and foreign policy approach, arguing that his rhetoric of peace masks an aggressive quest for a better “return on investment” for the American empire. Far from dismantling U.S. hegemony, Trump seeks to extract maximum benefits from allies and adversaries alike through transactional deals, tariffs, and resource grabs, as seen in Ukraine’s minerals deal. Hudson warns that this hubristic strategy, coupled with a failure to grasp global economic shifts, accelerates America’s decline. This article explores Hudson’s critique of Trump’s imperial ambitions, the flaws in his economic tactics, and the transformative potential of BRICS in a multipolar world.

## Trump’s “Peace” Plan: Surrender or Annihilation

Hudson challenges Trump’s self-proclaimed image as a peace president, arguing that his vision of peace equates to unconditional surrender by adversaries. In Ukraine, Trump’s ceasefire proposal demands Russia cede control to NATO, allowing Western troops and missiles to escalate tensions. In Gaza, his call to “kill them all” envisions ethnic cleansing to create a “Middle Eastern Riviera,” prioritizing profit over justice. For Iran, Trump’s plan involves inspections to pinpoint and bomb nuclear sites, aiming for regime change. These aggressive stances, Hudson contends, exploit public anti-war sentiment while advancing a militaristic agenda under the guise of peace, ensuring U.S. dominance at any cost.

Trump’s transactional mindset, evident since the 1980s, frames global protection as a costly burden allies must repay. Hudson sees this not as a retreat from empire but an attempt to make it more lucrative, demanding that nations like Japan and Europe fund U.S. military presence or face economic penalties. This approach, rooted in Trump’s real estate tactics of double-crossing partners, alienates allies and fuels global resistance.

## The Ukraine Minerals Deal: A Fantasy of Exploitation

A centerpiece of Trump’s economic strategy is the Ukraine minerals deal, which cedes over half of Ukraine’s natural resources to U.S. control. Marketed as a security guarantee tying American and Ukrainian interests, Hudson dismisses it as a “fantasy” driven by misconceptions about rare earths and territorial control. The Zaporizhzhia nuclear plant, a key target, would monopolize electricity for Western Ukraine, not Russia, while rare earth mining’s high costs offer no economic gain. Instead, Hudson sees the deal as a pretext for deploying U.S. troops disguised as miners, securing strategic assets.

The deal’s structure, Hudson notes, exploits Ukraine by valuing U.S. contributions—overpriced arms like missiles and tanks—above Ukrainian resources. This leaves Ukraine with minimal profits, as American investors, already buying up farmland, dominate the post-war economy. President Volodymyr Zelenskyy’s objection, citing the loss of Ukraine’s patrimony, underscores the deal’s predatory nature. Hudson views it as a distraction, potentially culminating in symbolic gestures like a Trump hotel in Kyiv, rather than a viable economic strategy.

## Transactional Diplomacy: Alienating Allies

Trump’s transactional approach—demanding that nations “lose” for America to “win”—strains alliances. Japan, facing 25% tariffs on auto exports, has pushed back, with leaders rejecting U.S. extortion. Hudson suggests Japan is exploring Plan B: reorienting trade toward China, leveraging existing auto plants there. Japan’s threat to sell U.S. treasuries, as 30-year bond yields near 5%, signals growing defiance. South Korea, similarly impacted in autos and electronics, may follow suit, seeking Asian markets to offset U.S. aggression.

This economic war, Hudson argues, extends to Europe, where leaders like the UK’s Keir Starmer, beholden to U.S. influence, agree to isolate China, sacrificing access to Asia’s growing markets for a shrinking U.S. economy. Such deals, prioritizing political loyalty over national interest, isolate Europe from global opportunities, reinforcing U.S. unipolar control at the expense of allies’ prosperity.

## The Dollar Dilemma: Weakening the Empire’s Foundation

Trump’s push to devalue the dollar for export competitiveness clashes with the need for a strong dollar to maintain reserve currency status. Hudson warns that a weaker dollar, erasing interest gains for foreign investors, deters treasury purchases, as seen with Japan’s hesitance. Trump’s threats to blacklist BRICS members exacerbate this, accelerating de-dollarization. Countries like China and Japan, holding significant dollar reserves, are diversifying into gold, currency swaps, and mutual investments, reducing reliance on U.S. markets.

Hudson critiques the U.S.’s failure to anticipate global responses, blinded by hubris. The belief that nations like China are “desperate” for U.S. markets ignores their robust growth and self-reliance. China’s response to Biden’s chip bans, continued by Trump, exemplifies this: developing domestic chip production and redirecting exports to the Global South. This resilience undermines U.S. leverage, as nations see diminishing returns in a stagnating U.S. economy.

## BRICS and the Path to a Multipolar Economy

Hudson views Trump’s actions as irreversibly fracturing the U.S.-centric world order, forcing nations to seek alternatives. BRICS, comprising China, Russia, and others, is poised to lead this shift, but Hudson laments its lack of a coherent economic doctrine. Unlike 19th-century classical economists like Adam Smith, who advocated freeing economies from rent-seeking landlords and monopolies, BRICS economists, often U.S.-trained, lack a framework to counter neoliberalism.

China’s success, Hudson argues, stems from a mixed economy model, socializing infrastructure like transportation and education to lower costs and boost competitiveness. By keeping banking public, China avoids financial rent-seeking, recycling profits into tangible investments. BRICS, however, has not articulated a similar strategy, failing to distinguish productive GDP from rentier profits like interest and land rent, which inflate U.S. statistics. Hudson urges BRICS to adopt classical economics’ focus on minimizing economic rent—land, monopoly, and financial overhead—to foster efficient, equitable growth.

Michael Hudson’s analysis exposes Trump’s quest for imperial profit as a delusional gamble, alienating allies and accelerating America’s decline. His aggressive “peace” plans and extractive deals, like Ukraine’s minerals grab, prioritize short-term gains over global stability. By weakening the dollar and threatening BRICS, Trump undermines the empire’s foundation, pushing nations toward alternatives. China’s strategic diversification and BRICS’s potential, if guided by classical economic principles, could reshape a multipolar world. Yet, without a doctrinal shift, BRICS risks missing this opportunity. Hudson’s critique underscores the urgent need for a new economic vision to navigate the death of U.S. unipolarity, warning that Trump’s hubris may hasten an irreversible collapse.
