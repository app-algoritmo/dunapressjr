## West Losing Propaganda War: Michel Collon Exposes Elite Lies and Anti-War Solutions

In a revealing interview with Pascal Lottaz of Neutrality Studies, Belgian journalist and anti-war author Michel Collon dissects the systemic propaganda fueling Western wars and the elite’s growing desperation to maintain narrative control. With 35 years of investigating media manipulation, Collon, author of books like Ukraine: War of Images and October 7th: An Inquiry into Facts and Lies, argues that the West’s propaganda machine is faltering as public trust erodes and alternative voices gain traction. This article draws on Collon’s insights to explore the mechanics of war propaganda, its historical roots, and the urgent need for a coordinated independent media to counter elite disinformation.

## The Five Principles of War Propaganda

Collon outlines five core principles that Western elites use to sell wars, a strategy honed since the 1991 Gulf War. First, they hide economic interests—oil, gas, or strategic dominance—behind noble causes like democracy or women’s rights. Second, they obscure colonial histories of plunder and “divide and rule” tactics that sowed conflict in regions like the Middle East and Africa. Third, they demonize adversaries, portraying leaders or groups as monsters through fabricated atrocities, such as the debunked “beheaded babies” narrative post-October 7, 2023. Fourth, they invert aggressor and victim roles, casting the West or allies like Israel as perpetual victims, despite decades of aggression. Fifth, they monopolize narratives, suppressing alternative perspectives to prevent public debate.

These tactics, Collon notes, are not new. From Julius Caesar’s self-serving war chronicles to Britain’s World War I tales of German atrocities, propaganda has always justified imperialist ambitions. Modern tools—marketing techniques, AI, and media consolidation—have amplified its reach, but the formula remains consistent: emotional manipulation over factual scrutiny.

## Case Studies in Manipulation: From Yugoslavia to Ukraine

Collon’s work exposes recurring media lies across conflicts. In the 1990s Yugoslav wars, Western media falsely blamed Serbs for market bombings in Sarajevo and alleged genocide in Srebrenica, ignoring evidence of Muslim provocations and UN findings exonerating Slobodan Milošević. The 2002 Caracas coup against Hugo Chávez saw snipers, likely CIA-backed, kill both pro- and anti-Chávez demonstrators to justify his ouster, a tactic repeated in Ukraine’s 2014 Maidan coup. Ivan Katchanovski’s research, cited by Collon, confirms snipers shot protesters to incite chaos, enabling a U.S.-orchestrated regime change, as admitted by Stratfor’s George Friedman. Yet, Western media buried these facts, framing Maidan as a “revolution of dignity.”

The October 7 Hamas attack on Israel, Collon argues, was mischaracterized as a genocidal, religiously motivated assault. His book reveals it as a coordinated anti-colonial operation by multiple Palestinian groups, targeting illegal military bases, not civilians. Fabricated stories of rapes and beheaded babies, debunked in Israel but persistent in Western narratives, fueled calls for war, drowning out calls for historical context or dialogue.

## The West’s Declining Grip: A Sign of Weakness

Collon sees the West’s recent censorship—banning RT and Sputnik in the EU—as a sign of desperation. Unlike Switzerland, which rejected such bans as illiberal, the EU’s move betrays a fear of losing the information war. Public distrust in mainstream media is rising, with polls showing widespread skepticism. In the Global South, from Latin America to Africa, Western narratives on Ukraine or Gaza are dismissed as imperialist posturing, echoing the late Pope Francis’s view that NATO provoked Russia’s actions in Ukraine.

This shift, Collon argues, reflects a broader crisis. The U.S. and Europe, grappling with economic decline and moral decay, resort to repression—evidenced by the killing of over 200 journalists in Gaza and the vilification of whistleblowers like Julian Assange. Unlike earlier eras, when figures like Daniel Ellsberg faced less severe backlash, today’s dissenters are demonized to silence anti-war voices. Collon praises Assange as a hero, urging legal action and public mobilization to protect journalists.

## The Left’s Capitulation and the Need for a New Anti-War Movement

Collon laments the European left’s ideological surrender, even among radicals, who often buy into Western war narratives. From Iraq to Libya, leftists equate local “dictators” with Western aggressors, ignoring imperialist motives. Libya’s Muammar Gaddafi, for instance, was targeted not for tyranny but for resisting multinational control and funding African independence. This capitulation, detailed in Collon’s The Left and the War, has dismantled the anti-war movement that once ended the Vietnam War. In Switzerland, even social democrats push for arms to Ukraine, a betrayal Collon finds “disgusting.”

To rebuild resistance, Collon advocates engaging the Global South’s perspectives, where Western propaganda holds less sway. He calls for a citizen-led information movement, encouraging ordinary people to challenge media lies through discussion and fact-checking. His short videos, La Minute Michel, aim to reach overworked audiences with concise analyses, but he stresses the importance of deep study through books to counter emotional manipulation.

## Building a Counter-Narrative: The Power of Independent Media

Collon proposes a global network of independent media to amplify suppressed voices. Initiatives like his Investig’Action collective, which exposes media omissions, face financial and political attacks but persist through public support. He envisions a daily TV journal showcasing hidden images from Gaza, Mali, or Latin America, translated across languages to bridge fragmented audiences. AI-driven tools, like those used by Lottaz’s team, could facilitate this, making content accessible in multiple languages.

The challenge, Collon warns, is avoiding hierarchical structures prone to subversion. Instead, he advocates horizontal coordination, with regular conferences to share resources, translate works like Ukraine: War of Images, and promote collective action. This network would counter NATO’s “cognitive warfare,” which Collon cites as a deliberate strategy to manipulate both foreign and domestic populations.

Michel Collon’s analysis exposes the West’s propaganda as a decaying system, desperate to maintain control amid declining trust and global resistance. By hiding economic motives, distorting history, and silencing dissent, elites perpetuate wars from Yugoslavia to Gaza. Yet, their censorship and repression betray weakness, opening opportunities for a revitalized anti-war movement. Collon’s call for a citizen-driven, globally coordinated independent media offers hope to dismantle these lies. As poverty and fascist risks grow in Europe, the fight for truthful information is not just a journalistic duty but a moral imperative to reclaim peace and justice.
