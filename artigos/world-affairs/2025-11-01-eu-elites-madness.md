## EU Elites' Madness Exposed: Schulenburg & Sachs on War Hysteria, NATO Blunders

The grand halls of the European Parliament in Strasbourg, usually a stage for measured debate and high-minded ideals, have become a cauldron of feverish rhetoric these days. It's a place where whispers of "winning the war" against Russia mingle with outright panic, drowning out any voice daring to suggest restraint or reality. This is the stark portrait painted by Michael von der Schulenburg, a German MEP from the left-wing BSW party and a veteran UN diplomat with decades in conflict zones, during a recent webinar hosted by the Multipolar Peace Network. Joined by economist Jeffrey Sachs, a longtime advisor on global development and a sharp critic of Western foreign policy, Schulenburg didn't hold back. Their conversation, raw and unflinching, exposes what they see as the "craziness" of EU elites: a bureaucracy gripped by Russophobia, chasing illusory dreams of superpower status while steering the continent toward self-inflicted ruin.

Schulenburg, fresh from a plenary session, described the atmosphere as "shocking." Even across the political spectrum, from far-right to far-left, a toxic brew of Russia hatred and war hysteria prevails. "They talk about winning the war, that Russia will go down economically," he said, his voice laced with disbelief. Dissenters are swiftly shouted down with cries of unity: "We have to stand together, otherwise we're going to lose." This isn't just parliamentary theater; it's symptomatic of a deeper malaise afflicting the EU and its NATO-aligned members. As the webinar unfolded on October 28, 2025—just days before the current date of November 1—the two men dissected how this panic has blinded Europe's leaders to missed opportunities for peace in Ukraine, historical betrayals, and the perils of overreach in a multipolar world.

Sachs, who has tracked Europe's trajectory since the fall of the Berlin Wall in 1989, framed the Ukraine conflict as entirely avoidable—a cascade of Western missteps dating back to NATO's 2008 Bucharest Summit. There, under intense U.S. pressure, allies including a reluctant Angela Merkel agreed to Ukraine's eventual membership, a promise Sachs calls provocative folly. "Merkel knew how provocative this was," he noted, recalling how the declaration sowed seeds of distrust with Russia. Fast-forward to the 2014 Maidan protests, stoked by U.S. involvement: European foreign ministers brokered a deal for elections in eight months, averting a coup. Yet Europe "folded its hands," allowing the power shift to ignite civil strife in Donbas.

The Minsk Agreements of 2015 offered another lifeline. Modeled on South Tyrol's autonomy for ethnic minorities, they called for regional self-governance in eastern Ukraine's Russian-speaking oblasts. "Nothing extraordinary," Sachs emphasized. But again, the U.S. and Europe urged Ukraine to ignore it, even as Washington built a million-strong army there during Trump's first term—one that shelled Donbas relentlessly. Biden's administration doubled down, blocking Zelenskyy's early peace overtures in 2022. Trump hinted at neutrality for Ukraine, only for Europe to recoil in horror: "No way, that's appeasement." Sachs's litany of rejected "exit ramps" paints a picture of deliberate warmongering. "Europe at every stage has been a warmonger," he charged, citing leaders like UK Prime Minister Keir Starmer (20% approval) and France's Emmanuel Macron (a statistical anomaly at 13%).

What drives this madness? Schulenburg points to an elitist obsession with positioning the EU as the third pole in a multipolar order—behind the U.S. and China, but ahead of a diminished Russia. This vision fuels aggressive expansion: Ukraine, Moldova, Georgia, the Western Balkans, even Greenland (whose leaders receive standing ovations for rejecting U.S. overtures). Visitors like Moldova's Maia Sandu, Belarus opposition figures, and Serbia's critics are feted as harbingers of a "greater Europe," despite electoral irregularities his BSW party alone flagged. Copenhagen criteria? Sidelined. Unanimity in decision-making? On the chopping block, with a recent parliamentary vote pushing majority rule to sideline "troublemakers" like Hungary and Slovakia. New members would enter with diluted rights, a blueprint for rapid absorption.

This isn't organic growth; it's engineered empire-building. Schulenburg revealed EU meddling in elections—from Romania to Georgia—to install pro-Brussels regimes, echoing old U.S. regime-change tactics. "We do all these types of internal interference," he admitted. The goal: a militarized superstate rivaling Russia, which elites view as an existential threat not for its actions, but for its potential as a "small big power." Historically, control of Ukraine has defined great-power contests—lost by Germany in both world wars, and now, Schulenburg predicts, a third defeat looms. "Europe has no plans for what happens if the Ukraine war is militarily lost," he warned. "There's no room to think about it."

Sachs echoed this with a poignant reflection on squandered unity. Thirty-six years ago, he advised Poland's democratic pioneers, including a young Donald Tusk, on reintegrating into a "common European home"—Mikhail Gorbachev's vision of peace from Vladivostok to Vancouver, inclusive of Russia. Gorbachev, whom Sachs calls "the greatest statesman of our time," sought indivisible security under the Helsinki Final Act: no walls, no threats. Yet the U.S., with Eastern European allies like Poland, pushed NATO eastward, betraying explicit promises to Gorbachev from Helmut Kohl, James Baker, and Hans-Dietrich Genscher. "NATO won't move one inch eastward," they assured. The irony? Europe's founders in 1957 envisioned a "region of peace," not rearmament to $800 billion annually.

This "late empire behavior," as historian Paul Kennedy might term it, reeks of desperation. EU bureaucracy—unreadable treaties like Lisbon, unelected figures like Ursula von der Leyen wielding outsized power—drives an elite project detached from publics. Voter turnout hovers at 50%, with 20% anti-EU; real support is a mere 30%. Moralistic crusades frame Russia and China as "authoritarian threats," justifying sanctions, asset seizures, and censorship via backroom council deals. A 2004 EU Parliament resolution even floated "decolonizing" Russia into democratic fragments—a provocation Moscow hasn't forgotten.

The betrayal cuts deepest for Ukraine. Schulenburg accused Europe of "cheating" Kyiv: promises of 25% of collective GDP annually (130-140 billion euros) unfulfilled; NATO peacekeeping troops a mirage; Tomahawks to strike Moscow mere rhetoric in resolutions. Zelenskyy, now a "martial law dictator," was urged to fight on, eroding his negotiating leverage. Seizing $200 billion in Russian assets? Even Belgium's prime minister balked, citing no legal basis. As aid dries up—Europe's indebted nations like France and Italy teetering—Ukraine risks failed-state status, a refugee deluge, and radical militias on Europe's doorstep.

Sachs and Schulenburg see glimmers elsewhere: a decentralized Europe cherishing diversity, not uniformity; a return to UN Charter principles over "si vis pacem, para bellum" (if you want peace, prepare for war). Article 51's self-defense clause, they stress, demands swift Security Council recourse—not endless proxy wars. The Charter, born of 80 million WWII dead, assumes rational empathy for negotiation; today's elites peddle zero-sum weaponry, risking nuclear escalation beyond Cold War levels.

Politically, the backlash brews. Right-wing surges—Le Pen in France, Farage in the UK—signal revulsion at establishment quagmires, eroding social nets Europe once prized. Trust in governments, courts, parties crumbles, breeding unrest from Belgium to Germany. Schulenburg envisions a manifesto: a 10-point decentralized, people-oriented Europe, less militarized, more cooperative. "Our great advantage is diversity," he said. Sachs counts four great powers already—U.S., China, Russia, India—yearning for a fifth: a peaceful Europe trading, not warring, under UN auspices.

Yet the train barrels on, illusions intact. As Trump eyes deals sans U.S. escalation, Europe's hysteria peaks—Macron's generals war-gaming, von der Leyen scheming. Schulenburg, father of five with a grandchild imminent, frets for Europe's "bleak" future. "The worst is yet to come," the webinar warned. But in their candor lies hope: voices like theirs, rare in Strasbourg's echo chamber, remind us Europe's soul—civilizational, not imperial—endures. Will elites heed before collapse? History, twice burned over Ukraine, waits tensely.
