## Ukraine Front Lines Collapsing Amid Russia Strikes and US Proxy War Tactics

The ongoing conflict in Ukraine has reached a critical juncture, with Russian forces intensifying their operations and Ukrainian defenses showing signs of imminent collapse. In a recent interview, Professor Glenn Diesen, a geopolitical analyst, sat down with Brian Berletic, a former US Marine turned journalist and host of The New Atlas, to dissect the latest developments. Their discussion, titled "NATO Panics Over the Collapse of Ukraine's Front Lines," sheds light on the broader implications of Russia's massive drone and missile strikes, the diplomatic maneuvers under the Trump administration, and the underlying US strategy that frames this as a proxy war against Russia.

The conversation opens with Diesen highlighting Russia's recent escalation: over 800 drones complemented by missiles targeting Ukrainian infrastructure. This comes amid President Trump's diplomatic overtures, which have been met with skepticism. Trump has expressed disappointment in the lack of progress toward peace, vowing new sanctions on Russia. However, Berletic argues that the narrative pushed by NATO and Western media—that Russia is uninterested in peace—is fundamentally flawed. He emphasizes that the Trump administration itself has acknowledged this as a US-initiated proxy war, blaming the Biden era for its inception. "It really is not up to Russia to end a war that is being waged against them by the United States," Berletic states, pointing to the US military command in Germany overseeing Ukraine's forces and the CIA's control over Ukrainian intelligence.

This proxy dynamic is central to understanding the conflict's persistence. Berletic explains that ending the war would require the US to dismantle its command structures, cease funding and arming Ukraine, and withdraw CIA operations—steps the Trump administration shows no intention of taking. Instead, Trump's pre-election rhetoric about freezing the conflict aligns with a long-term US agenda of "strategic sequencing." This concept, drawn from US policy papers, involves prioritizing threats to American global primacy. The US cannot confront Russia, China, Iran, and their allies simultaneously due to resource limitations. Thus, the strategy is to tie down one adversary while focusing on another.

In this case, Berletic notes, the US views China as the primary threat to its hegemony. Freezing the Ukraine conflict would allow a pivot to Asia-Pacific, where allies like Japan, South Korea, and the Philippines are being mobilized under a "division of labor" to encircle China. Europe, meanwhile, is tasked with sustaining the Ukrainian front, channeling public resources into the proxy war. This echoes Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth's February directive, urging Europeans to redirect funds toward containing Russia.

Diesen probes deeper into why the US would relinquish control over Ukraine's military, intelligence, and government—assets built since the 2014 coup. Berletic concurs, recalling how the US rebuilt Ukraine's intelligence from scratch post-coup to sever ties with Russia. The investment is immense: control over hundreds of thousands of soldiers, media, and civil society through NGOs. Surrendering this for genuine peace seems improbable, especially given historical precedents like the 2004 Orange Revolution, which temporarily pulled Ukraine into NATO's orbit before democracy reverted it to neutrality under Yanukovych in 2010.

The Minsk Agreements of 2014-2015 serve as a cautionary tale. Berletic describes them as a freeze that allowed the US and Europe to rebuild Ukraine's military for renewed conflict. Today's efforts mirror this: freeze before collapse, rearm, and resume later. Trump's team, influenced by policy thinkers advocating primacy, sees no value in true decoupling. Instead, they perpetuate wars inherited from previous administrations—Yemen, Iran, and now Venezuela—while preparing for Asia.

A key theme is the illusion of diplomacy. Diesen praises Trump's willingness to engage adversaries, like meeting North Korea's Kim Jong-un or Putin. Yet, Berletic reveals the duplicity: during North Korean talks, US Navy SEALs conducted operations killing civilians; amid Iranian negotiations, strikes were planned. This echoes the 2008 US-Russia "reset" post-Georgia proxy war, which was a ruse to draw Russia in. Trump's push for a Putin-Zelensky meeting is dismissed as futile—Zelensky holds no real power; the US pulls the strings. Berletic quotes an analogy: negotiating with Zelensky is like barking back at a dog instead of addressing its owner.

The absence of a genuine peace concept from the US is telling. Diesen contrasts indivisible security—mutual arms reductions and harmonized interests—with hegemonic peace, where dominance overrides others' concerns. The US clings to the latter, with figures like Marco Rubio acknowledging multipolarity only as an obstacle to unipolar restoration. This mindset risks alienating allies and uniting enemies against America, as Diesen warns.

Media narratives accusing Russia of stringing along Trump invert reality. Berletic stresses Russia's consistency: demands for NATO withdrawal from borders, demilitarization, and an end to the proxy war remain unchanged since day one. The US, posing as mediator while escalating (e.g., sending 3,000 Iran missiles designed for peer conflicts), seeks only a pause. Trump's "strategic ambiguity"—claiming the war wouldn't have happened under him while boasting of arming Ukraine more than Obama—exemplifies wanting it both ways: tough yet peaceful.

On the battlefield, signs of collapse abound. Russia's strikes on energy infrastructure and bridges signal preparation for a major offensive. Berletic describes Russia's "aggressive attrition" strategy: grinding down Ukraine's capacity rather than rapid territorial gains. Western analysts fixate on territory, missing the point—hollowed-out forces lead to exponential collapse, as seen in Syria. Ukraine admits unmanned defenses; reserves are overstretched. Desertion rates, though hard to verify amid information warfare, spike as morale crumbles. Rebuilding brigades requires experienced leadership, impossible under high attrition.

NATO's response? Direct intervention looms. Berletic notes urgency in forming a "coalition of the willing," per Hegseth's directive. Europeans, despite weak economies and unpopular governments (e.g., France's collapse, Germany's instability), are seen as expendable proxies. The EU's bureaucracy, aligned with US interests, smothers sovereignty, ensuring continuity regardless of elections. Leaders like Italy's Meloni promise change but deliver status quo.

Economically, Europe's decoupling from Russia—via sanctions and Nord Stream sabotage—has backfired. Pre-2014 interdependence favored Europe; now, Russia pivots east via Power of Siberia 2, cementing irreversible shifts. Diesen recalls NATO's Stoltenberg selling the war as providing battle-hardened Ukrainians to contain Russia, freeing the US for China. Europeans risk becoming the next proxies, fighting "to the last euro—and soldier."

Unverified reports of British and French reinforcements in Kyiv and Odesa underscore NATO's Black Sea ambitions. Yet, as Berletic warns, intervention won't defeat Russia; it buys time for strategic sequencing. The conflict's resolution lies on the battlefield, with Russia poised to dictate terms post-collapse.

In uncharted territory, the risk of NATO-Russia confrontation escalates. Diesen and Berletic's analysis reveals a grim reality: no genuine peace without US concessions, which aren't forthcoming. The panic in NATO stems from this inexorable logic—Ukraine's lines are cracking, and the proxy war's architects scramble to freeze the inevitable.
