## Russia Preparing Retaliation: Oreshnik Deployment and Potential Seizure of Odessa

In the ongoing Ukraine-Russia conflict, signs point to Russia preparing retaliation following a series of provocative attacks on its energy infrastructure and civilian vessels. This escalation, as discussed in a recent interview between Professor Glenn Diesen and Lt. Col. Daniel Davis, highlights the precarious path toward potential direct confrontation. Davis, a four-time combat veteran and host of the Daniel Davis Deep Dive YouTube channel, emphasizes how Russia is bolstering its arsenal, including the deployment of the hypersonic Oreshnik missile, while contemplating strategic moves like seizing Odessa. With diplomacy faltering, these developments underscore the urgent need for de-escalation to prevent a broader war.

The interview, conducted amid reports of Ukraine's strikes on Russian oil tankers in the Black Sea and even the Mediterranean, reveals a Western strategy that ignores Russian security concerns. Davis argues that Europe and NATO's actions—ranging from sanctions on Russian energy to direct support for attacks on Russian territory—could push Russia to respond more aggressively. Russia preparing retaliation is not mere rhetoric; it's a calculated buildup evident in Putin's statements and military preparations. As Davis notes, the West's insistence on a "just and lasting peace" on its terms alone perpetuates the conflict, disregarding mutual security needs.

### Russia Preparing Retaliation: Attacks on Russian Vessels and Energy Infrastructure

Recent events have intensified the maritime dimension of the war. Ukraine has targeted Russia's "shadow fleet" of oil tankers, which evade sanctions to transport energy exports. Strikes on four vessels signal Kyiv's intent to expand the conflict's scope, aiming to cripple Moscow's military funding. For instance, Ukrainian drones reportedly hit a Russian tanker in the Mediterranean, launched possibly from Greece, marking a significant geographical escalation. Putin has vowed revenge, indicating Russia preparing retaliation could involve symmetric strikes on Ukrainian shipping or infrastructure.

Davis points out that these attacks on civilian vessels border on acts of piracy or war in traditional terms. Yet, the West appears undeterred, continuing to provide intelligence, weapons, and planning support. This has led to a rapid climb up the escalation ladder, with Russia preparing retaliation through enhanced military readiness. Reports confirm Russian strikes on Ukrainian ports like Chornomorsk in retaliation for similar actions. The Black Sea, once a conduit for grain exports, now risks becoming a full naval battleground.

Empirical evidence from conflict analyses shows that such tit-for-tat maritime strikes heighten the risk of miscalculation. Academic studies on escalation in the Ukraine-Russia war highlight how asymmetric warfare, like drone attacks on vessels, can spiral into broader confrontations. Russia, with superior naval and missile capabilities, could overwhelm Ukrainian defenses if provoked further. Davis warns that ignoring Russian red lines—such as attacks on its nuclear deterrent or core infrastructure—invites severe responses.

### The Oreshnik Missile: A Key Element in Russia Preparing Retaliation

Central to Russia preparing retaliation is the Oreshnik missile, a hypersonic intermediate-range ballistic missile (IRBM) capable of speeds exceeding Mach 10. First tested against Ukraine in November 2024, the Oreshnik—Russian for "hazelnut tree"—has been deployed to Belarus, enhancing Russia's strike options. Putin announced its mass production and combat readiness before the new year, signaling a deterrent against Western intervention.

Unlike the RS-26 Rubezh, which remains in limited status, the Oreshnik is designed for rapid, unstoppable strikes. Davis assesses that Russia is stockpiling these missiles not just for deterrence but for potential use in a wider conflict. With hypersonic speeds making interception nearly impossible, the Oreshnik could target Western arms factories or stockpiles in hours, crippling NATO's sustainment capabilities.

Studies on nuclear and hypersonic escalation warn that such weapons lower the threshold for conflict expansion. Russia preparing retaliation with Oreshnik deployment reflects a strategy to overwhelm adversaries before they can mobilize. As Davis explains, Russia's "go-slow" approach in Ukraine allows time to build stockpiles of Kinzhals, glide bombs, and air defense systems, while the West depletes its inventories. This asymmetry favors Russia in a prolonged war, making escalation management critical.

### Strategic Importance of Odessa and Russia Preparing Retaliation

Odessa, Ukraine's primary Black Sea port, holds immense strategic value. Handling 65% of Ukraine's imports and exports, it's vital for grain shipments and military logistics. Davis suggests that attacks on Russian vessels could prompt Russia preparing retaliation by seizing Odessa, cutting Ukraine's maritime access and linking Russian-held territories to Transnistria.

Recent Russian strikes on Odessa's bridges, ports, and energy facilities indicate preparations for such a move. If Europe intervenes—perhaps under French or British security pacts—the conflict could expand. Academic analyses underscore Odessa's role in Russia's broader objectives, potentially including annexing additional oblasts if peace talks fail.

Davis highlights the irony: while Ukraine nears military exhaustion, Russia builds reserves for a decisive push. Seizing Odessa would not only retaliate but reshape the region's geopolitics, denying NATO a foothold. This aligns with Putin's warnings that a larger war would see Russia unleashing full capabilities, far beyond the "surgical" operations in Ukraine.

### Broader Risks of Escalation and the Need for Diplomacy

The interview exposes a Western mindset detached from reality, where Russia preparing retaliation is dismissed as bluffing. Yet, empirical data shows Russia's military production outpacing usage, stockpiling for potential NATO clashes. European leaders like Macron and Starmer advocate for war readiness, ignoring their own depleted forces.

Academic studies on escalation management stress the dangers of manipulating risk, as Russia has done with nuclear rhetoric. Davis fears a nuclear threshold if non-nuclear powers like Germany are targeted first, prompting France or the UK to respond. With Trump pushing for negotiations, the window for a mutually acceptable peace narrows.

Russia preparing retaliation isn't inevitable, but continued provocations—like the Kursk incursion or vessel attacks—make it likely. As Diesen notes, the unipolar era's hubris has led to this brinkmanship. A steeper learning curve is needed to avert catastrophe.

Russia preparing retaliation through Oreshnik deployment and potential Odessa seizure reflects a strategic pivot amid failing diplomacy. Davis's analysis, grounded in military experience, calls for acknowledging realities to prevent a wider, possibly nuclear, war. The West must reconsider its approach, prioritizing mutual security over unilateral demands.
