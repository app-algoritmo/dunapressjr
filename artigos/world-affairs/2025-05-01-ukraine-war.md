**The End of NATO: Andrei Martyanov on the Ukraine War and the Collapse of Western Military Power**

The Ukraine war has exposed deep cracks in NATO's foundation, signaling the potential end of the alliance as a credible military bloc. In this interview with Prof. Glenn Diesen, military and naval expert Andrei Martyanov, a former officer and director in commercial aerospace in the US, outlines why NATO is a "dead man walking" and how the Ukraine conflict has accelerated its decline. Drawing on his extensive knowledge of Russian military strategy and Western military capabilities, Martyanov argues that NATO's obsolescence, coupled with Europe's military impotency and the US's strategic retreat, will lead to the alliance's disintegration, reshaping global geopolitics.

## The Ukraine War: A Military Solution Over Diplomacy

Martyanov asserts that a military solution has always been Russia’s primary approach in Ukraine, despite Western hopes for a diplomatic resolution. Following President Trump’s failed peace plan—rejected by Ukraine’s Zelensky—Russia launched a powerful missile and drone strike, breaking through Ukrainian defense lines around Chasiv Yar and Toretsk. Martyanov dismisses the idea of a ceasefire as proposed by Trump and echoed by French President Macron, arguing that diplomacy merely reflects battlefield realities. Quoting Chinese Premier Zhou Enlai’s rephrasing of Clausewitz—“diplomacy is the continuation of war by other means”—he emphasizes that Russia seeks not a ceasefire but a lasting peace, which requires dismantling the Zelensky regime, a puppet of Western interests led by the US and UK.

The atrocities committed by Ukrainian forces in Kursk, Martyanov notes, have hardened Russia’s resolve. The goal is not just territorial control but the destruction of the “Nazi regime” in Kiev, followed by operations to root out remaining elements through special forces and intelligence efforts. Russia’s recent advances, such as capturing Kamyshevakha in the Kharkiv region, signal a steady push westward, with potential targets like Sumy next on the horizon. Martyanov highlights Russia’s strategic patience, noting that the Kremlin is in no rush, as NATO’s internal dynamics and military weaknesses play into Russia’s hands.

## NATO’s Decline: A Dead Man Walking

Martyanov labels NATO a “dead man walking,” a sentiment echoed by US military figures like Colonel Douglas McGregor, who noted as early as 1997 that the alliance was a “laundromat” rather than a real military force. The Ukraine war has exposed NATO’s vulnerabilities. The 2023 Ukrainian counteroffensive, pushed by the US and UK despite internal dissent, resulted in a massacre of Ukrainian forces, depleting NATO’s weapon stockpiles. Martyanov critiques Western media narratives, such as a *Washington Post* article claiming the counteroffensive was a “victory for NATO” due to increased demand for the alliance. In reality, he argues, NATO has been defeated, unable to supply Ukraine further, while the US signals a strategic retreat from Europe to focus on Asia and the Western Hemisphere.

Europe’s militaries, Martyanov contends, are woefully unprepared for modern warfare. France, the strongest European military, has only 113,000 troops, with technical readiness rates for tanks and aircraft ranging from 15% to 35%. The UK can muster a handful of operational aircraft, and Germany’s Bundeswehr is “worse than a joke.” In contrast, Russia’s conventional and nuclear capabilities far outmatch Europe’s, with the potential to “turn Europe into a parking lot in 25 minutes.” Martyanov cites the 2011 Libya intervention—where France, Italy, and the UK ran out of munitions within 72 hours against a weaker opponent—as evidence of Europe’s military impotency, a situation that has only worsened since.

## The West’s Strategic Miscalculations and Cultural Divide

Martyanov attributes NATO’s decline to a broader collapse of Western strategic thinking, driven by emotional rather than rational objectives. European leaders, he argues, set unrealistic goals—such as forcing Russia back to 1991 borders, demanding reparations, and establishing tribunals for Russian leaders—despite lacking the means to achieve them. This disconnect, as noted by former CIA Russia analyst George Beebe, stems from a lack of strategic planning and an emotional hatred of Russia. A 2017 Sputnik poll revealed that only a quarter of Europeans consider Russians as European, a sentiment Martyanov says has led Russia to reject its 300-year pursuit of European integration.

Culturally, Russia has shifted its identity. By 2021, 64% of Russians no longer identified as European, a figure Martyanov believes has grown since the 2022 conflict. Policies banning Russian athletes, literature, and tourists, alongside statements from figures like former US Ambassador Michael McFaul—who claimed there are “no innocent Russians”—have convinced Russians that the West’s animosity extends beyond Putin to the entire nation. Russia now focuses on consolidating the Eurasian landmass with China, a partnership Martyanov sees as economically dwarfing Europe, while seeking a modus vivendi with the US to manage superpower dynamics.

## The Future of the Conflict: Military and Political Scenarios

Without a political settlement, Martyanov envisions a prolonged military solution in Ukraine. Russia is unlikely to occupy western Ukraine, avoiding a drawn-out guerrilla war akin to Afghanistan. Instead, it may establish proxy governance in key cities like Odessa, Nikolaev, and even Kiev, ensuring NATO’s exclusion while allowing a new generation of Ukrainians to heal from Western influence. Odessa, historically a Russian city, could vote to join Russia, a scenario complicated by past Ukrainian efforts to resettle western Ukrainians there to dilute its Russian identity.

Martyanov warns that Ukraine will remain a “cesspool of terrorist activities,” fueled by Western arms. However, Russia’s strategy focuses on long-term political changes, such as federalization or confederalization of Ukraine, isolating it from NATO’s reach. The Kremlin’s distrust of Western agreements—highlighted by Foreign Minister Lavrov’s insistence on addressing root causes—ensures that any deal must be enforceable, a challenge given the West’s history of broken promises.

## Chaos in the US: Trump’s War and Military Impotence

Martyanov critiques the US’s role as a mediator, noting its position as the primary actor in this proxy war. The US, through NATO, has driven the conflict, yet now seeks to walk away, leaving Europe to manage the fallout. Trump’s administration, Martyanov argues, is chaotic, with figures like General Keith Kellogg—whom he calls a “loser” with no grasp of modern warfare—pushing unrealistic plans like dividing Ukraine with NATO peacekeepers. In contrast, Fred Witoff’s proposal to address root causes and Ukraine’s neutrality aligns more with Russian demands but lacks traction.

Trump’s claim that this is “Biden’s war” rings hollow after 100 days of continued US involvement, making it “Trump’s war,” as McGregor asserts. Martyanov dismisses the US military’s capabilities, noting that even a full mobilization would fail against Russia conventionally, potentially forcing the US to resort to nuclear options. The US’s technological lag—lagging behind Russia by two generations in weapons systems—further undermines its position, with Western arms paraded as trophies by Russian forces.

## A New Geopolitical Reality

The Ukraine war marks the end of NATO as a viable military alliance, exposing its obsolescence and the West’s strategic failures. Martyanov’s analysis reveals a Europe militarily impotent and emotionally driven, unable to sustain the conflict without US support, which is itself waning. Russia, having rejected its European aspirations, is forging a new path with China, prioritizing Eurasian integration over Western reconciliation. The war’s outcome—whether through military victory or a protracted political solution—will reshape global power dynamics, leaving NATO a relic of a bygone era. As Martyanov warns, the West’s refusal to face reality risks not only defeat in Ukraine but a broader reckoning with its own decline.
