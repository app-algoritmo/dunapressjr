## Victory Day 2025: Col. Douglas Macgregor on Russia’s Ukraine Triumph and NATO’s Decline

On May 9, 2025, at 09:45 AM CEST, Prof. Glenn Diesen hosted a compelling interview with Colonel Douglas Macgregor on his program, streamed live on YouTube, titled *"Victory Day - Russia Already Won the War."* The date marked Russia’s Victory Day, commemorating the Soviet Union’s defeat of Nazi Germany in World War II, a pivotal moment in Russian historical memory due to the staggering 26-27 million lives lost. Macgregor, a retired U.S.

Army Colonel and military strategist, analyzed the significance of this year’s celebrations—attended by Chinese President Xi Jinping and leaders from the Global South, but notably absent Western figures—against the backdrop of Russia’s ongoing war in Ukraine. He argued that Russia has already won, criticized U.S. policy under President Trump for escalating the conflict, and warned of broader geopolitical ramifications, including NATO’s potential collapse and a looming Middle East crisis.

## Victory Day 2025: A Shifting Global Landscape

The 80th anniversary of Victory Day saw Moscow host a grand parade, with President Vladimir Putin leading the event alongside Xi Jinping and leaders from 29 nations, primarily from the BRICS bloc and Global South, as reported by *Times Now World*. Unlike past celebrations where Western leaders were invited to symbolize unity, this year’s event highlighted Russia’s pivot away from the West, a shift Macgregor attributed to deteriorating relations amid the Ukraine war.

In the U.S., Victory Day (celebrated on May 8 as VE Day) holds less significance, with Macgregor noting that Americans historically focused more on the Japanese surrender in August 1945 due to Pearl Harbor’s direct impact. He contrasted this with Russia’s deep reverence for the day, despite the Soviet Union’s horrific losses under Stalin, whom Macgregor criticized for unnecessary sacrifices and a lack of true de-Stalinization in Russian narratives.

Macgregor argued that Russia uses Victory Day to foster unity with allies like China, which suffered immensely under Japanese occupation, and other nations seeking economic ties with Moscow. He lamented the West’s absence, stating, “We should show them respect and understanding, but we won’t,” reflecting the U.S.’s current stance. The parade, featuring advanced weaponry and contingents from 13 countries, was a display of Russian strength, but also a diplomatic signal of its growing influence, as Western isolation efforts falter.

## The Ukraine War: Trump’s Misstep and Russia’s Strategic Patience

Macgregor was unequivocal in his assessment: “Russia has already won the war.” He criticized Trump for reversing his campaign promise to end U.S. involvement, noting that after initially advocating withdrawal, Trump was “besieged by people” convincing him to frame any resolution as a personal “win.”

This led to a “180-degree turn,” with Trump escalating the conflict by shipping weapons to Ukraine following a mineral deal for rare earth access, ratified by Ukraine’s legislature on May 8. Macgregor called this a “serious mistake,” predicting that Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky “will not last,” rendering the deal moot. He argued that the mineral agreement, while providing the U.S. with valuable resources, has made the conflict “Trump’s war,” exposing him to blame if it collapses like Afghanistan.

Russia’s unilateral ceasefires on Easter and Victory Day, Macgregor suggested, were less about testing peace prospects and more about reinforcing Putin’s narrative of moral superiority, tied to Russia’s Christian identity and historical memory. Putin, he noted, has made it clear the war will end on Russia’s terms, dictated by “the logic of the battlefield.” Macgregor highlighted Russia’s military buildup, with heavy equipment amassed in the rear, as a precaution against potential NATO intervention, but also as preparation for a possible major offensive to secure the Dnieper River line—a natural barrier historically marking Europe’s western frontier—or even to seize Odessa, cutting off Ukraine’s grain exports and strangling its economy.

## The West’s Historical Amnesia and NATO’s Fragile Future

The interview explored how World War II narratives shape current conflicts. In Russia, Victory Day links the Ukraine war to the fight against fascism, with Putin framing it as a battle against “neo-Nazism,” a narrative Macgregor acknowledged has roots in Ukraine’s real Nazi nostalgia, tied to historical grievances like Stalin’s 1929-1932 purges, which killed millions of Ukrainians.

In the West, leaders portray Putin as a “new Hitler,” a trope Diesen noted is common in justifying wars, while Xi Jinping’s comparison of U.S. actions to Hitler’s armies, reported by the *Financial Times*, reflects escalating rhetoric. Macgregor dismissed this as “bluster” with domestic roots, aimed at legitimizing Sino-Russian cooperation within BRICS, but warned of deeper tensions, particularly over Taiwan, which China views as a strategic redline akin to its historical vulnerabilities during the Opium Wars.

Macgregor predicted NATO’s collapse, arguing, “Whenever this war ends, NATO will end.” He cited the U.S.’s fragile economic position and historical overreach, noting that NATO’s expansion was driven by hubris and miscalculations about Russia’s resilience. The proxy war in Ukraine, acknowledged by figures like Marco Rubio and Boris Johnson, has exposed NATO’s limitations, with Macgregor asserting that only a nuclear escalation—which no sane leader wants—could stop Russia. He foresaw the European Union’s disintegration, driven by divergent national perspectives and economic turmoil, with countries like Germany, France, and the U.K. facing internal nationalist movements against globalist elites, exemplified by Germany’s AFD party, which the government labels extremist to suppress opposition.

## Ukraine’s Internal Dynamics and Path to Resolution

Macgregor painted a grim picture of Ukraine’s internal state, arguing that Zelensky’s regime is unsustainable. Public sentiment, he claimed, overwhelmingly desires peace, with many Ukrainians saying, “We’ve had enough,” but the Ukrainian secret police stifle dissent through violence. He speculated that Zelensky’s rule could end through various means—Russian action, American withdrawal, or internal nationalist or military pressure—but emphasized that Western leaders, unlike their pragmatic predecessors in the 1950s-70s, lack the will to intervene decisively. He referenced Eisenhower’s support for Austrian neutrality as a model, lamenting the absence of such pragmatism today.

The war’s resolution, Macgregor predicted, will be dictated by Russia, not Washington. Putin’s patience, rooted in a historical understanding of the region, avoids occupying western Ukraine, which he sees as distinctly non-Russian, echoing Alexander Solzhenitsyn’s call for Russia to focus on its own people. Macgregor suggested Russia might seek a buffer zone along the Dnieper or control Odessa, but any lasting solution would require neighboring countries—Lithuania, Latvia, Estonia, Poland, Slovakia, Hungary, Romania, and Moldova—to participate in border agreements, a process Putin likely anticipates.

## Broader Geopolitical Risks: The Middle East Powder Keg

Macgregor concluded with a stark warning about the Middle East, where Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s actions could ignite a regional war. He posited two scenarios: Netanyahu accelerating the destruction in Gaza, prompting Egypt and Jordan to intervene, or directly attacking Iran. In either case, Iran’s pledge to support Egypt, alongside potential Turkish involvement, could escalate the conflict, with Saudi Arabia and the UAE unlikely to back mass atrocities in Gaza.

Macgregor criticized the influence of AIPAC and pro-Israel billionaires on U.S. policy, noting that Netanyahu’s confidence in U.S. support—despite personal tensions with Trump—poses a significant risk. Trump’s desire to avoid war with Iran, Macgregor noted, may be undermined by these dynamics, urging him to focus on dismantling America’s “meddling networks” like the National Endowment for Democracy, which he called a “CIA front.”

## A War Russia Has Won, A West in Denial

Colonel Douglas Macgregor’s interview on Victory Day 2025 revealed a sobering reality: Russia has effectively won the Ukraine war, leveraging its military dominance and strategic patience, while the West, led by a misguided U.S. policy under Trump, clings to a failing proxy conflict. The 9th of May celebrations underscored Russia’s global realignment, with allies like China signaling a new world order amid Western decline. As NATO faces potential collapse and Europe grapples with internal divisions, Macgregor’s warnings of a Middle East escalation highlight the broader stakes. Without a pragmatic shift away from hubris and interventionism, the West risks further marginalization, leaving Russia to dictate the war’s end on its terms, with profound implications for global stability.
