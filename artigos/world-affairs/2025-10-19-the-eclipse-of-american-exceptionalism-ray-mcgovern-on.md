## Trump-Putin Budapest Summit: Ray McGovern on Why Maximum Pressure Failed and Ukraine's Path to Neutrality

In the swirling vortex of modern geopolitics, where bluster often masquerades as strategy, few voices cut through the noise with the clarity of Ray McGovern. A 27-year CIA veteran who chaired National Intelligence Estimates and briefed President Reagan on the President's Daily Brief, McGovern brings a lifetime of dissecting intelligence and policy to his latest conversation with Professor Glenn Diesen. Their discussion, captured in a revealing YouTube interview, lays bare the crumbling edifice of U.S. "maximum pressure" tactics under Donald Trump. What emerges is not just a critique of bombast, but a sobering roadmap for why threats—from economic sanctions to missile saber-rattling—have predictably faltered against great powers like Russia, China, Iran, and even India. As the world edges toward a potential Trump-Putin summit in Budapest, McGovern's insights feel less like prophecy and more like postmortem on a strategy doomed from the start.

At the heart of their exchange is Trump's negotiation style: a cocktail of threats, personal insults, and deal-making flair that Diesen likens to a "great deal" mirage. Trump, McGovern observes, operates from a narcissistic worldview, convinced—as his PR machine once proclaimed—that he "can move mountains by the sheer force of his will." Yet reality, that stubborn adversary, has intruded harshly. China has retaliated with export controls on rare earth minerals, vital for U.S. military tech, effectively wielding a "demilitarizing" counterpunch. Russia remains immovable on its red line: halting NATO's eastward creep into Ukraine. Iran shrugs off sanctions, accelerating its nuclear ambitions, while India—once a reliable partner—threatens to pivot away if pressed too hard. "What a revolting development this is," McGovern quips, channeling old comedian Jimmy Durante, capturing Trump's oblivious march into a multipolar wall.

McGovern, drawing from decades in the intelligence trenches, doesn't mince words: this isn't diplomacy; it's an alternative born of delusion. Traditional statecraft, he argues, thrives on mutual respect and predictability—qualities the Russians prize above all. Putin, ever the pragmatist, has responded not with mirror-image aggression but with calculated calm. Advised by what McGovern calls "the best psychiatrist" (a retired expert he consulted), the Kremlin playbook is simple: stay composed, affirm Trump's ego without condescension, and gently steer toward shared realities. Lavrov and Putin have heaped praise on Trump—floating Nobel Peace Prize nods, no less—while dismissing his barbs as mere "style." It's obsequious, perhaps, but effective. As Maria Zakharova put it, "This is a style we have to get reconciled to."

Nowhere is this dynamic more evident than in the Tomahawk cruise missile saga—a three-week tempest that McGovern brands a "red herring" and "canard." Trump floated the idea of arming Ukraine with these ship- or sub-launched weapons, ostensibly to counter Russian advances in Donbas. But practicality evaporates under scrutiny: Ukraine lacks the naval assets to deploy them, and land-based canisters would take years to improvise. Lavrov called it a "suck-up to the Europeans" at the Valdai Forum on September 30, 2024, while Putin, the next day, dismissed it as "another way to distract attention from domestic problems in Europe." True to form, a two-and-a-half-hour Trump-Putin call on October 17 preempted Zelensky's White House visit. Trump, in a pre-meeting Q&A, pivoted: "We are sympathetic but we don't want to give away things that we need for our own national defense." Zelensky, landing to fanfare that never materialized—greeted only by the chief of protocol—must have sensed the chill.

This isn't Trump's first feint. Recall the 50-day ultimatum for a Ukraine ceasefire in Alaska, which morphed into talks sans preconditions. Or the crippling sanctions threat that dissolved into dialogue. Patterns repeat because, as McGovern notes, Putin knows Trump holds the nuclear codes—and prefers a pliable narcissist to Biden's intransigence. The Budapest summit, hosted by Hungary's Viktor Orbán (himself EU-punished for Moscow outreach), looms as the next act. Trump envisions a three-way huddle, though personal animosities might relegate Zelensky to a side room for the verdict. "This is what we've decided," McGovern imagines Trump declaring, echoing the Anchorage playbook.

Beneath the tactical theater lies a deeper truth: Russia has already won. Donbas advances—fueled by unprecedented missile and drone barrages—have demilitarized Ukraine to 90% of Moscow's goal, per McGovern's assessment. The other pillar, denazification, proves thornier. Since NATO-standard buildups began in 2011, Ukraine's forces have glorified figures like Stepan Bandera, whose fascist ties during WWII still echo in Azov Battalion iconography. Russians won't abide a regime "that praises people like [Stepan] Bandera," McGovern insists, tracing the rot to Victoria Nuland and Geoffrey Pyatt's 2014 machinations.

That leaked call—posted on YouTube weeks before the Maidan coup—remains a smoking gun of U.S. meddling. Nuland, Assistant Secretary for European Affairs, and Pyatt, U.S. Ambassador to Kyiv, divvied up cabinet posts, deeming some "too rightist" or outright "Nazi." When Pyatt flagged EU qualms, Nuland's infamous retort: "Fuck the EU." Nuland later boasted of $5 billion funneled since 1991 for "Ukrainian aspirations" toward the West—a euphemism for regime change that birthed neonazi dependencies Zelensky now navigates uneasily.

McGovern weaves this into a broader tapestry of American hubris, contrasting it with a 2013 high-water mark in U.S.-Russia ties. Obama's "red line" on Syria's chemical weapons—crossed in the Ghouta false flag attack near Damascus—threatened Tomahawk strikes. Veteran Intelligence Professionals for Sanity, including McGovern, warned it was a rebel-orchestrated hoax to provoke intervention. Cornered, Obama deferred to Congress and huddled with Putin in St. Petersburg. The Russian president, fresh from Olympics euphoria, brokered Syria's chemical arsenal surrender under UN supervision aboard a specialized vessel. Kerry, hawkishly touring Europe for war support, was sidelined. Putin penned a New York Times op-ed praising the "increasing trust" between leaders, but landed a subtle jab at Obama's "American exceptionalism," invoking that all nations are "created equal" before God.

Six months later, the idyll shattered. Nuland's $5 billion bet paid off in the advertised coup, catching even Putin off-guard—he lingered in Sochi, assured by German, British, and Polish guarantees of a peaceful Yanukovych transition. Chaos ensued; Crimea's fate sealed the rift. McGovern recounts Putin's national security huddle on February 15, 2014: Protect Sevastopol's ice-free naval base from NATO. Crimea, transferred to Ukraine in 1954 as a Khrushchevian "gift" to bolster ties amid post-Stalin politicking, reverted via a 97% plebiscite—legally dubious to the West, but a fait accompli born of perceived existential threat.

Obama grasped the perils, confiding in 2015: "The worst possible thing we could do… [is] to give [Ukrainians] the idea that they could prevail over a much stronger Russia." Then-Deputy Secretary Tony Blinken concurred: For every U.S. weapon, Russia could match, double, triple, quadruple. Fast-forward to Biden: Ukraine as proxy, "for as long as it takes"—to the last Ukrainian. Trump, three months prior, echoed Obama: Ukrainians fight valiantly but are "no match for a much stronger Russia."

Enter the invasion's provocations. McGovern tallies them: NATO missile pledges ignored; draft treaties spurned. Biden's January 30, 2021, call promised no offensive arms in Ukraine—a commitment Wendy Sherman feigned ignorance of in Geneva talks, with Blinken later shrugging it off as a "Christmas alone" gaffe. By February 12, 2022—10 days pre-invasion—Biden stonewalled again. The incursion? Limited, 90,000 troops probing Kyiv as leverage for neutrality talks. Istanbul in April 2022 yielded initials: No NATO for Ukraine, Russian pullback. Then Boris Johnson swooped in, per Ukrainian and Russian negotiators: No deal, or no Western support. Tanks reversed as goodwill; the war prolonged.

McGovern floats "Finlandization" as salve—a neutral Ukraine, sovereign yet non-aligned, mirroring Finland's post-WWII pact with Moscow. Helsinki balanced autonomy with deference, prospering sans subjugation. Recent scholarship revives it amid war fatigue, though critics warn it risks emboldening Putin without guarantees. Diesen, from Norway's Nordic perch, nods to Finland's twice-savvy compromises: dodging Nazis, then Soviets.

Europe, meanwhile, reels as bystander. Convinced in 2022 to proxy-fund U.S. folly, capitals now foot energy bills and refugee waves sans sway. Trump's barbs—nicknaming leaders, complimenting Zelensky's "little jacket" while condescending—wear thin, save for Putin's icy poise. "Tit for tat" temptations abound, but restraint prevails. As McGovern reflects, from LBJ's "big hat, no cattle" to Biden's hot-mic bravado ("You don't f*ck with the Bidens"), U.S. arrogance peaked post-WWII. Now, multipolarity humbles it.

Optimism tempers caution: Budapest could de-escalate, enlisting Trump to enforce neutrality, demilitarization, and denazification. Putin, winning, needs concessions minimally—lipstick on defeat's pig, as McGovern wryly puts it. Russians prioritize U.S. ties; Ukraine's secondary. If Zelensky balks, Trump might retort: "Talk to your European friends—they're broke." Absent U.S. personnel, Tomahawks demand years; escalation's folly.

McGovern's candor—honed briefing Reagan, debunking Syria hoaxes—illuminates why "as long as it takes" became "until it breaks." Trump, albatross chafing, seeks off-ramp; Putin, patient, obliges. As Diesen notes, war ends on Washington-Moscow terms; Europe's emotional pleas echo hollow. From 2013's trust to 2022's trenches, history indicts coercion. Budapest beckons not as panacea, but pivot—toward realism, if egos yield.
