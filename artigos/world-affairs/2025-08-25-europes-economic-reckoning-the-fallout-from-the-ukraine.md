## Europe's Economic Apocalypse: Consequences of the Ukraine Proxy War

In the shadow of the ongoing conflict in Ukraine, Europe finds itself teetering on the edge of an economic precipice. What began as a proxy war against Russia, backed by fervent European support, has morphed into a self-inflicted wound that's bleeding the continent dry. Drawing from a candid conversation between Professor Glen Diesen and market analyst Alex Krainer, this article delves into the harsh realities facing Europe today. It's a story of misplaced bets, lost opportunities, and a looming crisis that could reshape the continent for generations.

The roots of Europe's troubles run deep, predating the escalation in Ukraine but exacerbated by it. For years, the continent has been navigating a perfect storm of challenges: the lingering effects of the 2020 pandemic, aggressive net-zero policies that prioritized environmental goals over energy security, and now, the fallout from severed ties with Russia. Germany, once the industrial powerhouse of Europe, exemplifies this decline. Starting in 2000, the country began phasing out its nuclear power plants, culminating in the shutdown of the last one in April 2023. This move, coupled with the destruction of the Nord Stream pipelines, has cut off access to cheap, reliable Russian natural gas—a lifeline for German manufacturing.

Krainer paints a vivid picture of what this loss means: "The cost of about $20 billion of Russian gas were powering the creation of $2 trillion of German final industrial products that they were then exporting to China, to the United States, and elsewhere." Without this affordable energy, Germany's export-driven economy is in jeopardy. Industries that once thrived on low-cost inputs are now facing skyrocketing expenses, leading to de-industrialization. As factories shutter or relocate, jobs vanish, and the ripple effects spread across the EU.

But the economic woes go beyond energy. Europe's leaders went "all in" on the proxy war, hoping for a decisive victory over Russia that would allow them to destabilize the country, enact regime change, and seize Russian assets. The frozen $300 billion in Russian reserves—mostly held in Europe, with about $180 billion in Belgium's Euroclear, $70 billion in France, and $37 billion in Britain—were seen as a potential windfall. These funds could repay war loans to Ukraine, collateralize new debt, fund infrastructure, and even support rearmament efforts.

Reality, however, has dashed these hopes. With Russia prevailing in Ukraine, those loans to Kyiv—hundreds of billions of dollars—have turned toxic. "That would have been good debt," Krainer notes, but now they're "bad loans" with no repayment in sight. Worse still, the incoming Trump administration's outreach to Vladimir Putin could redirect these frozen assets to the United States. Krainer predicts a deal where Trump unfreezes the funds in exchange for Russian investments in America, sweetening the pot with relaxed sanctions. For Russia, diversifying away from Europe makes sense; for the U.S., it's a boon to their economy.

This scenario spells disaster for Europe, already mired in catastrophic fiscal positions. The EU and Britain were counting on these assets to kickstart a credit cycle, create jobs, and stabilize budgets. If they flow to the U.S. instead, Europe hits a "dead end." Krainer emphasizes the subservience of European leaders to Washington: "If Trump says, 'Free up those assets and send them over to New York,' the Europeans are going to have to comply." Historical precedents, like the 2013 incident where European countries closed airspace to Bolivian President Evo Morales's plane at the CIA's behest, underscore this dynamic. It's a violation of international law, but compliance is the norm.

The U.S., meanwhile, is poised to capitalize on Europe's weakness. Tariffs on European goods, incentives for industries to relocate stateside (kickstarted by Biden's Inflation Reduction Act), and demands for Europe to buy American energy and weapons all point to a strategy of extraction. As Diesen observes, "Wars can be very good business, but not if you're losing them." The U.S. isn't willing to sink with Europe; instead, it's maneuvering for advantage, even hinting that a fractured EU would allow for more favorable bilateral deals.

Trump's pitch—that America can pull itself out of unsustainable debt by making others "pay back"—hinges on the U.S.'s unique strengths: sovereign monetary policy, the dollar's dominance as the global reserve currency, and the ability to attract foreign investment. While the U.S. faces its own debt crisis—$37 trillion in public debt alone, ballooning to $110 trillion in total credit market debt—Krainer believes it has more "maneuvering space" than Europe or Japan. Bond markets reflect this: Since the U.S. withdrawal from Afghanistan in 2021, American Treasury bonds have declined about 13% from their peak, compared to 24% for European bonds and a staggering 32% for British ones. This volatility signals eroding confidence in the UK and EU's ability to service debts.

Britain emerges as the "weak link" in the West, its desperation evident in the conflict. If London could secure a defeat or stalemate in Ukraine, it might lock the U.S. into continued involvement, keeping the financial ship afloat. But with America stepping back, Europe is left holding the bag—buying U.S. weapons to arm Ukraine, further straining budgets.

European leaders' responses reveal a mix of denial and panic. Recent meetings in Washington, where figures like Emmanuel Macron, Friedrich Merz, Ursula von der Leyen, and Giorgia Meloni faced Trump, were telling. Their platitudes—calls for ceasefires or saving children—lacked substance, as Krainer describes: "They were just issuing platitudes to make themselves sound relevant." Von der Leyen's moral posturing, invoking children's welfare, was swiftly dismissed by Trump: "That's not why we're here."

This reliance on moral framing is a hallmark of European politics, Diesen argues. By casting issues like immigration, climate, gender, or foreign policy in absolute moral terms, opposition is silenced. "If you discuss immigration, there's one thing you're allowed to say because it's moral, and the other one is you're a racist." In Ukraine, this manifested as framing the war as a battle for democracy and human rights, justifying endless arms supplies while blocking diplomacy. Slogans like "the war ends when Russia decides to pull out" ignore Russia's existential concerns about NATO expansion.

Yet, behind the rhetoric, leaders must recognize the severity. They've alienated Russia and China, bet everything on U.S. support, and now face abandonment. No Plan B exists; complacency in the post-WWII order left them without independent strategies for security, economy, or diplomacy. As the center of global gravity shifts eastward—to China, India, and beyond—Europe risks becoming a peripheral player.

The frozen assets add intrigue. While leaders claim they've only touched the interest (deemed "okay" by some twisted logic), discrepancies suggest otherwise. France's holdings reportedly dropped from $71 billion to $25 billion, hinting at Macron's government dipping in—explaining his war enthusiasm and panic over defeat. Returning funds post-peace could expose theft, leading to bankruptcy or worse: "Driving out the guillotines," as Krainer quips, evoking French revolutionary traditions.

Looking ahead, Europe's path is fraught. Krainer warns of EU dissolution and NATO's collapse, potentially sparking social revolts, revolutions, or civil wars in France, Britain, and Germany. To avert this, outreach to Russia for a common security architecture is essential, rather than moral grandstanding.

A chilling escalation looms in the Balkans, potentially opening a new front. Tensions in Bosnia-Herzegovina—between Serbs, Croats, and Bosnian Muslims—are rising, with provocations like a Wahhabi leader organizing prayers in a Croatian village, funded oddly through Serbia (likely via British NGOs). Protests in Serbia trace back to Croatian funding, again British-linked. The Royal United Services Institute has dubbed the Balkans the "inevitable second front" in the West's war on Russia.

Echoing history, from the 1914 assassination in Sarajevo to staged atrocities in the 1990s (like the Ahmići massacre, detailed in Carol Hodge's book), these tactics "shake the jar"—instigating divisions where none existed. In Yugoslavia, intermarried families turned on each other; in Ukraine, language bans and demonization campaigns fractured society. Similar playbook in Syria: stoking ethnic fears to provoke overreactions.

As Krainer urges, informed citizens must counter propaganda to prevent another calamity. Europe's leaders, by thoughtlessly following financial elites, have led the continent to this brink. The economic apocalypse isn't coming—it's here, demanding urgent reckoning.
