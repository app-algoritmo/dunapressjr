## OECD migration: A Data-Driven Analysis

The concept of international migration has long been a cornerstone of global economic and social dynamics, shaping societies in profound ways. In recent days, a simple yet striking post on X (formerly Twitter) by tech visionary Elon Musk has ignited widespread conversation about this phenomenon. Reacting to a chart shared by user @iamyesyouareno, Musk's one-word response—"Wow"—accompanied by the stark label "Civilizational suicide," captured the attention of millions.

The chart in question illustrates the percentage of international migrants as a share of the population across select OECD countries in 1990, 2010, and 2024. What makes this data so compelling is not just the numbers, but the story they tell: a dramatic transformation in the demographic makeup of some of the world's wealthiest nations.

At its core, the chart highlights how OECD countries—home to about 1.3 billion people and representing advanced economies—have experienced a near-doubling of migrant shares over three decades. For instance, Switzerland's figure jumped from 18.7% in 1990 to 31.1% in 2024, while Australia's climbed from 23.3% to 30.4%. These aren't isolated cases; they reflect broader trends driven by labor demands, aging populations, and geopolitical upheavals.

But to fully grasp the implications, we must expand beyond the highlighted nations to encompass all 38 OECD members. This article draws on empirical data from authoritative sources like the United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs (UN DESA) and the OECD's own migration databases to provide a comprehensive view. We'll examine the trends, underlying causes, economic benefits and challenges, and what the future might hold for international migrants in OECD countries.

This representation underscores the universal upward trajectory, with some countries accelerating faster than others due to policy choices and external pressures.

This chart reveals a clear pattern: the international migrants OECD share has risen steadily, with an average increase of about 7-10 percentage points across most members since 1990. Luxembourg stands out as an extreme, reaching 47.2% in 2024, largely due to its role as a financial hub attracting cross-border workers from neighboring countries. Conversely, nations like Japan and Mexico maintain lower shares—2.3% and 1.1%, respectively—reflecting more restrictive policies or geographic factors.

Delving deeper into the data, the surge in international migrants in OECD countries can be attributed to several interconnected factors. First, economic disparities play a pivotal role. High-income OECD economies offer better job prospects, higher wages, and superior living standards, drawing workers from developing regions. According to UN DESA's 2024 International Migrant Stock report, the absolute number of international migrants worldwide reached 304 million by mid-2024, up from 153 million in 1990—a near-doubling that mirrors population growth but amplifies shares in destination countries like those in the OECD. In Europe, for example, the European Union's freedom of movement has facilitated intra-OECD migration, while external pressures from conflicts in the Middle East and Africa have boosted humanitarian inflows.

Aging populations in OECD countries further fuel this trend. With fertility rates below replacement levels in most members—averaging 1.5 children per woman—these nations face labor shortages in sectors like healthcare, construction, and technology. International migrants, often younger and more mobile, fill these gaps. The OECD's International Migration Outlook 2024 notes that in 2022, temporary labor migration permits rose 16% year-on-year, exceeding pre-COVID levels, with over 2.4 million issued across the bloc. This isn't just about numbers; it's about sustainability. Without such inflows, economies like Germany's, where international migrants now comprise 19.8% of the population, could see GDP growth stall by 0.5-1% annually, as per econometric models from the World Bank.

Yet, the rise of international migrants in OECD countries isn't without challenges. Integration remains a flashpoint, as evidenced by Musk's provocative framing. Rapid demographic shifts can strain public services, housing, and social cohesion. In Sweden, where the share hit 21.4% in 2024, studies link high migration to increased pressure on welfare systems, with non-EU migrants facing unemployment rates twice that of natives. Cultural clashes, language barriers, and perceptions of "replacement" have fueled populist movements across Europe and North America. The UN's data shows that while migrants contribute positively—remittances from OECD-based workers totaled $800 billion globally in 2023—the short-term fiscal costs can be high, estimated at 1-2% of GDP in host countries during peak influxes.

Economically, however, the benefits are undeniable. International migrants in OECD countries drive innovation and entrepreneurship. The OECD reports that 17% of self-employed individuals in 2022 were migrants, up from 11% in 2006, creating an additional 0.2 jobs per working-age migrant through business ventures. In the United States, with a 14.8% migrant share, immigrants founded 55% of unicorn startups valued over $1 billion, according to the National Foundation for American Policy. Similarly, Canada's points-based system has ensured that its 22.2% migrant population is highly skilled, boosting productivity in tech and healthcare.

Looking at regional variations sharpens this picture. Northern Europe, including Sweden and Norway (18.2% in 2024), has seen the fastest growth due to generous asylum policies post-2015 refugee crisis. Southern Europe, like Spain (18.5%), experienced a boom from Latin American and North African inflows, transforming from emigration to immigration hubs. Asia-Pacific OECD members present a contrast: Australia's selective policies yield a stable 30.4% share, while Japan's 2.3% reflects cultural insularity despite labor needs.

Policy responses vary widely, underscoring the tension between openness and control. Canada's model emphasizes economic migration, with 80% of permanent residents selected for skills. In contrast, the UK's post-Brexit points system aims to curb low-skilled inflows, yet its migrant share still rose to 17.1%. The OECD recommends balanced approaches: investing in integration programs, like language training, which yield a 20-30% employment boost for migrants, per longitudinal studies.

As we approach 2030, projections from UN DESA suggest the international migrants OECD share could climb another 5-7 points if current trends persist, driven by climate displacement and post-pandemic recovery. This isn't "suicide," as Musk quipped, but evolution—provided policies adapt. The data urges nuance: celebrate contributions while addressing strains. In a interconnected world, managing international migration isn't optional; it's essential for prosperity.
