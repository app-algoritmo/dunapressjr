## China and Russia Industrial Capitalism vs. US Neoliberal Chaos: Michael Hudson’s Economic Insights

In a profound interview with Prof. Glenn Diesen, renowned economist Michael Hudson explores the reorganization of the global economy, contrasting the industrial capitalism of China and Russia with the neoliberalism of the United States. Hudson, a prolific author on economic systems, argues that the era of US-centric globalization—characterized by American dominance in technology, transportation, banking, and currency—has ended. He examines China’s spectacular rise, Russia’s economic resilience despite Western sanctions, and the US’s destructive neoliberal policies, which prioritize rent-seeking over competition, ultimately undermining its global position.

## The End of US-Centric Globalization

Hudson begins by noting a broad consensus that America-centric globalization, where the world integrated under US-led systems like SWIFT, the dollar, and development banks, has largely collapsed. This model, often equated with the “Americanization” of the world, relied on free markets that disproportionately benefited US industries and technologies. However, as global economic power shifts, debates rage over whether globalization is fragmenting into regional blocs or being led by new powers like China. Hudson argues that the US, rather than competing economically, has adopted a destructive approach, seeking to disrupt rivals like China and Russia through sanctions, military pressure, and regime change, reflecting a deep-seated “anti-Chinese passion” tinged with racism.

## China’s Rise: A Model of Industrial Capitalism

China’s unprecedented economic ascent, Hudson asserts, mirrors the industrial strategies of the US and Germany in the late 19th century. Central to China’s success is its retention of key public utilities, especially banking and credit, in the public domain. Unlike the West, where private financial classes dominate, China lacked a wealthy industrial elite to demand high-interest loans for development. Instead, it “reinvented the wheel,” creating its own fiat currency to finance infrastructure like railroads and roads, much like the American colonies did in the 17th and 18th centuries. By self-financing its growth, China avoided the financialization that plagues Western economies, industrializing its financial system to support production rather than rent-seeking.

Addressing criticisms of China’s “neo-mercantilist” export strategy, Hudson clarifies that China initially maximized exports to accrue foreign reserves, a tactic used by the US, Germany, and others in the 19th century. However, in recent weeks, China has shifted inward, halting imports and exports to the US and Europe, focusing on its domestic market to raise living standards and invest in technology and productivity. This “win-win” approach, exemplified by President Xi’s recent engagements in Vietnam, contrasts sharply with the US’s “America First” mercantilism, which Hudson describes as exploitative, aiming to extract economic surplus from other nations.

## Russia’s Economic Resilience: Overcoming the Energy Curse

Hudson then turns to Russia, which, alongside China, has transitioned from a Cold War adversary to a competitive capitalist economy. In the 1990s, under Boris Yeltsin, Russia embraced neoliberalism, leading to an “energy curse.” The US and Europe, through advisors like Jeffrey Sachs, encouraged privatization of energy giants like Yukos and Norilsk Nickel, aiming to financialize rents and prevent industrial reinvestment. This policy, Hudson notes, turned Russia into a “gas station with atom bombs,” as profits were siphoned to Western stockholders, with the Russian stock market soaring in 1995-96.

When Vladimir Putin assumed power, he confronted this rent-seeking kleptocracy, adopting an ad hoc solution: allowing oligarchs to retain ownership of banks, oil wells, and utilities stolen under Yeltsin, but requiring them to reinvest profits into Russian industrialization. This shift enabled Russia to diversify, using energy revenues to bolster agriculture and technology. Despite Western sanctions, Russia’s economy has grown, becoming the world’s fourth-largest by purchasing power parity, according to the World Bank. Hudson attributes this resilience to Putin’s pragmatic constraints on kleptocrats, though he notes Russia lacks a robust economic theory to guide development, as Stalinism erased classical economic thought like Marxism’s focus on rent and value theory.

## US Neoliberalism: A Destructive Force

Hudson sharply critiques US neoliberalism, which he argues is not about competition but disruption. The US, unable to rival China industrially, seeks to “hurt” it through measures like supporting Wahhabi terrorism in Xinjiang, encouraging Taiwan’s conflict with the mainland, and renaming the South China Sea as the “Philippine Sea” to sow division. Domestically, neoliberal policies under leaders like Trump prioritize the 1%, shifting tax burdens onto labor, privatizing public utilities, and financializing politics via rulings like Citizens United. Hudson cites Britain’s Thames Water as a “poster child” for privatization’s failures—costs soared, and the company teeters on bankruptcy after owners extracted dividends through borrowing.

To compete with China, the US would need to return to industrial capitalism, de-privatizing utilities, implementing progressive taxation, and adopting modern monetary theory to self-finance development, as China does. However, Hudson sees no such intent, as Trump’s policies favor rentiers, financial interests, and monopolies, aiming to lower living standards and concentrate wealth, the opposite of China’s focus on rising productivity and living standards.

## Russia’s Struggle with Economic Theory

Russia’s economic model, Hudson explains, is constrained by its lack of theoretical grounding. Stalinism’s “vulgarization” of Marxism erased classical economic concepts like rent theory, leaving Russia without a framework to develop post-communism. Hudson’s efforts in the 1990s to advocate for a land tax-based system—echoing 19th-century classical economists like Adam Smith and John Stuart Mill—met resistance. In 1991, US appraiser Ted Gwartney proposed taxing land rent in St. Petersburg, but American pressure, via the National Endowment for Democracy, thwarted these ideas by manipulating the Duma elections to exclude Hudson’s allies like Vyacheslav Vyatlov.

Russia’s central bank, still influenced by neoliberalism, raises interest rates to combat inflation, which Hudson attributes to US sanctions forcing higher capital investment in self-sufficiency, not labor costs. Unlike China, where Hudson’s works on industrial capitalism are widely translated and taught, Russia struggles to conceptualize an alternative to neoliberalism, leaving its rent-yielding monopolies in kleptocratic hands, albeit politically constrained by Putin.

## A Tale of Two Capitalisms

Michael Hudson’s analysis reveals a stark contrast between the industrial capitalism of China and Russia and the neoliberalism of the US. China’s rise, built on public banking and self-financed development, mirrors the US’s 19th-century success, while Russia, despite neoliberal setbacks, is rebuilding through pragmatic reinvestment. The US, trapped in a rent-seeking, financialized system, resorts to disruption rather than competition, targeting rivals with sanctions and chaos. As the world economy reorganizes, Hudson underscores the need for a return to classical economic principles—freeing markets from rent and monopoly—to foster sustainable growth, a path China and Russia are navigating, while the US remains mired in neoliberal decline.
