## Europe Nears War: NATO's Arctic Drills and Proxy War Tactics Risk Catastrophe

In a sobering interview titled "Europe on the Brink of War with Russia," Professor Glenn Diesen, a geopolitical analyst, engages Colonel Douglas Macgregor, a decorated combat veteran and former senior advisor to the US Secretary of Defense, to dissect NATO's escalating military activities and their perilous implications. Focusing on recent anti-submarine warfare drills in the Barents Sea and the failing proxy war in Ukraine, their discussion reveals a pattern of provocation, denial, and dangerous miscalculations that threaten to drag Europe into direct conflict with Russia.

### NATO's Arctic Provocations

The conversation begins with Diesen noting US, German, and Norwegian anti-submarine drills in the Barents Sea, near Russia’s strategic Kola Peninsula and Northern Fleet bases. Macgregor calls this more than a minor provocation, framing it as part of a broader pattern of aggression. He highlights a June statement by Lieutenant General Donahue, commander of US Army ground forces in Europe, who boasted that NATO could seize Kaliningrad—Russian sovereign territory—in hours. Such public threats, Macgregor argues, are unprecedented and reckless, signaling a belief in Washington that bullying tactics can intimidate Moscow.

These drills, involving Tomahawk missile-equipped vessels and B-2A bomber flights near Russia’s air defense zones, coincide with Russia’s planned Zapad 2025 exercise. Macgregor dismisses NATO’s claim that these are defensive responses, seeing them instead as deliberate attempts to pressure Russia into conceding in Ukraine. He warns that Norway, a key participant, is “in the line of fire” if tensions escalate, yet public awareness there seems alarmingly low.

### The Proxy War’s Collapse and Escalation

Diesen contextualizes these Arctic maneuvers within the failing proxy war in Ukraine, where front lines are crumbling. NATO’s initial goal of turning the Black Sea into a “NATO lake” failed when Russia preempted plans to occupy Crimea in 2014. Now, with Sweden and Finland in NATO, the Baltic Sea is similarly claimed as a NATO-controlled zone, with proposals to blockade Russian oil tankers or St. Petersburg. The Arctic, too, is militarizing, with US bases expanding in Scandinavia and fears stoked about Russian threats to Finland’s Lapland, near the Northern Fleet.

Macgregor sees these moves as desperate escalations to compensate for Ukraine’s inevitable defeat. “There’s an unwillingness to admit this truth,” he says, noting that NATO’s narrative—Ukraine is winning, Russia is weak—has been exposed as propaganda. Russia’s military-industrial capacity far surpasses NATO’s expectations, and its strategy of attrition has hollowed out Ukrainian forces. Yet, instead of seeking diplomacy, NATO doubles down with provocative acts, risking a Russian response that could target Europe directly.

### The Illusion of Air Power Supremacy

A key driver of this escalation, Macgregor argues, is Washington’s “infatuation with air power” as a low-risk tool for intimidation. From the B-2A flyover during Trump’s meeting with Putin to ongoing missile drills, NATO projects strength without ground engagement, assuming impunity. “Air power is the equivalent of sex without consequences,” Macgregor quips, but warns that Russia’s advanced air defenses and long-range missiles could strike Norway to Portugal, exposing NATO’s vulnerability. NATO’s own estimates suggest it can defend only 5% of its territory, rendering such provocations suicidal.

This reliance on air power reflects a broader disconnect: Western leaders, insulated from war’s realities, underestimate Russia’s capabilities and resolve. Macgregor cites Trump’s team—Secretary of Defense Hegseth, Secretary of State Rubio, and CIA Director Ratcliffe—as enamored with bullying tactics, believing threats will force compliance. This mindset extends beyond Ukraine, with similar pressures applied to Venezuela, Iran, and even India, alienating potential partners like BRICS nations, which control over 40% of global resources.

### Hypocrisy and Moral Supremacy

Diesen highlights the hypocrisy in NATO’s rhetoric, noting that leaders like NATO’s Mark Rutte label Russia’s actions criminal while ignoring Western war crimes in Iraq, Libya, and Syria. Macgregor agrees, calling out the “immoral hypocrisy” of claiming moral superiority while pursuing aggressive policies. He recalls Germany’s shift from racial to moral supremacy, a trend echoed across Europe, where questioning escalation is dismissed as “Kremlin talking points.” This stifles dissent, creating a “suicidal pact” that blinds leaders to the risks of confronting a nuclear power.

The refusal to engage in diplomacy, Diesen notes, stems from a belief that only Russia’s military defeat can end the war. Macgregor counters that Russia has no interest in conquering Europe, only in securing eastern Ukraine to prevent future conflicts. He contrasts NATO’s push for a ceasefire—a pause to rearm Ukraine—with Russia’s demand for a negotiated settlement, as seen in World War I’s armistice. NATO’s coalition of the willing, led by European nations, avoids admitting failure, as it would expose three years of false narratives.

### Economic and Social Fallout

The interview also addresses Europe’s domestic crises, which exacerbate the folly of escalation. Macgregor points to Britain’s near-financial collapse, Germany’s economic woes, and France’s political instability, driven by globalist policies and open borders. These weaken NATO’s ability to sustain a prolonged conflict. “When prosperity fails,” he warns, “everything will change very rapidly and very violently.” Europe’s revolutionary moment looms, as publics grow disillusioned with leaders who prioritize war over welfare.

Diesen reflects on the generational disconnect: younger leaders, unscarred by war, treat it as a distant game, unlike past generations who respected its costs. Macgregor recounts a historical parallel: Britain’s 1914 declaration of war on Germany, based on miscalculations like Winston Churchill’s belief in a quick naval victory. The reality, as General Kitchener warned, was years of grueling conflict—a lesson unheeded today.

### A Path to De-escalation?

Macgregor urges Europe to reject globalist regimes and embrace pragmatic leadership before economic collapse fuels unrest. He sees no strategic need for war, as no great power threatens the West absent provocation. Russia’s patience, he warns, is nearly exhausted, with figures like Medvedev signaling severe retaliation for further strikes, such as those on Murmansk. The US, despite Trump’s campaign promises to exit the Ukraine quagmire, appears committed to escalation, driven by a misguided belief in military dominance.

This interview paints a grim picture: NATO’s provocative drills, refusal to negotiate, and reliance on outdated tactics risk a catastrophic war. Europe, weakened and divided, stands on the brink, misled by narratives of invincibility and moral superiority. Macgregor and Diesen call for urgent reflection before incremental escalations lead to irreversible consequences.
