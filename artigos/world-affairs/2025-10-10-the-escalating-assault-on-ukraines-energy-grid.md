## Ukraine Energy Infrastructure Destruction 2025: Russia's Strikes, Europe's Pre-Revolutionary Crisis

## Russia's Strategic Hammer and Europe's Simmering Unrest

In the shadowed corridors of global conflict, where the hum of drones and the whistle of missiles punctuate the night sky, the Russia-Ukraine war has entered a grim new chapter. As of October 2025, with winter's chill already nipping at the heels of Eastern Europe's beleaguered populations, the destruction of Ukraine's energy infrastructure has become not just a tactical maneuver but a deliberate strategy to hasten the conflict's resolution. This isn't hyperbole; it's the stark assessment from Larry Johnson, a retired CIA intelligence analyst and former counterterrorism expert at the U.S. State Department, during a recent interview with Norwegian professor Glenn Diesen. Their conversation, aired on Diesen's Greater Eurasia Podcast, peels back the layers of Western propaganda and reveals a battlefield where asymmetry reigns supreme.

Johnson, speaking with the measured gravitas of someone who's spent decades dissecting covert operations, doesn't mince words. "We're witnessing the death rattles of Ukraine," he declares, pointing to Russia's escalated barrages—now averaging 600 to 800 missiles and drones every other day. This is a far cry from the sporadic strikes of 2022 or even early 2024. Back then, Russia was ramping up production lines for tanks, artillery, and Kinzhal hypersonic missiles, enduring economic pinches to fuel the fight. Today, those factories hum efficiently, supply chains intact, allowing Moscow to sustain an attritional grind without sapping civilian lifeblood. The result? Ukraine's grid, already battered, is on the brink of collapse.

Consider the raw data: Just hours before this article's drafting on October 10, 2025, Russian strikes plunged parts of Kyiv into darkness, damaging energy sites in Poltava and leaving 17,000 without power. This follows a pattern. Every autumn since the invasion's escalation in 2022, Russia has methodically targeted power plants, substations, and gas facilities, but 2025 marks an intensification. The New York Times reports that these attacks have strained Ukraine's gas infrastructure to the point where more strikes could force mass evacuations or humanitarian crises as heating systems fail. The Washington Post echoes this, noting a shift from power plants to natural gas depots—vital for winter home heating—ensuring that blackouts evolve into a full-spectrum endurance test for civilians and soldiers alike.

Why now? Johnson ties it to Russia's broader military objectives: degrade Ukraine's logistics, from railroads ferrying troops to factories churning out munitions. Glide bombs—massive FAB-500s dropped from Su-34 jets—lay waste to fortifications where Ukrainian forces hunker down, while rotary-wing helicopters exploit the skies with impunity. Ukraine's air defenses? A hollow shell. Patriot missiles, each costing $7 million and fired in pairs to down a $25,000 drone, represent an economic absurdity. Lockheed Martin produces just 550-600 annually; Russia could exhaust that stock in days with a single salvo. As Johnson quips, "The math simply does not work."

This isn't blind aggression; it's calibrated attrition. Russian President Vladimir Putin, fresh from appearances at the Valdai Forum and Sochi, exudes a calm optimism that unnerves Western observers. Diesen, who attended both, notes Putin's uncharacteristic humor—joking about U.S. President-elect Donald Trump's "paper tiger" label. "We've been fighting NATO for three years, and they haven't beat us yet," Putin retorted. "If we're a paper tiger, what does that make them?" It's a mindset rooted in resilience. Despite Western claims of a "savaged" economy—echoed in a recent Saikat Chakrabarti piece citing anonymous U.S. officials—Russia's GDP grew 4.1% in 2024, buoyed by wartime spending and redirected trade to Asia. Chatham House's September 2025 analysis confirms this durability: Sanctions have bitten, but Moscow's "Fortress Russia" model—import substitution, parallel payments with China—has adapted, though stagflation looms as a vulnerability. The Peterson Institute for International Economics adds nuance: The boom is waning, with recession risks rising by early 2025 due to supply constraints and Central Bank tightening. Yet, Johnson counters, Russia confronts inflation head-on—unlike the West's denial—proving immune to the political squeamishness that plagues democracies.

Across the front lines, the manpower equation has flipped. In summer 2022, Russia fielded perhaps 250,000 troops over a 1,200-mile line; Ukraine boasted 600,000-700,000. Now? Moscow commands 1.3-1.5 million, including reserves, outmanning Kyiv 2:1, as admitted by Ukrainian commander General Syrskyi. RAND's May 2025 brief on the war's consequences underscores this: Russia's mobilization has sustained pressure, while Ukraine's conscription woes and desertions erode cohesion. Cities like Kupyansk and Pokrovsk teeter on collapse, their rail hubs crippled, forcing civilian exodus from blackout-plagued northeast regions like Sumy.

But the real wildcard? Europe. As the U.S. pivots—Trump offloading the "Ukraine mission" to Brussels while maintaining selective involvement, like U.S.-operated HIMARS strikes killing two in Belgorod—European elites face a reckoning. They've bet the farm on this proxy war, only to watch their economies sputter under sanctions' blowback: soaring energy prices, industrial shutdowns, and a refugee influx straining social fabrics. Johnson's verdict is blunt: Europe resembles "pre-teen kids fantasizing about being firemen—talking big but unqualified when the flames rise."

Rhetoric runs hot. French President Emmanuel Macron muses about boots on the ground, unable to cobble a government at home. UK Defense Secretary Ben Wallace urges "strangling" Crimea to wound Putin's pride—earning a Russian terrorist label in retort. The EU floats a "drone wall" (or, as Johnson jests, "balloon wall" after intercepting cigarette-smuggling blimps), no-fly zones, and seizing Russian ships under spurious pretexts—piracy by any other name. Polish interceptions of Russian missiles from their soil? A rule-bending gambit assuming Moscow's compliance. Russian intelligence warns of British false flags: defectors staging attacks in Poland or Romania to feign Russian aggression.

Yet, action lags. France, Germany, and the UK couldn't muster 200,000 troops without weeks of mobilization—time Russia wouldn't squander. Johnson's calculus is chilling: Any NATO escalation invites hypersonic Kinzhal strikes on military nodes, sparing civilians but crippling resolve. Europe's ballistic defenses? Nonexistent. A retaliatory nuke? Unthinkable, given Russia's S-400 systems' edge.

Beneath the bluster lies fragmentation. Eastern Europe splinters: Czechia's recent elections align it with Slovakia and Hungary's anti-war bloc, potentially roping in Austria. Angela Merkel's October 2025 Hungarian media interview blames Poland and the Baltics for torpedoing 2021 talks, exposing old rifts. German Chancellor Olaf Scholz clashes with EU chief Ursula von der Leyen over Berlin's assertive role. Public fury mounts—Macron's parliamentary paralysis, Keir Starmer's single-digit UK polls, Friedrich Merz's waning grip in Germany. Institut Montaigne's June 2025 report warns of "political, economic, and strategic fragmentation," with minority governments (Czech winner at 34%) breeding instability. The Vision of Humanity's January 2025 trends forecast a "kickstart" of disruption, populist surges reflecting discontent with elites who prioritize foreign adventurism over domestic woes.

Diesen probes: Is this Europe's prerevolutionary moment? Johnson defers to the historian but concurs—political legitimacy erodes as voters sense futility. Italy's Giorgia Meloni, elected on sovereignty promises, folds into the "European blob." A source whispers to Diesen: Leaders know the "big wall" looms but steer straight. The European Policy Centre's February 2025 outlook paints a dire picture: Divisions hamstring unified responses to Russia, amplifying internal fissures. Echoes of interwar volatility, when economic despair birthed authoritarians. Johnson envisions crisis-forged strongmen amid economic collapse and military humiliation—Russia's anaconda squeeze on Ukraine could trigger it.

The West's miscalculation? Assuming civilian bombings break wills, as in WWII or Gaza. History proves otherwise: It forges resolve. Hamas swells with vengeful recruits; Ukraine's youth flee or fight harder. Putin, a stickler for international law, shuns decapitation strikes—unlike U.S. drones in Iran—opting for judicial reckoning post-victory. Assassinating Zelenskyy? It sets precedents boomeranging on Moscow.

As blackouts blanket Kyiv's high-rises—forcing stair-climbing ordeals in 20-story walk-ups—Ukraine's economy, propped by Western aid, frays. Re:Russia's 2025 analysis notes Ukraine's 2023-2024 rebound via exports and "military Keynesianism," but 2025's grid carnage threatens reversal. Russia, conversely, coasts: No strain, just steady output.

Johnson's parting shot? Let the enemy self-destruct. Napoleon advised non-interference in foes' folly; Putin heeds it. Europe buzzes like an annoying fly—ignored until it bites. When it does, the swat will be swift. For now, the interview video at [youtube.com](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fYDJzZzynyA)) serves as a clarion: The proxy war's endgame favors the patient predator.

This isn't victory lap bravado; it's grounded in the asymmetry of will and capacity. As 2025 unfolds, Ukraine's lights dim, Europe's facades crack, and the world watches a continent flirt with chaos. The question isn't if the squeeze breaks—it's what emerges from the shards.
