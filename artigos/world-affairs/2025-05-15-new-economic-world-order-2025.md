## New Economic World Order 2025: Yanis Varoufakis on U.S. Hegemony and Europe’s Decline

On May 12, 2025, Professor Glenn Diesen hosted a compelling interview with Yanis Varoufakis on his YouTube channel, titled *"Yanis Varoufakis: New Economic World Order 2025 & Europe's Suicide."* Varoufakis, a renowned economist, former Greek Finance Minister, and prolific author, offered a searing analysis of the shifting global economic landscape, the U.S.’s persistent drive for hegemony, and Europe’s self-inflicted decline through a lack of strategic vision and investment. The discussion, set against the backdrop of recent U.S. trade disruptions under President Donald Trump, painted a grim picture of Europe’s inability to adapt to a new economic world order, risking irrelevance as China and other global players surge ahead.

## The U.S.’s Historical Hegemony and Trump’s Economic Shock

Varoufakis began by tracing the U.S.’s economic dominance to the post-World War II era, when it inherited the British Empire’s global role after Britain’s bankruptcy. The Truman Doctrine and Bretton Woods system, he explained, were designed to maintain U.S. hegemony by ensuring net exports, with American surpluses recycled to Europe and Japan via the Marshall Plan and loans to sustain demand for U.S. goods.

This system, however, collapsed in 1971 when President Richard Nixon ended [Bretton Woods](/bretton-woods-global-economic-reset/), a move Varoufakis likened to Trump’s current trade war. Nixon’s “shock” shifted the global recycling mechanism: U.S. deficits, rather than surpluses, fueled demand for German, Japanese, and later Chinese exports, with dollars returning to Wall Street to finance U.S. markets and real estate.

This inverted system, Varoufakis argued, led to the 2008 financial crisis, as deregulated Wall Street banks built toxic derivatives, followed by “socialism for bankers” through quantitative easing and austerity for the majority. The result was a collapse in investment, with only Big Tech giants like Amazon and Google capitalizing on the influx of central bank money. Varoufakis introduced his concept of “cloud capital,” a new form of power that extracts rents rather than produces goods, exacerbating de-industrialization in the U.S. Midwest.

Trump’s recognition of this imbalance—promising to “make America great again” by rebalancing the dollar’s role and manufacturing capacity—echoes Nixon’s strategy, but Varoufakis cautioned that success depends on future administrations continuing the policy, a continuity Nixon achieved through Carter, Reagan, and Bush.

## Europe’s Paralysis and Economic Decline

Turning to Europe, Varoufakis was scathingly critical, describing its response to Trump’s trade war as “panic without planning.” He traced Europe’s economic woes to Nixon’s 1971 expulsion of Europe from the dollar zone, which led to the euro’s creation as a cartel-like mechanism to stabilize exchange rates for industries like coal and steel. However, the eurozone’s failure to establish a common treasury or democratic institutions resulted in the euro crisis, marked by harsh austerity and money printing. This, Varoufakis argued, toxified European politics, with leaders like Angela Merkel—despite her flaws—replaced by “headless chickens” like Friedrich Merz and Emmanuel Macron, who lack political capital or legitimacy.

Europe’s investment shortfall, estimated at €600-700 billion annually, has left it lagging in critical technologies like solar panels and electric vehicles, where China has surged ahead by two industrial revolutions. Varoufakis debunked claims of Chinese “dumping,” asserting that China’s technological edge stems from consistent investment, while Europe has prioritized regulation over innovation. He ridiculed Brussels’ threats of digital taxes on U.S. Big Tech, noting Europe’s lack of equivalents to China’s Tencent or Alibaba, leaving it vulnerable to U.S. retaliation—such as cutting off access to YouTube or X—which could collapse the European Commission within a week.

## Europe’s Missed Opportunities and Strategic Recommendations

Varoufakis proposed three urgent measures for Europe to regain footing, though he doubted their implementation due to ideological and expedient barriers. First, Europe should ignore Trump, neither retaliating nor begging for trade deals, and instead open communication with Beijing to coordinate economic strategies.

This includes scrapping EU tariffs on Chinese goods like solar panels, imposed at U.S. behest, to lower costs for Europeans. Second, he advocated for a €600-700 billion annual investment program via the European Investment Bank (EIB), backed by the European Central Bank (ECB) to ensure low interest rates, targeting green and digital technologies without involving governments directly. Third, he suggested creating a digital wallet at the ECB for every European citizen, offering free payments and competitive interest rates to challenge commercial banks, mirroring China’s digital currency model.

These proposals, Varoufakis lamented, are stymied by entrenched interests. The German establishment, despite using the euro, retains an exit strategy by resisting common debt, fearing it would lock them into the eurozone like California in the U.S. dollar union. European bankers, meanwhile, fiercely oppose a digital payment system that would erode their monopoly, as Varoufakis experienced in 2015 when his proposal led to fierce backlash and “discursive decapitation.”

## China’s Strategic Maturity and the Global Economic War

In the U.S.-China economic war, Varoufakis praised China as “the adults in the room,” responding rationally with a clear macroeconomic and investment plan. China’s strategic autonomy—through diversified partnerships and technological sovereignty—contrasts with Europe’s dependence on the U.S., which Trump exploits by rejecting Europe as a subordinate. Varoufakis highlighted China’s refusal to escalate retaliation, focusing instead on building resilience, a strategy Europe lacks.

## Europe’s Path to Irrelevance

Varoufakis’s interview with Diesen underscored a stark reality: the U.S.’s tariff-driven reorganization of the global economy, while risky, builds on a historical playbook of maintaining hegemony, whereas Europe’s lack of cohesion and investment condemns it to irrelevance. As China advances technologically and diplomatically, Europe’s failure to pursue strategic autonomy—choosing instead to subordinate itself to a dismissive U.S.—marks a self-inflicted “suicide.” Without bold action, Europe risks becoming a footnote in the new economic world order, a cautionary tale of missed opportunities in a rapidly shifting global landscape.
