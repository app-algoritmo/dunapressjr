## Europe's Economic Collapse and Civil War Fears in 2025: Alex Krainer's Insights on Glenn Diesen Interview

In a thought-provoking interview on September 10, 2025, market analyst and author Alex Krainer sat down with Professor Glenn Diesen to dissect the unraveling "coalition of the willing"—a group of European nations steadfastly supporting the proxy war against Russia in Ukraine. Krainer, a former hedge fund manager with over two decades of experience in financial markets, painted a grim picture of Europe's political and economic landscape, warning of impending collapse, authoritarian drifts, and even the specter of civil war. As France's government teeters, the UK's budget woes deepen, and Germany's economy stagnates, Krainer's analysis highlights how these crises are intertwined with geopolitical misadventures and elite interests.

The conversation opens with the fragility of this coalition, echoing the ill-fated group that invaded Iraq in 2003. Krainer argues that European leaders are depleting resources—weapons, soldiers, money, and political capital—in a futile bid to sustain the conflict. In France, the government's collapse on September 8, 2025, exemplifies this strain. Prime Minister François Bayrou lost a no-confidence vote in the National Assembly over a contentious budget aimed at curbing the nation's €3.345 trillion debt, equivalent to 114% of GDP. This marks the third government downfall in 14 months under President Emmanuel Macron, plunging the country into turmoil. Krainer sees this as a symptom of long-brewing discontent, exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic's psychological operations that delayed inevitable reckonings.

Macron's approval ratings hover in the single digits, reflecting widespread disillusionment. Krainer questions the legitimacy of such leaders, noting authoritarian tactics to suppress opposition. In Germany, the rise of the Alternative for Germany (AfD) party, which opposes the war and seeks better ties with Russia, has been met with suspicious events: seven candidates "died suddenly" within two weeks, dismissed as coincidence despite the party's labeling as "extremist." Similar assaults on democracy appear in Slovakia (assassination attempt on Robert Fico) and Romania (disqualification of Calin Georgescu). Krainer posits that these unpopular regimes, aligned with globalist banking interests, are clinging to power by eviscerating economies and stoking war fears.

Economic ruin forms the crux of Krainer's warnings. Europe's leaders, he says, are sacrificing prosperity to fuel the proxy war, creating conditions ripe for conflict. In the UK, Prime Minister Keir Starmer has decried a £22 billion "black hole" in public finances, downplaying earlier estimates of £51-76 billion while imposing austerity on citizens and businesses. The October 30, 2024, budget featured lavish spending on net-zero schemes amid rising unemployment and debt-to-GDP over 100%. Krainer accuses the Bank of England of concealing the crisis through opaque repo market interventions, a tactic that historically leads to hyperinflation, as seen in Zimbabwe (2009), Argentina, and Venezuela (2015-2016). He predicts spiking interest rates, currency collapse, and stock market bubbles that fail to preserve wealth.

Germany faces an even direr predicament with massive deindustrialization. The economy shrank 0.3% in Q2 2025, with forecasts of stagnation or zero growth for the year. High energy costs, credit woes, and skilled labor shortages have crippled manufacturing, the nation's backbone. Chancellor Friedrich Merz's rhetoric blames Russian sabotage, ignoring the Nord Stream pipeline's destruction. Krainer notes that government bonds in the UK and Europe have plummeted since 2021, with UK 10-year gilts down 30%, signaling market skepticism. This "weak link" status for Britain stems from post-Afghanistan hysteria and failed imperial ventures.

Amid these woes, Krainer fears civil unrest escalating to war. Citing researcher David Betts, he describes Europe as "explosively configured" for civil wars, with high youth unemployment and stolen futures breeding desperation. Leaders like Macron, Starmer, and Merz—tied to banking elites (Macron a former Rothschild banker, Merz ex-BlackRock head)—escalate anti-Russia rhetoric to divert blame. A leaked French Health Ministry document urges hospitals to prepare for major war by March 2026, amid collapsing Ukrainian front lines. Krainer warns of false flags to galvanize support, referencing historical precedents.

Yet, optimism flickers in potential democratic triumphs leading to EU and NATO disintegration, with nations joining BRICS or SCO for Eastern partnerships. The recent SCO summit in China marked a milestone, with plans for an SCO Development Bank and centers combating threats, showcasing Beijing's vision for a multipolar order. Central banks worldwide are hoarding gold over U.S. Treasuries, signaling eroding dollar dominance.

Across the Atlantic, Krainer echoes Putin adviser Anton Kobyakov's claims: the U.S., burdened by $37 trillion federal debt (plus state and private totaling $110-120 trillion), may shift liabilities into stablecoins for devaluation. President Trump's GENIUS Act, signed in 2025, regulates stablecoins, potentially creating a dual circulation system inspired by China. This could reset debts via inflation or outright repudiation, though Krainer doubts seamless execution amid market volatility.

Krainer's interview underscores a clash between people and banks, as Lord Acton prophesied. Europe's path—revolution, war, or renewal—hinges on resisting elite narratives. With internet amplifying dissent, traditional control falters. False stories, like alleged Russian jamming of Ursula von der Leyen's plane (debunked), fail to rally support. As front lines crumble and rhetoric heats, desperation may breed recklessness, but public apathy towards dying for proxies offers hope.

This crisis, rooted in post-2008 dependencies, has spurred alternatives like China's Belt and Road and AIIB. Trump's tariffs on India for Russian oil purchases push diversification towards BRICS. Krainer predicts Europe deteriorating "from bad to ugly," with leaders fermenting fear to cling to power. Yet, civil war risks—evident in France's impeachment proceedings and UK's austerity protests—could spark guillotine-era reckonings.

In conclusion, Krainer's insights reveal a continent at the brink, where economic collapse fuels political chaos and war fears. As global orders shift, Europe's fate depends on reclaiming sovereignty from banking cartels and proxy obsessions.
