## US Foreign Policy Chaos: Washington’s Rivalries, Coups, Wars, and Cover-Ups - Col. Lawrence Wilkerson on the Decline of US Strategy

In a revealing interview with Prof. Glenn Diesen, Colonel Lawrence Wilkerson, former chief of staff to Colin Powell during the Bush administration, offers a scathing critique of US foreign policy, tracing its decline from the Clinton era to the present. Wilkerson, a seasoned political commentator with insights from teaching national security decision-making, exposes the pervasive influence of neoconservatives, the chaos within Trump’s administration, and the CIA’s unchecked operations in conflicts from Ukraine to Syria. He warns that the US empire, lacking strategic vision, is unraveling amidst internal divisions, covert operations, and a legacy of regime change.

## The Neocon Legacy: A Persistent Influence

Wilkerson begins by identifying the rise of neoconservatives as a defining shift in US policy, starting with Bill Clinton’s administration. Historically present—evidenced by Marine General Smedley Butler’s 1930s testimony about fascist solicitations—neocons gained significant power under Clinton, embedding themselves across government. Their influence, Wilkerson notes, has persisted through subsequent administrations, infecting Trump’s team despite his initial America First rhetoric opposing militarized hegemony. Trump’s campaign criticized the Iraq invasion as a mistake, but his administration’s actions—supporting genocide in Gaza, escalating tensions with Iran, and continuing the Ukraine war—reflect neocon priorities.

Trump’s cabinet, Wilkerson argues, is a “circus” of unqualified appointees, including Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and National Security Advisor Michael Waltz, whom he calls a “Victoria Nuland clone.” Waltz and Secretary of State Marco Rubio, both neocons, clash with more pragmatic figures like special envoy Steve Witkoff, whom Wilkerson sees as the administration’s only “sane and sober” member. This internal division, coupled with Trump’s lack of strategic focus, fuels chaos, as he fails to address critical axes in Ukraine—between Kyiv and the EU, Washington and Kyiv, Kyiv and Moscow, and within Ukraine itself—leaving negotiations with Putin incomplete and ineffective.

## The Deep State and CIA Autonomy

Wilkerson attributes much of the US’s foreign policy dysfunction to the CIA’s unchecked autonomy, a problem exacerbated since Clinton’s era. The agency, he reveals, often operates independently, conducting operations—like arming jihadist groups in Syria—without presidential oversight. In Syria, CIA officers and Marines clashed due to poor coordination, a result of the agency’s secrecy even from its own government. Wilkerson recounts instances where CIA directors withhold information from ambassadors to provide “plausible deniability,” a practice he deems reckless, as it involves killing without formal presidential sanction.

Trump’s appointment of Tulsi Gabbard as Director of National Intelligence (DNI) aimed to curb this autonomy, but Wilkerson doubts her effectiveness without budget and personnel control—powers Trump promised but has not delivered. CIA Director John Ratcliffe, aligned with neocons, continues operations in Ukraine unknown to Trump, perpetuating a legacy of covert actions that Wilkerson traces back to the 2004 Orange Revolution and earlier interventions.

## Color Revolutions and NATO Expansion

Wilkerson provides a detailed account of US-orchestrated “color revolutions,” starting in 2002 under the Bush administration, to expand NATO and secure compliant governments. In Ukraine, the 2004 Orange Revolution and 2014 Maidan coup weaponized genuine discontent over corruption, redirecting it toward NATO membership despite only 20% public support. The CIA, alongside the National Endowment for Democracy (NED) and other NGOs, ran sophisticated campaigns—buying media, funding political minorities, and deploying diplomatic pressure—to install pro-NATO leaders. Similar efforts succeeded in Montenegro and Albania, despite their “criminal” governance, but failed in Hungary and Slovakia, where anti-Russian sentiment was weaker.

In Scandinavia, the US targeted Sweden and Finland, historically neutral, to abandon their neutrality. Jens Stoltenberg, a key US ally, was groomed as NATO Secretary General from 2003 onward, leveraging post-9/11 political support and his role in the 2011 Libya intervention—where Norwegian F-16 pilots dropped 10% of the bombs—to secure his position. Stoltenberg’s interventionism and commitment to NATO expansion aligned with US goals, ensuring European vassalage despite public resistance.

## Syria and the Middle East: Chaos and Opportunism

Wilkerson details the US’s role in Syria, where the CIA helped create ISIS by arming jihadist groups to destabilize Bashar al-Assad. After taking out al-Qaeda leader Abu Musab al-Zarqawi, the US facilitated a coalition of Iraqi Republican Guard members and terrorists, aiming to establish a caliphate spanning northern Iraq and Syria. The plan, intended to pressure Assad into capitulation, backfired as ISIS gained ground, forcing Obama to redeploy troops to Iraq in 2014 after the Iraqi army, poorly trained despite General David Petraeus’s glowing reports, failed to contain the insurgency.

The 2025 Syrian crisis, Wilkerson notes, saw the US back jihadist leader Abu Mohammad al-Golani to weaken Assad, only for him to seize power. Initially a bounty target, Golani was later hailed as a democratic leader, a “cartoonish” shift reflecting US strategic disarray. Israel, under Netanyahu, exploited the chaos, expanding into water-rich Syrian territories beyond the Golan Heights, while a US-brokered ceasefire in Lebanon diverted Israeli forces to Syria. Wilkerson suggests Israel may never leave, potentially holding significant Syrian territory if it remains a cohesive state.

## Obama’s Reluctance and the Bias Toward War

Wilkerson recounts a 2015 meeting with Obama, where the president admitted a “bias in this town toward war,” reflecting on how Samantha Power and Hillary Clinton persuaded him to intervene in Libya. Obama’s regret was palpable, as was his frustration with John Kerry’s push for ground forces in Syria, evident during the meeting. Clinton’s infamous “We came, we saw, he died” remark, accompanied by a chuckle, underscored her political ambitions over diplomacy, a trait Wilkerson observed during her Senate tenure as she positioned herself for a presidential run.

Obama’s initial intent to withdraw from Iraq was thwarted by appointees from the American Security Project, who knew the US was building ISIS to destabilize the region. Wilkerson’s revelation that the CIA and military clashed in Syria due to lack of coordination highlights the broader strategic failure, as the empire’s fragmented decision-making undermines coherent policy.

## Conclusion: An Empire in Decline

Colonel Lawrence Wilkerson’s analysis paints a grim picture of a US empire in decline, driven by neoconservative influence, CIA autonomy, and a lack of strategic vision since Clinton’s era. Trump’s administration, a “colossal mess” of unqualified appointees and conflicting agendas, exemplifies this chaos, perpetuating wars and covert operations in Ukraine, Syria, and beyond. The US’s history of color revolutions, from Ukraine to Scandinavia, reveals a pattern of manipulating nations into compliance, often against their publics’ wishes. As the empire unravels, Wilkerson warns of the dangers of unchecked power, urging a return to strategic clarity to avert further global instability.
