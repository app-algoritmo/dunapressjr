## The **US-China Economic War: A Historical Rebalancing and the Clash of Statecraft

The escalating economic war between the United States and China, marked by tech rivalry, currency competition, and efforts to repatriate supply chains, is more than a trade dispute—it’s a pivotal moment in global history. In this interview with Prof. Glenn Diesen, renowned economist Jeffrey Sachs provides a long-view perspective, framing China’s rise as a natural rebalancing after centuries of Western dominance. Sachs, who has advised governments worldwide, also explores how China’s historical statecraft, rooted in unity and harmony, differs fundamentally from the conflict-driven European model, challenging Western assumptions about geopolitics.

## A Historical Rebalancing: The Return of Asia

Sachs begins by contextualizing the US-China economic war within a 2,000-year historical arc. For much of history, Asia, home to 60% of the world’s population, accounted for roughly 60% of global economic output. From 500 to 1500 AD, China was a technological leader, pioneering innovations like gunpowder, the steam engine, paper currency, the compass, and large-scale ocean navigation. However, the Industrial Revolution and European imperialism shifted the balance. By 1950, after 150 years of Western dominance, Asia’s share of world output had plummeted to 20%, with China’s share dropping to just 2% despite comprising 20% of the global population.

China’s decline, Sachs notes, was marked by a “century of humiliation” from 1839 to 1949, encompassing the Opium Wars, the Taiping Rebellion, European extraterritorial dominance, Japanese invasions, and civil war. The establishment of the People’s Republic of China in 1949 marked the beginning of its recovery. Today, China’s rise—alongside the rapid development of India, Southeast Asia (ASEAN countries), and parts of West Asia—signals a return to Asia’s historical role as a global economic powerhouse. Sachs estimates that Asia’s share of the world economy is rebounding toward its historical norm, diminishing the North Atlantic’s dominance that defined the 19th and 20th centuries.

## Western Ideologies and the Fear of China’s Rise

The West’s reaction to China’s resurgence, Sachs argues, is shaped by centuries of Eurocentric ideologies that justified European and later American dominance. From the Renaissance through the Enlightenment, Romantic Nationalism, and into the 20th century, Western thought—epitomized by figures like Immanuel Kant—embedded notions of European superiority, often tinged with racism. Kant, despite his universalist philosophy, expressed race-based views of European dominance, a mindset that persisted through ideologies like Nazism and the American belief in “exceptionalism.”

This ideological framework, Sachs explains, makes China’s rise alarming to the West. American leaders do not view it as a natural rebalancing but as a threat to be contained, often framing China’s success as artificial, fraudulent, or doomed to collapse. Sachs highlights the absence of equanimity in US discourse—no leaders acknowledge China’s historical greatness or its right to reclaim its global role. Instead, narratives of fear, disdain, and racial bias dominate, reflecting a deep-seated belief in Western superiority cultivated over two centuries.

## Contrasting Statecraft: Europe’s Conflict vs. China’s Harmony

Sachs delves into the fundamental differences between Western and Chinese statecraft, rooted in their historical trajectories. Europe’s political culture, he argues, was shaped by the 476 AD fall of the Western Roman Empire, which fragmented the continent into a kaleidoscope of competing entities—city-states, kingdoms, and duchies. This fragmentation led to 1,500 years of intra-European conflict, embedding a view of statecraft as inherently anarchic and competitive. International relations theories like realism, which assume states inevitably clash, reflect this European reality, from 500 AD to the mid-20th century.

China, by contrast, unified in 221 BCE under the Qin Dynasty and has largely remained a centralized administrative state for over 2,200 years, despite periods of fragmentation. Bounded by the Himalayas, the East China Sea, tropical forests, and northern steppes, China developed a cohesive “box” of governance. Its statecraft, influenced by Confucian philosophy, prioritizes internal harmony and stability over external conquest. Sachs notes that from 1368 (the start of the Ming Dynasty) to 1839 (Britain’s invasion in the First Opium War), China engaged in minimal conflict with its East Asian neighbors—Japan, Korea, and Vietnam—maintaining what scholars call the “Confucian peace.” Unlike European powers, China did not seek overseas territories, focusing instead on regional dominance through cultural and economic influence.

## Implications for the Economic War

The US-China economic war, Sachs suggests, is exacerbated by these clashing worldviews. The US, steeped in a Eurocentric tradition of geopolitical rivalry, perceives China’s rise as a zero-sum threat, leading to policies aimed at containment—tech bans, currency competition, and supply chain repatriation. China, however, approaches global relations with a mindset of harmony and mutual benefit, seeking to restore its historical role without replicating Western imperialism. Sachs disagrees with scholars like John Mearsheimer, who argue that China will behave like any other great power, asserting that China’s 2,000-year history of non-expansionist statecraft offers a different model.

This divergence complicates the economic war. The US’s aggressive posture risks escalating tensions, while China’s focus on stability and development challenges the Western assumption of inevitable conflict. Sachs advocates for a recognition of this historical rebalancing, urging the West to move beyond outdated ideologies and engage with China as a legitimate global power, not a threat to be contained.

## Toward a Multipolar Understanding

Jeffrey Sachs’s analysis reframes the US-China economic war as a moment of historical correction, where Asia reclaims its traditional role after centuries of Western dominance. The West’s fear, rooted in Eurocentric ideologies of superiority, clashes with China’s Confucian-influenced statecraft, which prioritizes harmony over conquest. As the world becomes less Western-centric, understanding these historical and cultural differences is crucial to navigating the economic war without escalating into broader conflict. Sachs’s long view offers a path toward a multipolar world, where mutual recognition, rather than rivalry, could define the future of global relations.
