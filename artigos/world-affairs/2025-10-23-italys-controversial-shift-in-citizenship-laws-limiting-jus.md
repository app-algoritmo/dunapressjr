## 2025 Italian Citizenship Reform: Meloni's Restrictions on Jus Sanguinis Explained

Italy has long been celebrated for its generous approach to citizenship by descent, allowing individuals around the world to claim Italian nationality through ancestral ties without generational limits. This principle, known as jus sanguinis or "right of blood," has enabled millions of descendants of Italian emigrants—particularly from the late 19th and early 20th centuries—to obtain passports, fostering a sense of global Italian diaspora. However, in a significant policy shift, Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni's government introduced reforms in 2025 that dramatically curtailed this access. The new law, enacted through Decree-Law 36/2025 and ratified by parliament, now limits citizenship claims to those with Italian parents or grandparents, effectively closing the door on more distant ancestry. This change has sparked widespread debate, touching on themes of national identity, immigration control, demographic challenges, and constitutional rights.

To understand the implications of this reform, it's essential to delve into the historical context of Italian citizenship laws. Italy's modern citizenship framework traces its roots to the unification of the country in 1861. The Civil Code of 1865 established jus sanguinis as the primary mode of acquiring nationality, reflecting a blood-based rather than territorial (jus soli) approach. This was further codified in the 1912 Citizenship Law (Law No. 555), which allowed citizenship to be passed down through paternal lines indefinitely, provided the ancestor did not renounce Italian nationality before the descendant's birth. Over time, amendments addressed gender equality; for instance, the 1983 reform ensured maternal transmission, and the 1992 Law (No. 91) modernized the system, confirming no generational cap on jus sanguinis while introducing residency-based paths for naturalization.

The 1992 law became a cornerstone for the Italian diaspora. With over 60 million people of Italian descent living abroad—primarily in countries like Brazil, Argentina, the United States, and Australia—applications surged in the 21st century. Factors such as economic instability in South America, the appeal of EU mobility, and even "passport shopping" contributed to this boom. By the early 2020s, Italian consulates were overwhelmed, with backlogs exceeding hundreds of thousands of cases. Courts in Italy also saw a rise in judicial recognitions, where applicants bypassed consular delays by filing directly in Italian tribunals. This system, while inclusive, was criticized for inefficiencies and perceived abuses, such as fraudulent documentation or claims from individuals with tenuous cultural ties to Italy.

Enter Giorgia Meloni, who assumed office in October 2022 as Italy's first female prime minister, leading a right-wing coalition including her Brothers of Italy party, the League, and Forza Italia. Meloni's administration has prioritized stricter immigration controls, aligning with her nationalist stance. While her focus has often been on curbing irregular migration across the Mediterranean, the citizenship reform targets what officials describe as the "commercialization" of Italian passports. On March 28, 2025, the Council of Ministers approved Decree-Law 36/2025, dubbed the "Tajani Decree" after Foreign Minister Antonio Tajani. This emergency measure immediately restricted jus sanguinis eligibility.

Under the new rules, automatic citizenship by descent is now limited to individuals whose parent or at least one grandparent was an Italian citizen by birth. For claims through grandparents, additional conditions may apply, such as the grandparent being born in Italy or the parent having resided in Italy for at least two years before the applicant's birth. This effectively ends claims based on great-grandparents or further ancestors, a practice that was previously unrestricted as long as the lineage could be documented. The decree also introduced residency requirements in certain cases, mandating that applicants demonstrate stronger connections to Italy, such as through consular applications rather than solely judicial paths.

The government's rationale for these changes is multifaceted. Primarily, it aims to alleviate administrative burdens. As of March 2025, there were over 60,000 pending legal proceedings for citizenship verification in Italian courts, with consulates facing similar overloads. Officials argued that distant claims often involved complex, costly verifications and sometimes exploitation by intermediaries charging exorbitant fees for document services. Meloni's administration framed the reform as a means to preserve the "authenticity" of Italian citizenship, preventing it from becoming a commodity for those without genuine ties. In a press release from the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, Tajani emphasized that the changes would "reinforce real connections" while addressing "abuses and tenuous descendant claims."

Demographic considerations also play a role. Italy faces a severe population decline, with birth rates at historic lows (around 1.24 children per woman in 2024) and an aging society. Meloni has publicly suggested that encouraging the return of Italian descendants could help, particularly "Christian families of Italian ancestry" from regions like Venezuela. In this light, the reform is not a complete rejection of the diaspora but a selective tightening: it prioritizes closer generations who might be more inclined to engage with Italy culturally or economically. However, critics argue this selectivity borders on discrimination, favoring certain profiles over others.

The reform's implementation has been swift and contentious. The decree took effect immediately upon approval, catching many mid-application off guard. Parliament ratified it into permanent law (Law 74/2025) on May 20, 2025, despite opposition protests. Applicants who had initiated processes before March 28, 2025, were grandfathered in under the old rules, but new claims must comply with the restrictions. This retroactive element has fueled legal challenges. Associations like Avvocati Uniti per la Cittadinanza Italiana (AUCI) and Associazione Giuristi Iure Sanguinis (AGIS) have filed suits, arguing the law violates constitutional principles of equality and non-discrimination. They contend that citizenship by descent is a "familial bond" that cannot be arbitrarily severed by administrative decree.

Internationally, the changes have reverberated through Italian-descendant communities. In Brazil and Argentina, home to the largest such populations (estimated at 30 million and 20 million respectively), there has been outrage. Many view the reform as a betrayal, severing ties to their heritage. For instance, in Argentina, where economic crises have driven citizenship applications, descendants now face barriers to EU opportunities. In the United States, Italian-American groups have lobbied against the changes, highlighting how they undermine family histories tied to Italy's emigration waves. Social media platforms buzzed with reactions, with hashtags like #JusSanguinisReform and #MeloniCitizenship trending in spring 2025.

Expert opinions are divided. Sociologists like Maurizio Taddone have warned that Italy is "in collapse and still rejects its children," pointing to the irony of restricting diaspora access amid demographic woes. Legal scholars, such as those from the University of Bologna's migration studies program, argue the reform aligns with broader European trends toward hybrid citizenship models—blending jus sanguinis with jus soli elements—but question its haste without parliamentary debate. A 2025 HAL-SHS paper titled "Are We Witnessing a Revolution of the Italian Citizenship Regime?" analyzes the shift, suggesting it moves Italy closer to Germany's 2000 reforms, which introduced conditional jus soli for long-term residents' children.

Comparatively, Italy's new policy still remains relatively open. Countries like Germany limit jus sanguinis to one generation born abroad, while Spain requires residency tests for descendants beyond grandparents. Yet, the abruptness of Italy's change stands out, especially given its historical emphasis on blood ties. In June 2025, a national referendum on easing citizenship rules—proposing jus soli for children born in Italy to long-term foreign residents—failed amid low turnout, with Meloni urging abstention. This highlights the polarized political landscape: her coalition views expanded citizenship as a threat to national cohesion, while opposition parties advocate for inclusivity to address labor shortages.

The broader implications extend to EU dynamics. Italian citizenship grants EU-wide rights, so restrictions could influence migration patterns. For affected individuals, alternatives include naturalization after 10 years of residency (or five if married to an Italian), but tighter visa rules under Meloni make this challenging for non-EU citizens. Economically, the reform might reduce "citizenship tourism," where applicants boost local economies through legal and translation services, but it could also diminish remittances or investments from the diaspora.

In conclusion, Giorgia Meloni's 2025 citizenship reform marks a pivotal moment in Italy's identity politics. By limiting jus sanguinis to parents and grandparents, it seeks to streamline processes and affirm "genuine" ties, but at the cost of alienating millions. As legal battles unfold and demographic pressures persist, the debate underscores the tension between heritage preservation and modern inclusivity. Whether this signals a "revolution" in citizenship regimes remains to be seen, but it undeniably reshapes how Italy connects with its global family.
