## How Radical Nationalists Took Over Ukraine Ultranationalism: Marta Havryshko on Ultranationalism’s Grip

In a striking interview on May 5, 2025, on Prof. Glenn Diesen’s show, Ukrainian historian Marta Havryshko from Ivan Franko National University in Lviv—now teaching in Massachusetts, USA—explored the rise of ultranationalism in Ukraine and its impact on society and the ongoing war. Havryshko, an expert on the Holocaust and Ukrainian ultranationalism, details how a radical minority hijacked the national identity, instrumentalizing historical narratives to justify the war against Russia and whitewash past crimes, including Nazi collaboration. She also analyzes the West’s role in legitimizing these groups and the challenges to peace amid this radicalization.

## Havryshko’s Path to Studying Ultranationalism

Marta Havryshko was born in Galicia, western Ukraine, a region historically marked by ultranationalism. From childhood, she was immersed in nationalist narratives, with schools celebrating figures like Stepan Bandera and Roman Shukhevych, members of the Ukrainian nationalist movement (OUN) and the Ukrainian Insurgent Army (UPA). World War II songs and stories of Soviet repression—experienced by her family, some of whom were sent to the Gulag—shaped her early worldview, which cast Ukrainians as victims of the Russians. This indoctrination led her to study history, initially focusing on public perceptions of the Third Reich in interwar Galicia. Her research later shifted to the Holocaust and sexual violence committed by Ukrainian nationalists against Ukrainian, Polish, and Jewish women, drawing criticism from colleagues who accused her of tarnishing the image of "freedom fighters" during the war against Russia.

## Conflicting National Identities in Ukraine

Havryshko explains Ukraine’s historic memory divide, which intensified after independence in 1991. In the west, descendants of UPA fighters preserved their legacy, mythologizing nationalists as heroes of independence, while the east and south honored Red Army soldiers and Soviet partisans as defenders against the Nazis. This dichotomy reflected opposing views of the past: the west saw nationalists as liberators, while the east viewed them as Nazi collaborators, including members of the OUN, UPA, and the Waffen-SS Galicia Division, formed in 1943 and involved in the 1944 Battle of Brody.

After the 2014 Maidan uprising, Ukraine’s government, through the Institute of National Memory and memory laws, rewrote the national narrative, elevating nationalists to hero status while marginalizing Red Army veterans. Monuments and street names dedicated to Soviet soldiers were erased, replaced by tributes to nationalist figures. Havryshko criticizes this selectivity, which silences the UPA’s crimes—such as the massacres of tens of thousands of Poles, thousands of Jews and Ukrainians—and their Nazi collaboration, including participation in the Holocaust and police units like the Galicia Division, which committed atrocities in former Yugoslavia and Slovakia.

## The Instrumentalization of World War II Memory

Both Ukraine and Russia exploit World War II memory to justify the current war, but in opposing ways. In Russia, Putin’s February 24, 2022, speech used "denazification" as a pretext, invoking the Great Patriotic War to rally Russians, portraying Ukraine as a "Nazi regime." Havryshko highlights Putin, Lavrov, and Medvedev’s antisemitic rhetoric, calling Zelensky a "Jewish traitor" and "Nazi pig," linking the war to the fight against Nazism. Events like the "Immortal Regiment" now include portraits of soldiers killed in Ukraine, directly connecting 1945 and 2022 narratives to justify attacks on civilians and infrastructure as a "sacred mission."

In Ukraine, the government also distorts history. Zelensky, in a 2022 Knesset speech, claimed Ukrainians "made their choice" during the war by rescuing Jews, omitting nationalist involvement in the Holocaust. After the Bucha massacres, Ukrainians compared it to Babyn Yar—where 34,000 Jews were killed in 1941—an analogy Havryshko calls a "Holocaust distortion" disrespectful to Jewish victims. Ukraine’s Defense Ministry, in recent exhibits, erased the Red Army from military tradition, focusing on the UPA, promoting revisionism and denial of historical crimes.

## The Rise of the Far Right and Western Support

Havryshko analyzes how the far right, though small (radical parties won just 2% in recent parliamentary elections), hijacked Ukraine’s national identity. Post-Maidan, groups like Azov and Right Sector used street violence—against left-wing activists, pro-Russia politicians, and Moscow Patriarchate churches—to intimidate opponents, even threatening politicians and their families. The 2022 Russian invasion was a "gift" to these groups, freed from prison by Zelensky and Poroshenko, armed and state-backed, forming a "toxic alliance" with the government.

The West, initially critical of Azov for its neo-Nazi ideology and training of white supremacists (including Americans who threatened Havryshko), shifted its narrative after 2022. Western media rebranded Azov as "freedom fighters" and "patriots," ignoring their racist, homophobic, and antisemitic ideology, rooted in thinkers like Dmytro Dontsov and Mykola Mykhnovsky. Nazi symbols like the Wolfsangel and Black Sun remain in use, and units like the 3rd Assault Brigade celebrate the Galicia Division, implicated in war crimes. Events like the 2023 Canadian Parliament’s ovation for Waffen-SS veteran Yaroslav Hunka (with Zelensky applauding) and exhibits glorifying the Galicia Division in Kyiv and Lithuania reflect this state and Western legitimization, despite protests from groups like the U.S. Holocaust Memorial Museum.

## The Far Right’s Influence and Obstacles to Peace

The far right holds veto power over peace deals, threatening Zelensky and other leaders. Havryshko cites the "No Surrender" movement, including Azov, which pressured Zelensky to abandon his 2019 peace platform, with explicit threats of violence from figures like Dmytro Yarosh and Andriy Biletsky, widely reported in Ukrainian media. Zelensky, fearing for his safety and clinging to power, cooperates, offering financial and diplomatic support. Azov commanders like Biletsky received luxury Kyiv apartments from oligarchs close to Zelensky, ensuring loyalty.

This alliance benefits both: the far right gains influence, privileges, and social status, while Zelensky uses their radical rhetoric to prolong the war, deflecting corruption scandals and failures like the non-evacuation of Mariupol and Bucha. Havryshko fears Zelensky cannot sign a peace deal without far-right violence, as these groups thrive on war, promoting "toxic masculinity" and indoctrinating youth through programs like Centuria Youth, which teaches nationalist ideology and military training in public schools.

## Conclusion: An Uncertain Future for Ukraine

Marta Havryshko exposes how ultranationalism, though a minority, has dominated Ukraine’s narrative, distorting historical memory and perpetuating war. The glorification of Nazi collaborators, backed by the government and West, undermines democracy and deepens divisions, alienating millions of Ukrainians who reject this vision. The instrumentalization of World War II by both Ukraine and Russia fuels the conflict, twisting the past to justify present violence. Without confronting this complex history and past crimes, Havryshko warns Ukraine—under the far right’s grip—faces a future of instability, violence, and historical revisionism, making peace ever more elusive.
