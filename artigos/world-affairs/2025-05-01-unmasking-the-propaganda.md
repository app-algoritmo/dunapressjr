## **Unmasking the Propaganda: Patrik Baab on the Ukraine War’s Geopolitical Layers

The Ukraine war, often framed as a simple Russian-Ukrainian conflict, is a multifaceted struggle with deep geopolitical consequences. In a recent interview, hosted by Prof. Glenn Diesen, veteran German journalist Patrik Baab, with over 40 years of experience reporting from conflict zones like the Balkans, Afghanistan, and Ukraine, dissects the war’s complexity. Baab, author of the German bestseller *On Both Sides of the Front Line*, reveals how media narratives, particularly in Germany, have distorted reality, serving Western geopolitical interests while suppressing dissent and ignoring the war’s broader dimensions.

## The Ukraine War: Beyond the Battlefield

Baab identifies three interconnected levels of the Ukraine conflict. First, the military clash spans a 1,300-kilometer front line, where Russian forces prioritize destroying the enemy over territorial gains—a strategy misunderstood by Western military planners. In regions like Kursk, Donetsk, and Bakhmut, Russia advances slowly, leveraging time to its advantage. Baab notes that Kremlin advisors have signaled intentions to hold occupied areas and possibly organize referendums in regions like Kharkiv and Odessa, allowing locals to decide their allegiance.

Second, the war extends to global economic warfare, with over 29,000 sanctions targeting Russia. This economic strangulation aims to weaken Russia, but Baab argues it also serves Western elites by transferring wealth from Europe’s working classes to economic elites, particularly in the US. The war facilitates the exploitation of Ukraine’s resources, such as its fertile black earth, benefiting large agricultural corporations like Monsanto while undercutting European farmers.

Third, a propaganda war shapes public perception, particularly in the West. Baab, drawing from his on-the-ground reporting in Donbas, highlights a stark disconnect between Western media narratives and reality. While German outlets like *Focus* and *The Economist* portray Russian-occupied areas as oppressive, locals told Baab they felt “liberated” by Russian forces, expressing frustration that intervention didn’t come sooner in 2014. This narrative suppression, Baab argues, stems from media alignment with political elites, prioritizing career interests over truth.

## The War’s Deeper Roots: A Timeline of Provocation

Contrary to the Western narrative of an “unprovoked” Russian invasion in 2022, Baab traces the conflict’s origins to 2014, following the Maidan coup. He cites American historian Nikolai Petro’s work, which reveals how Western-backed ultranationalists in Ukraine prepared for civil war by arming themselves during Maidan. The coup unleashed violence across Ukraine, including massacres in Odessa and Mariupol, alienating eastern regions like Donbas. The subsequent civil war saw Kiev’s forces shelling Donbas civilians, prompting the secession of Lugansk and Donetsk—actions Baab notes are permissible under international law, as seen with Kosovo.

Western leaders, including German politicians like President Steinmeier, were complicit in orchestrating Maidan, according to Baab. Citing Ivan Katchanovski’s research on the Maidan massacre, he accuses Western diplomats of involvement in mass murder—a criminal act they refuse to acknowledge, driving their nations deeper into conflict to avoid accountability. This denial, Baab argues, reflects a broader “apocalypse blindness” among European elites, who underestimate Russia and ignore the catastrophic risks of their policies, including the potential for nuclear escalation.

## The Transformation of German Media and Politics

Baab describes a dramatic shift in German media and political culture, driven by a new generation of leaders detached from the realities of war. Unlike earlier decades, when German leaders like Angela Merkel cautioned against NATO expansion into Ukraine—viewing it as a “declaration of war” by Moscow—current politicians like Annalena Baerbock and Friedrich Merz embrace confrontation. Merz’s claim that Russia “attacked Europe” and Germany must rearm reflects a Russophobia Baab attributes to narcissistic elites living in a “bubble.” These leaders, often groomed by transatlantic organizations like the German Marshall Fund and Soros foundations, prioritize career advancement over national interest, pushing Germany toward war hysteria.

The German media, once a platform for diverse perspectives, has become a propaganda tool. Baab recalls how ARD, where he worked for 40 years, withdrew correspondents from Donbas in 2014, abandoning on-the-ground reporting. Today, journalists align with political elites to secure careers, self-censoring to fit narratives. Digitalization exacerbates this, as reporters rely on internet sources—often skewed by propaganda—rather than firsthand accounts. Baab’s own experience reflects this suppression: after reporting from Donbas, he lost teaching positions at German universities, and dissidents struggle to find public venues for discussion.

## The Cost to Civil Society: A Slide Toward Authoritarianism

The war has eroded civil liberties in Germany, with Baab declaring that “there is no freedom of speech” anymore. Article 5 of the German Constitution, guaranteeing free expression, is effectively void in political practice. The government uses legal mechanisms, including state attorneys and police, to suppress dissent. Public contracts are terminated for those labeled as dissidents, creating a climate of fear. Baab cites the banning of Russian and Soviet flags on Victory Day and the seizure of Russian tourists’ assets as evidence of a broader Russophobia, mirroring Western trends of collective punishment that vilify all Russians as part of “Putin’s war machine.”

Baab sees Germany as a “front democracy,” where a political cartel has hijacked institutions, stifling genuine democratic discourse. This aligns with Diesen’s observation of a hijacked civil society, where government-funded NGOs blur the line between democracy and totalitarianism, amplifying propaganda rather than checking power.

## Breaking the Bubble: A Call for New Media

Baab believes reforming mainstream media is impossible, as it is too entrenched in elite interests. Instead, he advocates for alternative media platforms like Diesen’s blog, Germany’s *NachDenkSeiten*, *Overton* magazine, and outlets run by figures like Milena Preradović. These spaces allow for unfiltered discourse, challenging the dominant narrative that the war began in 2022 and was “unprovoked.” Baab emphasizes the need to reframe the timeline, starting with the 2014 Maidan coup, to reveal Western provocations and Russia’s reactive stance—a perspective echoed by historical figures like George Kennan and William Perry, who warned against NATO expansion.

## Facing Reality to Avert Catastrophe

The Ukraine war, as Baab and Diesen reveal, is not a simple conflict but a geopolitical struggle driven by Western ambitions to weaken Russia and secure resources, at the expense of Ukraine and Europe’s stability. The German media and political elite, trapped in a self-reinforcing bubble, perpetuate propaganda that risks nuclear escalation while eroding democratic freedoms at home. Breaking this cycle requires a return to reality—through independent media, honest timelines, and a rejection of Russophobia. Without such a shift, Europe risks not only losing Ukraine but also plunging into a broader, more devastating conflict. Baab’s firsthand insights underscore the urgency of this reckoning, urging societies to prioritize truth over narrative to ensure a safer, freer future.
