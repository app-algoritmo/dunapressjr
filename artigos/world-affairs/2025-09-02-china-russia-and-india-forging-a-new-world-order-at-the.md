## 2025 SCO Summit: China, Russia, and India Build a New World Order – Gilbert Doctorow’s Insights

The air was thick with anticipation on September 1, 2025, as the Shanghai Cooperation Organization (SCO) Summit unfolded in Tianjin, China, drawing global attention to a historic convergence of power. In an insightful interview between Professor Glenn Diesen and historian Gilbert Doctorow, author of *War Diaries: Volume 1: The Russia-Ukraine War, 2022-2023*, the stage was set to unpack this pivotal moment. Doctorow, a seasoned international affairs analyst, highlighted the unprecedented alignment of China, Russia, and India—three Eurasian giants—whose leaders, Xi Jinping, Vladimir Putin, and Narendra Modi, displayed a warmth that belied years of tension. For Modi, this marked his first visit to China in seven years, a gesture underscored by hugs with Putin and handshakes with Xi, signaling a potential reset in relations. As someone who has watched geopolitical tides shift for decades, Doctorow sees this summit as more than a diplomatic photo op—it’s the embryonic stage of a new world order, one where sovereignty and mutual interest trump ideological blocs.

The SCO, originally founded 30 years ago by Russia and China to ensure security in Central Asia and curb U.S. influence, has evolved dramatically. With 25-26 countries represented—ranging from full members like Pakistan and Iran to observers like Belarus—the summit showcased a diverse tapestry of interests. Doctorow points to the joint declaration condemning cross-border terrorism, a subtle jab at Pakistan, which India celebrated as a diplomatic victory. Yet, he cautions against oversimplification; Pakistan’s ties to China and Iran’s role in the North-South Corridor, vital for India’s access to Central Asia, complicate the narrative. The unexpected warmth between Armenia’s Nikol Pashinyan and Putin, aired on Russian TV, further illustrates how intertwined and sometimes conflicting interests are being navigated.

Economically, the summit marked a bold expansion. Xi’s announcement of a planned SCO development bank signals a move toward financial integration, challenging the U.S.-dominated global trade system. With the SCO contributing $24 trillion to global GDP, this initiative, potentially backed by Middle Eastern nations like Saudi Arabia and the UAE, aims to reduce dollar dependency. The working languages—Russian and Mandarin—underscore China and Russia’s leadership, though Doctorow sees India’s Modi positioning his nation as a rising co-governor, a shift evident in the leaders’ final-hour huddles.

This alignment contrasts with Western alliance models. Doctorow argues that the SCO isn’t about erasing competing interests but managing them for mutual benefit—China and India resolving border tensions, or India and Pakistan finding economic incentives despite rivalry. Unlike NATO’s rigid blocs, the SCO offers a loose framework where sovereignty reigns, a philosophy echoed in India’s defiance of Trump’s pressure to curb Russian oil imports. This stance, debated on Indian channels like NewsX and CNN-18, highlights a double standard: China faces no such tariffs despite buying more oil, straining U.S.-India ties. The Financial Times noted this could dismantle the Quad, a U.S.-led containment strategy against China, a move Doctorow sees as intentional under Trump’s Kissinger-inspired vision of a multipolar balance.

Trump’s tariffs, targeting India’s textile sector (2% of its workforce), reflect this strategy, though they spare India’s $80 billion IT and pharma trades. Doctorow interprets this as a calculated blow to break India from the Quad, aligning with Kissinger’s 1994 *Diplomacy*, which favored a pre-WWI concert of powers over ideological alliances. This contrasts with neoconservative values driving U.S. policy, a tension Kissinger later softened in his 2014 *World Order*. Trump, advised by Kissinger in 2016-2017, pursues this realist approach, dismantling blocs like NATO and the Quad within his limited powers.

The summit’s significance lies in its rejection of unipolarity. Doctorow sees India’s refusal to join an anti-China camp as a declaration of sovereignty, mirrored by Russia’s trade surplus with China—hydrocarbons, agriculture, and now jet engines for China’s sanctioned airliner. India-China ties, lacking trade compatibility, won’t mirror this depth but may end enmity, fostering Eurasian economic and security cooperation. This shift accelerates a multipolar world, with the SCO offering an alternative economic architecture as trust in the dollar wanes.

Yet, challenges remain. Modi’s stop in Japan and absence from Beijing’s military parade—honoring WWII’s Pacific end, where Indian soldiers died—drew Doctorow’s critique, missing a chance to honor history. Trump’s absence, too, underscores U.S. isolation. Western commentary often fixates on SCO’s competing interests, missing its strength in addressing conflicts pragmatically, unlike Europe’s compromise of national interests. Doctorow predicts Trump’s tariffs will ease post-Ukraine, as India and the U.S. recalibrate ties, though India’s non-alignment persists.

This summit isn’t just geopolitics—it’s a human story of nations reclaiming agency. From Modi’s hugs to Putin’s jet engine deals, it reflects a world tired of dictated alliances. Doctorow’s analysis, grounded in his *War Diaries*, invites us to see this as a landmark in a multipolar dawn, where sovereignty and cooperation redefine global relations.
