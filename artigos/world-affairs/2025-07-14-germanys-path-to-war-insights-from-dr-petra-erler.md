## Germany's Path to War - Dr. Petra Erler on Germany’s Role in Escalating Tensions with Russia

In a revealing interview on the Neutrality Studies channel of Pascal Lottaz, Dr. Petra Erler, a German political scientist and former East German politician, discusses the complex historical and political dynamics that have shaped Germany’s foreign policy and its role in escalating tensions with Russia.

Drawing from her book, *The Long Road to War: Russia, Ukraine, and the West - Escalation Instead of Détente*, co-authored with prominent EU politician Günther Verheugen, Dr. Erler provides a critical perspective on the broken promises and geopolitical missteps that have led Europe to the brink of conflict. This article delves into the key themes from the interview, exploring the betrayal of post-Cold War hopes, the impact of NATO’s expansion, and the resurgence of militaristic rhetoric in Germany. ## The Broken Promises of the Post-Cold War Era

Dr. Erler begins by highlighting the optimism that swept Central and Eastern Europe during the 1989 peaceful revolutions, which promised to overcome the continent’s Cold War divisions. The vision of a “common European home,” as envisioned by Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev, aimed to foster cooperation and demilitarization.

Agreements like the Anti-Ballistic Missile (ABM) Treaty and the Paris Charter reflected these hopes, with commitments to abolish nuclear weapons and respect mutual security interests. However, these promises were systematically broken. Dr. Erler argues that the West, particularly the United States, prioritized maintaining its dominance over Europe rather than fostering equal partnerships. The dissolution of the Soviet Union left the U.S. as the sole superpower, leading to a unilateral approach that disregarded Russian security concerns and European aspirations for a demilitarized world.

The NATO expansion eastward, despite assurances to the contrary, is a central point of contention. Dr. Erler notes that the 1990 agreements, including those in Belarus and Alma-Ata, emphasized respecting Soviet (later Russian) interests. However, NATO’s enlargement, particularly the 2008 decision to consider Ukraine and Georgia for membership, was perceived by Russia as a direct threat. Former German Chancellor Angela Merkel’s acknowledgment that this move was seen as a “declaration of war” by Vladimir Putin underscores the recklessness of Western policymakers. Dr. Erler criticizes this as a display of arrogance and irresponsibility, noting that Russia had not committed any significant transgressions at the time to justify such provocations.

## The Betrayal of East German and European Hopes

As a former East German politician instrumental in Germany’s reunification, Dr. Erler emphasizes the betrayal felt by East Germans and other Europeans who hoped for a peaceful, unified continent. The 1989 revolutions were driven by a desire to end the fear of nuclear annihilation and create a cooperative Europe. Yet, the integration of Eastern European countries into the EU, while a step toward unity, did not erase the lingering Cold War divisions. Instead, the “front” merely shifted eastward, with Russia increasingly viewed as an adversary rather than a partner. Dr. Erler points out that the lack of genuine reconciliation with Russia, coupled with the failure to address historical grievances from World War II, has perpetuated mistrust.

In Germany, the narrative of the “clean Wehrmacht” and the incomplete acknowledgment of atrocities committed against Soviet citizens during Operation Barbarossa have hindered reconciliation. Dr. Erler references Putin’s personal connection to the Siege of Leningrad, where his family suffered immense losses, to illustrate the missed opportunities for dialogue. She finds it incomprehensible that Germany, given its historical responsibility, has taken a leading role in remilitarizing against Russia, ignoring the devastating consequences of past conflicts.

## The Resurgence of Militarism in Germany

A striking theme in the interview is the resurgence of militaristic rhetoric in Germany, particularly since the 2022 “Zeitenwende” (turning point) declared by Chancellor Olaf Scholz. Dr. Erler is alarmed by calls for increased military spending and the readiness to sacrifice lives for geopolitical goals. She cites disturbing statements from German media, where psychologists advocate for mothers to prepare to send their sons to war, evoking chilling parallels to past eras. The term “Kriegstüchtigkeit” (war readiness), used by a Social Democratic defense minister, is particularly troubling given its historical connotations.

Dr. Erler argues that this shift was not inevitable. The 1990s were a decade of hope, marked by successful disarmament treaties like the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces (INF) Treaty and the Chemical Weapons Convention. However, the U.S. withdrawal from the ABM Treaty in the early 2000s, dismissed as unnecessary in a post-Cold War world, marked a turning point. Putin’s efforts to maintain transparency in arms control were ignored, paving the way for a renewed arms race. Dr. Erler sees this as part of a broader failure to engage in dialogue, driven by a narrative that paints Russia as an inherent threat.

## The Role of Propaganda and Fear

The interview also addresses the role of propaganda in shaping public perception. Dr. Erler notes that in Germany, a significant portion of the population fears expressing dissenting opinions due to social pressures. This silence, coupled with aggressive anti-Russian narratives, has stifled debate and enabled the escalation of tensions. In Ukraine, she argues, the initial desire for peace, as evidenced by Volodymyr Zelenskyy’s election as a peace candidate, was undermined by violent nationalists and external influences. Western support for these elements, without addressing their extremism, has contributed to the ongoing conflict.

Dr. Erler also critiques the double standards in Western foreign policy, particularly regarding human rights and international law. While Russia’s actions in Ukraine are condemned, similar violations by Western allies, such as U.S. and Israeli strikes on Iran, are often excused. This selective outrage, she argues, reflects a broader failure to uphold universal principles, further alienating non-Western nations.

## The Global Context and the Decline of Western Hegemony

Dr. Erler situates the Ukraine conflict within a broader global shift, where the West’s dominance is waning. The Munich Security Conference’s 2020 “Westlessness” report acknowledged the West’s diminishing ability to control global affairs, fueling frustration and aggressive posturing. She sees the current war rhetoric as a desperate attempt to cling to power in a multipolar world. The economic fallout from sanctions on Russia has hit Germany hard, with high energy prices threatening its industrial base. Dr. Erler views this as a self-inflicted wound, driven by blind allegiance to U.S. policies that prioritize weakening Russia over European interests.

She also draws parallels with historical attempts to control Russia’s vast resources, a goal that dates back to the 19th century and persists in modern U.S. strategy. The notion that Russia must be “kept small” or fragmented reflects a colonial mindset, ignoring its historical resilience against invasions. Dr. Erler warns that this approach risks catastrophic consequences, including the potential for nuclear escalation, which would devastate Europe.

## Hope in Resistance and Dialogue

Despite the grim outlook, Dr. Erler finds hope in public resistance to militarism. Polls in Germany show majority support for negotiated solutions and opposition to deploying intermediate-range missiles. She believes that people instinctively recognize the falsehoods in war propaganda, even if they lack access to all information. Young people, in particular, are reluctant to be drawn into conflicts, offering a glimmer of hope against the tide of militarization.

Dr. Erler advocates for dialogue as the only viable path forward. She rejects comparisons of Putin to historical villains like Hitler, arguing that such rhetoric trivializes past atrocities and shuts down communication. Her book aims to challenge the dominant war narrative, aligning with the majority of humanity that seeks cooperation over conflict. By engaging with global perspectives, particularly from Africa, Asia, and Latin America, she believes Europe can move beyond its self-centered approach and work toward a more inclusive world order.

Dr. Petra Erler’s interview offers a sobering analysis of Germany’s and Europe’s descent into militarism, driven by broken promises, historical amnesia, and external pressures. Her insights underscore the need for introspection and dialogue to prevent further escalation and preserve the vision of a peaceful, cooperative Europe. As the world grapples with shifting power dynamics, her call for a return to diplomacy and mutual respect is both timely and urgent.
