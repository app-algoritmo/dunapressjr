## Norwegian Generals' "Beredt" Book on Russia Threats Launches During NATO Steadfast Noon Exercise

In the crisp autumn air of mid-October 2025, as leaves turned golden across Scandinavia, two brothers stood at the forefront of Norway's national conversation on security. Eirik Kristoffersen, the nation's Chief of Defence, and his younger sibling Frode Kristoffersen, freshly promoted to Major General and serving at NATO's Joint Force Command in Norfolk, Virginia, unveiled their collaborative work, "Beredt: Forsvarsevne og Motstandskraft" (Prepared: Defensive Capability and Resilience). Published by Gyldendal on October 15, the book arrives not in isolation but in the shadow of a monumental event: NATO's annual nuclear deterrence exercise, Steadfast Noon, which kicked off just two days earlier on October 13 and ran through October 24. This convergence of literary debut and live-fire simulation—albeit without actual warheads—feels less like coincidence and more like a clarion call, echoing the brothers' central thesis that Norway must confront its vulnerabilities head-on in an era of resurgent great-power rivalry.

Eirik, at 56, has helmed Norway's armed forces since 2020, navigating the country through the seismic shifts wrought by Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022. His tenure has seen a dramatic pivot: defense spending surging to meet NATO's 2% GDP target, F-35 stealth fighters bolstering the skies over the Arctic, and a renewed emphasis on territorial defense after decades skewed toward expeditionary missions. Frode, two years his junior, brings a complementary perspective from the alliance's nerve center in the U.S., where he oversees strategic planning that bridges Norwegian specifics with transatlantic imperatives. Their partnership isn't new; the siblings have long shared a intellectual bond forged in military service, but "Beredt" marks their first joint foray into public authorship, blending memoir, analysis, and prescription into a 300-page manifesto for national fortitude.

The timing could scarcely be more poignant. Steadfast Noon, NATO's premier nuclear training drill, unfolded across the skies and seas of northwestern Europe, involving up to 70 aircraft from 14 member states and roughly 2,000 personnel. Hosted primarily by the Netherlands at Volkel Air Base, with support from Belgium and the UK, the exercise simulates the alliance's nuclear posture without deploying live weapons—a deliberate choice to underscore deterrence over provocation. Dual-capable fighters, tankers, and surveillance planes practiced interoperability, ensuring that in a crisis, NATO's response would be seamless and credible. Secretary General Mark Rutte, in announcing the drill, emphasized its routine nature: "This exercise helps to ensure that our nuclear deterrent remains safe, secure, effective, and credible." Yet, against the backdrop of Russia's war in Ukraine and escalating rhetoric from Moscow, the symbolism resonates deeply, particularly in Norway, whose 198-kilometer border with Russia in the Arctic makes it the alliance's frontline in the High North.

For the first time, Norway contributed not just observers or staff but actual combat assets: a contingent of F-35A Lightning II jets, the crown jewels of its air force. These fifth-generation stealth aircraft, numbering just a handful for the exercise, flew in escort and support roles alongside U.S. B-52 bombers and other allied platforms. It's a milestone, signaling Oslo's evolution from peripheral participant to integral player in NATO's nuclear umbrella. Historically, Norway has adhered to a "no nukes on soil in peacetime" policy, a legacy of its post-World War II pacifism and Cold War neutrality flirtations. The F-35s, while nuclear-certified in American service for the B61-12 gravity bomb, won't carry such ordnance under Norwegian command. Instead, their role here tests data-sharing, sensor fusion, and multi-domain coordination—vital for countering hybrid threats that blur the lines between peace and war in the Barents Sea region.

"Beredt" doesn't shy from this nuclear undercurrent; indeed, it devotes significant ink to the atomic specter, framing it as one of Russia's three-tiered threats to Norwegian sovereignty. The brothers argue that while the nuclear shadow is omnipresent, its invocation remains a high-threshold gambit. "The threat is real, but the bar for use is extraordinarily elevated," they write, drawing on declassified assessments and personal insights from command posts. At the apex sits the strategic level: outright nuclear exchange targeting NATO heartlands, including strikes on U.S. soil or European population centers. This isn't idle saber-rattling; Russia's doctrine, updated in 2020 and reaffirmed amid Ukraine, posits nukes as a viable escalatory tool if the state's existence is imperiled. For Norway, proximity to the Kola Peninsula—home to Russia's Northern Fleet and an estimated 70% of its nuclear arsenal—amplifies the peril. Severomorsk and Polyarnый buzz with Borei-class submarines and Iskander missiles, a "bastion" strategy designed to shield second-strike capabilities from Western incursion.

Descending the ladder, the regional threat looms as a more insidious prospect: Russian forces seizing Norwegian territory to fortify their Kola redoubt. In a crisis—say, heightened NATO maneuvers or a flare-up in the GIUK Gap—the Kremlin might deem it "necessary" to push across the border, occupying Finnmark or Troms to create a buffer. This isn't fantastical; Norwegian intelligence has long flagged such scenarios, noting Russia's militarization of the Arctic, from hypersonic missile deployments to expanded basing at Nagurskoye on Franz Josef Land. The brothers dissect this in "Beredt" with clinical precision, citing wargames where Russian paratroopers could air-assault key nodes like Kirkenes within hours. Yet, they temper alarm with realism: Moscow's Ukraine quagmire has stretched its conventional forces thin, and Article 5 invocation would summon the full fury of 32 allies. Still, the psychological toll of such a limited incursion—evacuating civilians, disrupting Svalbard fisheries—could fracture national cohesion without triggering all-out war.

The base layer, and perhaps the most pervasive, comprises "complex threats": a cocktail of sabotage, disinformation, cyber intrusions, and intelligence ops that erode resolve without firing a shot. Here, "Beredt" shines in its granularity, recounting real-world vignettes like the 2022 undersea cable cuts near Svalbard or GPS jamming over the Barents Sea, attributed to Russian actors. The brothers invoke the "grey zone" playbook from Crimea 2014, warning that Norway's open democracy and tech-dependent society make it ripe for exploitation. Cyberattacks on Equinor refineries or influence ops via TikTok could sow doubt faster than tanks roll. Their prescription? A "total defense" ethos, echoing Sweden and Finland's models, where civilians train in resilience, businesses harden supply chains, and politicians prioritize deterrence over diplomacy alone.

What elevates "Beredt" beyond dry strategy is its human pulse. The Kristoffersens interweave personal anecdotes—Eirik's dawn patrols in the F-16 era, Frode's Virginia war-room vigils—with broader historical reflections. They grapple with Norway's post-Cold War complacency, admitting their own complicity in the 2000s when budgets favored Kabul over Karasjok. "We were too eager for the world's applause," Eirik confesses in one passage, a rare vulnerability from a stoic general. The book nods to April 9, 1940—the Nazi blitzkrieg that spurred the resistance movement—urging modern Norwegians to channel that "forsvarsvilje" (defensive will). It's not jingoism; it's a plea for societal buy-in, from school curricula on hybrid warfare to community drills mimicking blackouts.

Critics have praised the volume's candor while nitpicking its scope. Aftenposten's reviewer lauded the brothers' accessibility but chided them for circling sensitive topics like procurement scandals or alliance frictions. Fair points, yet the restraint speaks to their insider status; Eirik, after all, briefs the Storting weekly. Altinget highlighted the invasion hypothesis as "chillingly plausible," underscoring how Kola's nukes could drag Norway into escalation despite its non-nuclear stance. Broader reviews echo this: the book isn't alarmist but awakening, aligning with Norway's 2024 defense white paper that earmarks billions for Arctic fortifications.

Steadfast Noon's choreography—F-35s weaving through contested airspace, simulating intercepts of hypothetical Tu-95 Bears—mirrors "Beredt"'s urgency. Norway's jets, based at Ørland and now Evenes, embody the shift: from observer to operator in the nuclear dance. This debut isn't mere optics; it's interoperability gold, fusing Norwegian sensors with American algorithms to deny Russian overmatch. As Rutte noted, such drills "promote transparency," a subtle jab at Moscow's opacity, where Zapad exercises routinely flex Oreshnik missiles.

Zooming out, the Arctic's thaw unmasks old fault lines. Russia's Northern Fleet, with its Delta IV subs lurking under ice, views Norway as a tripwire: tamper with Kola, and Oslo becomes ground zero. Norwegian assessments warn of radiological risks from tests like the Burevestnik drone, with no anomalies detected yet but vigilance eternal. Beijing's shadow lengthens too, eyeing rare-earth routes through the Northeast Passage. The brothers touch this peripherally, focusing on the Russo-Norwegian dyad, but their framework applies: resilience trumps reaction.

"Beredt" concludes on a defiant note: Norway's strength lies not in size but spirit, a 5.5 million-strong archipelago punching above its weight through alliances and ingenuity. As Steadfast Noon wrapped, with F-35s returning to hangars humming with after-action reports, the book's message lingers. In Virginia's command bunkers and Oslo's policy halls, Eirik and Frode's words challenge complacency. The nuclear threshold may be high, but thresholds erode. Preparation isn't paranoia; it's prudence. For a nation wedged between fjords and foes, "beredt" isn't optional—it's existential.
