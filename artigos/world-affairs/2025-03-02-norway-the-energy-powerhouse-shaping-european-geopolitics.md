Norway, known for its breathtaking landscapes and majestic fjords, is much more than just a captivating tourist destination. The country has established itself as a global energy powerhouse, being the world’s largest producer of hydropower and one of the top exporters of oil and natural gas. With a visionary strategy, Norway has successfully balanced attracting foreign investments while maintaining national control over its resources, ensuring economic prosperity and geopolitical influence. In this video, we explore how Norway has transformed its natural wealth into strategic power, shaping its role in NATO and the European economy.

These **hydropower stations** today are powerful enough to generate an overwhelming **95% of Norway's total electricity demand**. As of **2024, Norway is the world's largest producer of hydropower**, generating more than **half of the total U.S. hydropower output** but for a population just **1/67th the size of the United States**.

The **harnessing of Norway’s hydropower potential in the early 20th century** provided the country with its first major **economic boom**, enabling rapid industrialization and electrification. By **1920, Norway was consuming more electricity per capita than any other country in the world**, and more than **two-thirds of its population had access to electricity**, double the rate in the U.S. at the time.

This **huge energy surplus** for such a small population allowed Norway to develop **highly energy-intensive industries**, specializing in **refined aluminum production** under **Norsk Hydro**, which is now **one of the largest aluminum companies in the world**. Norsk Hydro continues to source **the majority of its energy** from Norway’s hydropower, and **34% of the company is publicly owned by the Norwegian state**. This has made Norway **Europe’s largest aluminum producer and the eighth-largest in the world**, further positioning it as **the top aluminum supplier to the European Union**.

However, just as **hydropower unlocked Norway’s early potential**, the industry soon became **dominated by large foreign companies** investing in and building the country's dams. To **regain control over its own resources**, Norway avoided a **quick nationalization** that could have scared off investors and instead took a **long-term strategic approach**—a method that would later **influence its oil and gas policies**.

Norway implemented laws requiring **foreign investors in hydropower projects** to **hand over all power plants and mines to the Norwegian government without compensation after 60 to 80 years of operation**. This **ensured a return on investment** for foreign companies while guaranteeing that, in the long run, **Norway would maintain control over its key resources**. Today, **over 90% of Norway’s hydropower capacity is state-owned**.

Thanks to this strategic approach, Norway was already a **wealthy, industrialized, democratic, and low-corruption country** when interest in its **offshore oil and gas reserves** emerged in the **late 1950s**. At the time, **few believed there were any oil or gas resources** to be found on Norway’s continental shelf in the **North Sea**, a region that had **only recently been submerged at the end of the last Ice Age, a few thousand years ago**.

In **1958**, the **Norwegian Geological Survey** wrote a letter confidently stating that there was **no possibility of finding oil or gas** in Norway’s maritime territory. However, in **1959**, a **huge natural gas field was discovered in the Netherlands**, sparking **massive interest**. The **Groningen gas field** became **the 11th largest natural gas field in the world** and **the largest ever discovered in Europe**, making the Netherlands a major gas producer and boosting curiosity about further resources in the **North Sea**.

By **1962**, **Phillips Petroleum**, an American oil giant, became the **first company to apply for exploration rights** in Norwegian waters. However, there was a **problem**—Norway’s **maritime borders were still undefined**.

To solve this, **in 1965, the UK and Norway agreed** to divide the **North Sea** based on the **median line principle**, which **marked the halfway point between their land territories**. Decades later, this decision would **greatly benefit Norway** as it considered **thousands of Norwegian islands**, shifting the boundary **a few kilometers westward**. As a result, when the **massive Statfjord oil field was discovered in the 1970s**, only **15% of it was in British waters**, while **85% belonged to Norway**, securing **billions of dollars in extra oil revenue** for the Norwegians.

Shortly after securing its maritime borders, **Norway granted exploration licenses to major foreign oil companies**. The **first oil discovery came in 1967** at the **Balder field**, but at the time, it was **not considered economically viable**. It wasn’t until the **1990s—three decades later—that Balder finally went into operation**.

Then, **just before Christmas in 1969**, **Phillips Petroleum made a historic discovery**—the **Ekofisk field**, one of the **largest offshore oil fields ever found**. Production began in **1971**, and in the following years, **more major oil and gas discoveries** were made across the Norwegian continental shelf, including **Statfjord, Oseberg, Gullfaks, and Troll**.

The **Troll field**, discovered in **1996**, gained **global attention** when Norway **transported the massive Troll A oil platform from the mainland to its location in the North Sea**. To this day, this remains **the largest and heaviest structure ever moved by humans** from one part of the Earth’s surface to another.

This story demonstrates **how Norway strategically secured control over its natural resources, first with hydropower and later with oil and gas**, transforming itself into **one of the wealthiest and most geopolitically influential countries in the world**.
