## Jack Matlock’s Warning: Western Leaders as Ideological War Criminals Amid Global Crises

This article stems from a compelling interview conducted on September 1, 2025, between Professor Glenn Diesen, a noted scholar of international relations, and veteran diplomat Jack Matlock, whose tenure as U.S. Ambassador to the Soviet Union from 1987 to 1991 positioned him at the heart of Cold War diplomacy. Their conversation, marked by Diesen’s incisive questions and Matlock’s reflective insights, delves into the collapse of post-Cold War peace and the rise of what Matlock describes as "ideological war criminals" among Western leaders, offering a rare glimpse into the mind of a key figure who helped shape a pivotal era.

In a world increasingly defined by conflict and division, the voice of Jack F. Matlock Jr., a seasoned U.S. diplomat who served as ambassador to the Soviet Union from 1987 to 1991, cuts through the noise with a sobering accusation. Matlock, instrumental in negotiating the end of the Cold War alongside Presidents Reagan and Bush, recently labeled Western leaders "ideological war criminals" for abandoning the diplomatic principles that once brought peace to a fractured globe. His remarks, delivered in a candid interview on September 1, 2025, reflect a deep frustration with a shift from pragmatic engagement to what he sees as reckless militarism and ideological zeal. For Matlock, the promise of a "common European home" envisioned by Mikhail Gorbachev—a vision once embraced by Reagan, Bush, and even Margaret Thatcher—has been betrayed by NATO’s eastward expansion and a failure to understand Russia’s security concerns. As someone who witnessed the Cold War’s end firsthand, his perspective invites us to reflect on how far we’ve strayed from those hard-won lessons and what it means for humanity today.

Matlock’s career offers a unique lens on this shift. During his tenure, he played a key role in the Malta Summit of December 1989, where Bush and Gorbachev formally declared the Cold War over and pledged mutual non-interference in Eastern Europe’s political evolution. Gorbachev’s rejection of Marxist-Leninist foreign policy, articulated in his December 1988 UN speech, and his commitment to democratization were seen as genuine by U.S. leadership, despite skepticism from the CIA. Matlock recalls that the U.S. aimed to support a reformed Soviet Union, not its dissolution, as evidenced by Bush’s “Chicken Kiev” speech in August 1991, where he urged Ukrainians to avoid “suicidal nationalism” and support Gorbachev’s Union Treaty. Yet, the Soviet collapse in December 1991 shifted narratives, with many claiming it marked the Cold War’s true end, overshadowing the earlier diplomatic breakthrough.

The diplomat’s critique centers on how this peace unraveled. He argues that NATO’s expansion into former Warsaw Pact countries, beginning under President Clinton, violated the spirit of Malta’s understandings. Initially, the Partnership for Peace was proposed as an inclusive alternative, approved by Russian President Boris Yeltsin, but it morphed into a stepping stone for NATO membership for nations like Poland, Hungary, and the Czech Republic. Matlock points out that Russia tolerated this expansion, even to the Baltic states, provided no foreign bases were established—a condition later ignored with U.S. military presence in the region. This shift, he believes, transformed NATO from a defensive alliance into an offensive one, provoking Russia at a time when its military was in disarray and posed no threat.

The consequences of this policy shift are stark in Matlock’s view. He draws a direct line from NATO’s 1999 intervention in Serbia—conducted without UN approval—to Russia’s annexation of Crimea in 2014, citing the Helsinki Final Act’s principle that border changes require mutual consent. The U.S. recognition of Kosovo’s independence without Serbia’s approval set a precedent Russia later mirrored, yet Western leaders condemned Putin’s actions while overlooking their own contradictions. Matlock extends this critique to the 2003 Iraq invasion, which he labels a “massive” and unprovoked act based on fabricated evidence, surpassing in scale Russia’s actions in Ukraine. For him, these moves reflect a broader failure to empathize with others’ perspectives—a skill he credits to Reagan and Bush, who engaged Soviet leaders with respect rather than insults.

This lack of empathy, Matlock argues, has fueled today’s crises. The West’s support for radical nationalists in Ukraine, including groups like the Azov Battalion, and its refusal to address Russian security concerns—such as the placement of NATO bases or the treatment of Russian-speaking minorities—have escalated tensions. He sees the demonization of Putin as a reaction to Western provocations, noting Russia’s 1990s chaos—crime, unpaid pensions, and oligarchic plunder—as a backdrop that shaped its current stance. The imposition of economic sanctions, often over internal Russian matters, and the denial of most-favored-nation trade status (granted to China but not Russia) further entrenched this pariah status.

Matlock’s most provocative claim is that Western leaders, by leading their nations into wars not directly defensive—Serbia, Iraq, Ukraine, and indirectly Gaza—qualify as war criminals. He contrasts this with his generation’s success in ending the Cold War, attributing today’s militarization to the military-industrial complex’s influence and a misguided “liberal world order” akin to the Soviet’s socialist vision. This ideology, he argues, justifies imperialism under the guise of cultural superiority, ignoring history’s diversity and the equal value of all human lives—whether lost in Europe or elsewhere.

The diplomat’s concerns extend beyond geopolitics to global challenges. He laments the West’s focus on arming conflicts while climate change, failed states, and mass migration strain societies. Europe’s increased defense spending, amid internal social issues, and the U.S.’s potential war with China reflect a loss of direction. Matlock sees hope in Trump’s acknowledgment of territorial compromises to end the Ukraine war, though he remains pessimistic about Europe’s willingness to negotiate.

Media plays a villainous role in this narrative. Matlock accuses outlets in the U.S. and Western Europe of peddling a “false ideology,” misrepresenting facts to support these policies. This propaganda, he suggests, blinds the public to the roots of violence and the need for diplomacy over destruction.

As a human story, Matlock’s words resonate with anyone who values peace over power. His journey from Cold War negotiator to critic reflects a personal disillusionment with leaders who’ve forgotten the human cost of their decisions. The Malta Summit’s handshake feels distant as we face a world where dialogue is drowned by drones. His call to rethink our approach—putting ourselves in others’ shoes—offers a path back to sanity, though the road is steep.

This isn’t just history; it’s a wake-up call. Matlock’s insights, rooted in decades of experience, challenge us to demand accountability from our leaders and restore the diplomacy that once saved us. Whether his warnings spur change or fade into the noise of war, the choice lies with us.
