## Broken NATO: Trump’s Confusion Fuels War on Russia, Europe Fractures

In the swirling fog of global geopolitics, where alliances once thought unbreakable now teeter on the brink, a recent conversation between Norwegian professor Glenn Diesen and retired U.S. Army Colonel Douglas Macgregor cuts through the noise like a sharpened bayonet. Titled "Broken NATO Escalates War on Russia," their discussion lays bare the contradictions plaguing Western strategy, from Donald Trump's bewildering policy pirouettes to the fissures cracking Europe's unity. Macgregor, a combat veteran, prolific author on military strategy, and former senior advisor to the U.S. Secretary of Defense, doesn't mince words: the West is hurtling toward defeat not through overwhelming Russian might, but through its own hubris and disarray.

The interview, aired just days ago, arrives at a precarious moment. With the current date marking November 1, 2025, the echoes of October's upheavals still reverberate. Mysterious explosions rocked oil refineries in Hungary, Romania, and Slovakia—facilities processing Russian crude—sparking whispers of sabotage amid escalating intra-European animosities. Poland's government has openly lambasted Hungary and Slovakia for refusing to join the anti-Russian "crusade," while Slovenia became the first EU nation to ban Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu. These aren't mere diplomatic spats; they're symptoms of a continent fragmenting under the weight of a war it can't win, a war NATO seems determined to prolong at any cost.

Macgregor begins by dissecting Trump's approach to Ukraine, a saga that has devolved from campaign trail bravado to a "ridiculous reality show." During his bid for the presidency, Trump decried NATO's eastward creep as folly, even floating the idea of no security guarantees for Ukraine and territorial concessions to Russia. By February, he pivoted to an unconditional ceasefire, teasing a summit with Vladimir Putin in Alaska. Yet, as Diesen notes, Trump swiftly reverted to ceasefire rhetoric without addressing root causes, and whispers of a Budapest meeting have evaporated. "He's the president of perpetual confusion," Macgregor quips, highlighting the former president's oscillation between poles on every issue. This isn't 4D chess; it's a leader chasing quick wins, like a Nobel Peace Prize photo-op, rather than forging enduring peace.

At its core, Macgregor argues, a ceasefire is no panacea—it's temporary, akin to the Korean armistice that has limped along for decades. What Europe craves, he insists, is a "strategic arrangement with Russia that codifies Russia's relationship with the rest of Europe." Such a deal would etch a legacy of stability, but Trump shows no appetite for it. Instead, he's fixated on superficial victories: "I met with President Putin. I charmed him. He made a deal. Peace is breaking out." Absent that ego-stroking narrative, disinterest sets in. This mindset blinds him to Russia's next moves. Macgregor points to fresh Russian bridgeheads across the Dnipro River in Kherson, predicting advances southward to reclaim Odessa—a historically Russian city, Russian-speaking until Ukraine's 2014 purges drove out its population. Kharkov looms similarly, with ongoing pushes near Lyman and Sloviansk signaling Putin's war council has abandoned restraint.

Russia's calculus has shifted, Macgregor explains. After enduring provocations, including British Storm Shadow missiles striking a chemical plant in Bryansk and alleged FSB reports implicating London in attacks on Russia's nuclear deterrent, Moscow is done playing defense. Power grids across Ukraine flicker out as Russian forces intensify operations, turning off lights in a calculated bid to hasten collapse. Yet, even as escalation mounts, Macgregor detects Russian reluctance for total conquest. "They'd actually like to see this war end," he says. Moscow has no desire to govern western Ukraine's hostile populace; its aim is securing Russian-speakers who wish to rejoin the fold. The West's persistent myth—that Putin dreams of conquering Europe—ignores this nuance, peddling fears that demand universal mobilization Russia could enact but prefers to avoid.

This delusion sustains NATO's escalatory spiral, a desperate bid to evade defeat. Trump briefly flirted with dispatching Tomahawk missiles—potentially nuclear-armed, operated by American contractors—eliciting holiday-like glee in European capitals. But he reconsidered, Macgregor notes, grasping the perils: U.S. personnel west of the Dnieper launching strikes into Russia invites direct war. Resource constraints sealed the retreat; America's munitions stockpiles, depleted by Ukraine aid, can't sustain endless largesse. "Our resources are not limitless," Trump admitted in a rare lucid moment, echoing Macgregor's warnings. The British, neck-deep via MI6 and military embeds, may face reprisals—Russia knows the UK's depleted forces could muster only 20,000-30,000 troops, bankrupt and overstretched. France fares no better, its army tethered domestically to quell volatility.

Europe's internal rot amplifies these vulnerabilities. Macgregor evokes a "civil war inside the European Union and NATO," spotlighting the refinery blasts on October 20. Hungary's MOL Danube facility, Romania's Petrotel-Lukoil in Ploiesti, and Slovakia's installations—all tied to Russian oil—erupted in flames within hours, vanishing from media like Nord Stream's ghosts. Poland's rhetoric radicalizes the divide: Warsaw deems it "legitimate" to target occupiers' energy infrastructure, even if it starves fellow Europeans, and refuses to extradite Nord Stream suspects to Germany, shaming Berlin for the pipeline's existence. Hungary's Viktor Orban fires back, decrying Poland as a poor ally for warmongering. This feud, between historic partners, portends broader disintegration. Slovenia's Netanyahu ban underscores revulsion at entanglements in Gaza, where Trump's "ceasefire" masks real estate grabs by Kushner and Witkoff—envisioning an "Israeli Disney World" on half the Strip.

Deeper still, Macgregor traces Europe's malaise to "late empire behavior," channeling Paul Kennedy's *The Rise and Fall of the Great Powers*. As internal decay festers—de-industrialization, ballooning debt, cultural fragmentation, demographic decline, eroded free speech, and lost technological sovereignty—leaders beat war drums against Russia to distract. Seizing Russian assets and Chinese firms paints the EU as "rogue," eroding global relevance. A French general Diesen consulted echoes this: Europe's woes are domestic, yet fixation on Moscow blames everything on the bear. Politicians, Macgregor laments, should yield to psychologists; the obsession defies logic.

America's gaze wanders southward, to Venezuela, where Trump's regime-change zealotry baffles. Campaigning against such follies, he now demands Maduro's ouster, sinking boats on drug pretexts (despite cocaine hailing from Colombia and Ecuador, fentanyl from China via Mexico). Oil, gas, emeralds, and gold lure, but Macgregor sees Vietnam 1965 redux: fabricated provocations, like a Gulf of Tonkin hoax, to justify invasion. CIA whispers urge a Venezuelan missile "incident" for cover. U.S. public splits: the "bombs away club" (a third) cheers displays of might; most others question the why, eyeing refugee floods northward. Even Nobel laureate Maria Corina Machado, Trump's TV ally, peddles privatization fantasies, a "Venezuelan Quisling" in Diesen's eyes.

Gaza's "peace" crumbles under similar illusions, with Iran's shadow lengthening. Trump's base—once "America First"—watches war hawks like Lindsey Graham thrive, while he torpedoes critics Rand Paul and Thomas Massie for decrying Israeli lobby excesses and Venezuelan illegality. Aligning with Graham, prisoner to defense and Zionist lobbies, spells "bodily evil" for America, Macgregor warns—siphoning souls into insecurity and decline.

Peering ahead, Macgregor foresees NATO's demise, as predicted in his 2022 National Interest interview. Trump's abdication—sanctions sans leadership—pours oil on flames, ceding Europe's fate. Russians, long-term strategists, press on undeterred; short-term Western impulses guarantee friction. Limits to U.S. power, unadmitted, curb rashness, but Europe's risk appetite surges as American backup wanes. Russia's Oreshnik arsenal buildup alarms; the cost of inaction may soon eclipse retaliation.

In this maelstrom, hope flickers dimly. Macgregor, no teetotaler, quotes a friend's grim counsel: "Drink heavily." Yet, as Diesen concurs, escalation inches Europe toward direct war, restraint's calculus tipping. Such is the world—unpredictable, unforgiving. But acknowledging fractures, as Macgregor does, is the first step toward mending, or at least surviving, the shatter.
