## New Tariff Narrative: China Gains Allies, Taiwan Shifts, and Shortages Loom in 2025

Pascal Lottaz of *Neutrality Studies* hosted a thought-provoking interview with political analyst Arnaud Bertrand, streamed on YouTube under the title *"New Tarif Narrative, China Winning Friends, US Shortages Coming, Taiwan Changing."* The discussion, set against the backdrop of Russia’s Victory Day celebrations on May 9, 2025, explored the unraveling of U.S. economic dominance through its aggressive tariff policies, China’s growing global alliances, the looming threat of U.S. consumer shortages, and a notable shift in Taiwanese public sentiment.

Bertrand, a prominent voice on X with a reputation for insightful analysis on China and global politics, offered a scathing critique of the U.S.’s “spiral of economic and moral bankruptcy,” highlighting how its actions are inadvertently strengthening China’s position while alienating allies and risking domestic fallout.

## The U.S. Tariff Strategy: A Self-Inflicted Wound

Bertrand opened by dissecting the U.S.’s tariff policy under President Donald Trump, initiated with the “Liberation Day” tariffs on April 2, 2025, which imposed a 10% universal rate and higher rates for specific countries, such as 54% on China. He expressed bewilderment at the lack of a coherent strategy, noting conflicting justifications from the Trump administration: one day, tariffs are about securing trade deals; the next, they aim to revive U.S. manufacturing or raise revenue.

“All of those are mutually exclusive,” Bertrand argued, pointing out that manufacturers won’t invest in the U.S. if tariffs are temporary, undermining the revival narrative. He cited Scott Bessent’s earlier claim, reported by *Bloomberg*, that tariffs would encircle China by pressuring its neighbors like Japan and South Korea to reduce trade with Beijing. However, the opposite occurred: a joint statement from finance ministers and central bankers of Japan, South Korea, China, and ASEAN countries, issued during a meeting in Italy, vowed to make East and Southeast Asia “more resilient” against U.S.-induced trade uncertainty, per *Reuters*.

This regional solidarity, Bertrand argued, reflects a broader counter-reaction to U.S. policies. Japan, typically a compliant ally, issued unusually strong rebukes, recalling the 1980s when it faced similar U.S. trade pressures, as noted by Lottaz. Vietnam, heavily reliant on U.S. exports, was among the top five countries affected by the tariffs, prompting a strategic rethink to reduce dependency, while Malaysia explicitly avoids rerouting Chinese goods to the U.S. to limit exposure, according to Bertrand’s logistics industry source. The U.S.’s inconsistent approach—imposing tariffs even on trade surplus partners—further erodes its credibility, with Bertrand suggesting it may be a “big mistake” the administration cannot admit, instead rationalizing it post hoc.

## China’s Ascendant Influence in Asia and Beyond

The U.S.’s missteps have accelerated China’s diplomatic and economic gains, particularly in Asia. Bertrand highlighted recent developments: Malaysia’s prime minister issued unprecedentedly pro-China statements during Xi Jinping’s visit two weeks prior, while Vietnam acknowledged China’s role in the Vietnam War, with 300,000 PLA soldiers aiding their fight against the U.S., a historical taboo broken after decades of denial. Vietnam’s inclusion of Chinese troops in a recent military parade further signaled warming ties, despite a 1979 border conflict. These shifts contrast sharply with the U.S.’s approach, as China offers stability—through consistent five-year plans and a non-coercive stance—while Trump’s unpredictability, driven by erratic Truth Social posts, creates chaos, Bertrand noted.

Japan, China’s largest trading partner, faces pressure to choose between the U.S. and China but is increasingly drawn to the latter’s reliability. Lottaz pointed to Japan’s pragmatic trade ties with China, intensified after Trump’s 2017 TPP withdrawal, which Japan revived as the CPTPP, including frameworks involving China like the RCEP. Bertrand emphasized that China never forces nations to choose sides, unlike the U.S., fostering a more flexible partnership. This dynamic extends to Southeast Asia, where countries like Malaysia and Indonesia are boosting intra-regional trade and ties with Europe and Africa to “de-risk” from the U.S. market, as confirmed by Bertrand’s logistics contact, who reported a 40-60% drop in China-U.S. trade without a corresponding rise in U.S. imports from elsewhere.

## U.S. Consumer Shortages: An Imminent Reality

The interview turned to the domestic fallout of U.S. tariffs, with Bertrand predicting “serious consumer goods shortages” in the near future. He noted that the 40-60% drop in U.S.-China trade, coupled with a generalized decline in global imports, will soon manifest on American shelves, potentially within weeks of Liberation Day’s impact. “It takes about one month for cargo ships to arrive,” he explained, forecasting empty shelves by late May or early June 2025.

Lottaz shared his experience of empty shelves in Japan after natural disasters, underscoring the psychological impact, while Bertrand referenced Trump’s own acknowledgment of shortages, joking that children might get “two dolls instead of 30” for Christmas, per a *CNN* report. This admission, coupled with the lack of exemptions for sectors like toys, signals a broader erosion of U.S. soft power, as Lottaz argued, noting that such policies dismantle the appeal of American consumer culture.

## Taiwan’s Changing Perspective Amid U.S. Pressure

A significant shift in Taiwanese sentiment emerged as a key topic, with Bertrand highlighting a large protest in Taipei on April 26, 2025, against the pro-independence, U.S.-aligned government of William Lai. The protest, organized by figures like former lawmaker Joanna Lei, received little Western media coverage despite the presence of outlets like CNN and BBC, a silence Lei criticized on Lottaz’s show.

Bertrand noted that Taiwanese perspectives are evolving, particularly after China’s response to a recent terrorist attack—widely admired even by PRC-skeptical Taiwanese—contrasted with the Lai government’s “pathetic” rush to negotiate with the U.S. post-tariffs. The U.S.’s pressure on TSMC to relocate plants, perceived as exploitative, has further fueled resentment, with Bertrand observing a “rapid change in mindsets” on X, where Taiwanese users increasingly favor pragmatic engagement with China over blind trust in the U.S.

## The U.S. Empire in Decline: A Moral and Economic Reckoning

Bertrand framed the U.S.’s tariff saga as a symptom of imperial decline, echoing Lottaz’s opening remark: “You know you are nearing the end of empire when the chief extortionists start asking for ever more money for ever less return.” He likened U.S. policy to a failing business model, “sucking the world so dry” that global backlash is inevitable, as seen in Asia’s unified resistance.

This extends to Ukraine, where the U.S. uses Ukrainians as an “implementation partner” to weaken Russia, demanding payment for their sacrifice—a dynamic Lottaz compared to the Afghan Mujahideen in the 1980s. Bertrand criticized Trump’s handling of Ukraine, noting his initial peace rhetoric but “amateurish” execution, such as sending an unqualified negotiator, Steve Witkoff, to Moscow, alienating Europeans and ensuring failure.

The U.S.’s broader imperial structures, from the military-industrial complex to the dollar’s reserve status, remain untouched, Lottaz argued, citing advisor Steve Miran’s claim that the world must “pay” for the dollar’s “burden,” despite its “exorbitant privilege,” as former French President Valéry Giscard d’Estaing called it.

Bertrand found this narrative “bizarre,” noting that the dollar allows the U.S. to import goods essentially for free, printing currency without liquidity crises, yet framing it as a burden fuels tariff justifications. He compared Trump’s approach to Khrushchev’s break from Stalin and Mao’s Cultural Revolution, suggesting Trump seeks a radical ideological shift—evident in his use of outsiders like Elon Musk and Robert F. Kennedy Jr.—but delivers chaos rather than progress, marking a “before and after” for America.

## A New Global Order on the Horizon

Arnaud Bertrand’s interview with Pascal Lottaz painted a stark picture of U.S. decline, driven by incoherent tariff policies that alienate allies, empower China, and risk domestic shortages. China’s stable, multilateral approach contrasts sharply with Trump’s volatility, winning friends in Asia and beyond, while Taiwan’s shifting mindset signals growing skepticism of U.S. reliability. As the U.S. grapples with its imperial overreach—demanding more from the world while offering less—Bertrand foresees a transformative moment, where global economic alignments may permanently shift, leaving the U.S. to confront the consequences of its own hubris in a rapidly changing world order.
