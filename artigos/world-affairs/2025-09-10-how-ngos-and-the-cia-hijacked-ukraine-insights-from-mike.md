## Unveiling the Hidden Mechanisms: Intelligence Agencies, Non-Governmental Organizations, and the Takeover of Ukraine's Civil Society

In the ever-shifting landscape of global politics, few topics stir as much intrigue and controversy as the shadowy interplay between intelligence agencies, non-governmental organizations (NGOs), and foreign policy agendas. On September 10, 2025, a compelling interview between Professor Glenn Diesen and Mike Benz, a former Deputy Assistant Secretary at the U.S. State Department and current Executive Director of the Foundation for Freedom Online, shed light on this very phenomenon. Titled "How NGOs and the CIA Hijacked Ukraine," the discussion delves into how ostensibly independent NGOs have been weaponized to influence and control civil societies abroad, with Ukraine serving as a prime example. Benz, who has previously appeared on platforms hosted by Tucker Carlson, Joe Rogan, and Russell Brand, argues that what appears as organic civil activism is often a carefully orchestrated extension of statecraft, particularly by the U.S. intelligence community.

The conversation begins with a fundamental premise: a vibrant civil society is essential for democracy, but its organization can be genuine or artificially engineered. Benz traces the roots of this practice back to World War II, when the Office of Strategic Services (OSS), the precursor to the CIA, forged alliances with European civil groups like churches, unions, and media to combat Axis powers. Post-war, as the Cold War dawned, these tactics transitioned from hot conflict to ideological warfare. The CIA, established in 1947, maintained partnerships with media, labor unions, universities, and cultural institutions to counter Soviet influence across Europe, Africa, Latin America, and Asia. This wasn't mere diplomacy; it was a national security imperative to co-opt civil society in geostrategically vital regions.

A pivotal shift occurred in the 1980s with the creation of the National Endowment for Democracy (NED) in 1983. Benz describes NED as a "CIA front" designed to provide plausible deniability after scandals in the 1960s and 1970s exposed CIA funding of domestic groups, leading to congressional oversight like the Church Committee hearings. Under President Jimmy Carter, the CIA faced significant cuts, including the firing of 30% of its operations division. Ronald Reagan's administration, seeking to restore robust foreign intervention without direct CIA involvement, established NED through congressional funding. Its founder, Carl Gershman, openly admitted in a 1986 Washington Post interview that NED was created to fund groups the CIA once supported covertly, avoiding blowback.

NED's structure is ingeniously bipartisan, ensuring broad political buy-in. It comprises four core institutes: the International Republican Institute (IRI), National Democratic Institute (NDI), Center for International Private Enterprise (CIPE, tied to the Chamber of Commerce), and the Solidarity Center (linked to unions like the AFL-CIO). This setup co-opts both labor and business, left and right, while funding media through the Center for International Media Assistance (CIMA). Benz emphasizes that NED funds every facet of civil society—media, unions, arts, culture, and even censorship efforts to combat "misinformation." Since its inception, NED has supported over 2,000 grants annually in more than 100 countries, with Ukraine as a top priority since 1989. By 2022, NED had partnered with Ukrainian civil groups, media, and human rights defenders, positioning itself as a staunch ally amid Russia's invasion.

The interview pivots to Ukraine, where Gershman once called it the "biggest prize" in the West-Russia geopolitical struggle. Benz details NED's involvement from the 1990s, including the 2004 Orange Revolution and the 2014 Maidan uprising. He references Victoria Nuland's 2013 speech, where she boasted of $5 billion in U.S. aid to Ukrainian civil society, delivered before ExxonMobil and Chevron banners—companies that signed multibillion-dollar deals with Naftogaz, Ukraine's state gas firm. This aid, Benz argues, aimed to privatize Ukraine's energy sector, diverting profits from Russian Gazprom to Western firms, including those linked to George Soros.

Soros emerges as a central figure in Benz's narrative. Through his Open Society Foundations, Soros has invested over $181 million in Ukrainian initiatives since the 1990s, supporting nearly 17,000 projects. Benz portrays Soros not as a philanthropist but as a hedge fund magnate leveraging NGOs for profit. Soros's foundations work hand-in-glove with U.S. agencies, influencing policy to benefit his investments, particularly in energy privatization. A 2014 CNN interview saw Soros discussing his foundation's role in Ukraine pre-independence, emphasizing support for reforms that aligned with Western interests. Critics, including Ukrainian officials, have accused Soros-backed groups of meddling, with a 2019 probe into one such organization halted under U.S. pressure.

Post-2014, after the Maidan revolution ousted President Viktor Yanukovych, NGOs solidified control. Benz highlights how 85-90% of Ukrainian media relies on Western funding, including from USAID and NED. Organizations like the Organized Crime and Corruption Reporting Project (OCCRP) and Kyiv Independent receive U.S. dollars, influencing narratives and even digging dirt on figures like Rudy Giuliani, contributing to U.S. domestic politics. Unions, education, and arts are similarly co-opted; for instance, U.S. teachers' unions collaborate with Ukrainian counterparts to purge Russian influences from curricula. Even pensions for Ukrainian public workers have been funded by USAID, ensuring loyalty.

A watershed moment came in 2019 with Volodymyr Zelenskyy's election on a peace platform, garnering 73% of the vote for Minsk Accord implementation and reconciliation with Donbas and Russia. However, within a month, the Ukraine Crisis Media Center (UCMC)—funded by USAID, NATO, and CIA-linked contractors like Chemonics—issued a "red lines" memo. Signed by 70 NGOs and media outlets, it outlined prohibitions: no referendums on Russia negotiations, no direct talks without Western partners, no compromises with Russia, and mandatory pursuit of EU/NATO membership. Crossing these would lead to "political instability," code for another color revolution. Benz interprets this as U.S.-led entities holding a gun to Zelenskyy's head, blocking democratic mandates.

This control extends beyond Ukraine. Benz notes similar tactics in Belarus (2020 protests), Georgia (attempted regime change), and India (criticism of Soros by officials). Domestically, these forces targeted Trump, using Russiagate—partly fueled by UCMC-linked figures like David Kramer—to undermine his détente with Russia. Benz warns that U.S. foreign policy now serves elite interests over citizens, calling for restructuring to end this "hijacking."

The interview underscores a broader crisis: NGOs, meant to foster democracy, often mask imperial ambitions. In Ukraine, this has perpetuated conflict, economic exploitation, and suppressed sovereignty. As Diesen notes, despite ample documentation on NED-CIA ties and color revolutions, the discourse remains muted. Benz's revelations challenge us to question the "humanitarian" facade of foreign aid and its true beneficiaries.

Yet, hope flickers. Public awareness, fueled by voices like Benz's, could pressure reforms. Ukraine's story is a cautionary tale: when civil society is captured, democracy suffers. As global tensions rise, understanding these mechanisms is crucial for reclaiming authentic self-determination.
