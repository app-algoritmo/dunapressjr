## 2025 Europe Subjugated: Sahra Wagenknecht on War Propaganda and U.S. Influence

On August 31, 2025, as Europe grapples with an identity crisis, a interview between Professor Glenn Diesen and Sahra Wagenknecht, a towering figure in German politics and former member of the Bundestag and European Parliament, shed light on a continent teetering on the edge of self-destruction. Wagenknecht, known for her sharp critiques of neoliberalism and globalization, painted a grim picture of a Europe subjugated to U.S. interests, propagandized into war fervor, and economically crippled by misguided policies. As a mother watching her children inherit a world of conflict, her words carry a personal weight, urging us to reconsider the path Europe has taken since the hopeful days of the 1990s. Her call for a peace rally on September 13, 2025, at Berlin’s Brandenburg Gate, signals a desperate plea to reclaim sovereignty and peace.

Wagenknecht traces Europe’s economic woes to a series of self-inflicted wounds. The Maastricht Treaty and subsequent EU decisions handcuffed industrial policy, stifling innovation and technological advancement. The EU’s climate policy, while environmentally noble, has driven industries away with burdensome regulations, a problem exacerbated by the 2022 economic sanctions against Russia. Germany, heavily reliant on cheap Russian gas, severed this lifeline, replacing it with expensive, lower-quality American fracking gas under a deal brokered by EU Commission President Ursula von der Leyen. This “subjugation,” as Wagenknecht calls it, allows duty-free U.S. goods into Europe while imposing 15% tariffs on European exports, undermining competitiveness and costing jobs.

This economic dependence reflects a broader loss of strategic autonomy. Once a goal of the EU, independence has morphed into vassalage, with European politicians—especially in Eastern Europe and increasingly the West—bowing to Washington’s directives. Wagenknecht points to networks like the “Young Global Leaders” program, suggesting U.S. influence over leaders, a claim dismissed as conspiracy by some but plausible given historical interference. Contrastingly, Hungary’s Viktor Orbán stands out, defying U.S. pressure by buying Russian oil, a rare act of national interest. Past German chancellors like Willy Brandt and Helmut Schmidt resisted Washington, but today’s leaders, including Friedrich Merz, lack such backbone.

Merz’s leadership amplifies this crisis. His ambition to make Germany Europe’s leading military power, coupled with a focus on military spending over infrastructure, misaligns with economic needs. Germany’s decaying roads, bridges, and rail networks, a housing shortage of 800,000 apartments, and a struggling education system cry out for investment, yet a rigid debt brake prevents it. Merz, influenced by his BlackRock tenure, prioritizes arms industries like Rheinmetall, benefiting corporate giants while offering little to ordinary citizens. The irony deepens as Germany buys American weapons, undermining any pretense of military Keynesianism.

Wagenknecht decries the media’s role in this descent. German mainstream press, echoing war calls, ignores Europe’s digital dependency on the U.S. and amplifies a narrative of Russian threat. Daily headlines warn of Putin’s imminent invasion, reviving 20th-century enemy images, a tactic she likens to the enthusiasm that fueled World War I. This propaganda masks domestic woes—growing poverty, low wages, and social division fueled by immigration—distracting from leaders’ failures. Merz’s unpopularity, despite broken promises, persists without street protests, a silence Wagenknecht aims to break with the September 13 rally, joined by artists like Peter Maffay and cabaret star Dieter Hallervorden.

The shift to war fervor is alarming. Once cautious, Europe now outspends the U.S. on Ukraine, with leaders like Macron and Starmer obsessing over foreign policy. This contrasts with Trump’s push for settlement, driven by the war’s cost and focus on China. Wagenknecht recalls how Europe once balanced U.S. NATO expansion, as with Merkel and Hollande during the Minsk agreement, but now embraces a “coalition of the willing to go to war.” Sending troops to Ukraine would trigger Russian retaliation, risking nuclear conflict that could render Europe uninhabitable.

This militarization stems from a need for an external enemy. Russia’s exclusion from a post-Cold War Europe, as George Kennan lamented, revived bloc politics, alienating a nation with vast territory, population, and military might. Wagenknecht echoes Kennan’s regret over missed opportunities for a “great European house” from Lisbon to Vladivostok, a vision thwarted by U.S. fears of a Russo-European power challenging its dominance. Russia’s pivot to Asia, with pipelines and trade shifting eastward, underscores this loss, though Putin’s pro-European leanings offer hope.

Wagenknecht envisions a better path. Decentralizing the EU, returning powers to nation-states, would restore democracy, eroded by unelected figures like von der Leyen and lobby-influenced European Parliament. Cooperation on technology and infrastructure—like digital networks or chip industries—should replace centralized bureaucracy, which often serves financial lobbies via the green taxonomy. Without this, she warns, Europe risks collapse, though fragmentation into small states wouldn’t suffice in today’s interconnected world.

As a European, I feel the weight of Wagenknecht’s words. The nostalgia for Brandt’s defiance clashes with today’s subjugation, where war enthusiasm drowns out peace. Her rally offers hope, a chance to reclaim a Europe that prioritizes its people over proxy conflicts. The path forward lies in sovereignty, cooperation with Russia, and a rejection of militarized illusions—before it’s too late.
