Recent results from the Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS) have raised significant concerns among Norwegian education officials. The study indicates a slight decline in mathematics and science performance among 5th-grade students, while 9th-grade students' results remain stable compared to the 2019 assessment. Notably, 43% of 9th graders are performing at the lowest level in science, and 37% in mathematics, positioning Norway closer to the international average.

Morten Rosenkvist, Director of the Norwegian Directorate for Education and Training, expressed concern: "We cannot be satisfied with 43% of ninth graders performing at the lowest level in science."

Erna Solberg, leader of the Conservative Party (Høyre), described the situation as a "realfagskatastrofe" (STEM catastrophe) for Norwegian schools. She emphasized that nearly half of Norwegian ninth graders are at the lowest proficiency level in science, which could impact the nation's future capabilities.

The TIMSS 2023 study involved 64 countries, with Norway participating through 5,301 5th-grade and 6,324 9th-grade students. Norwegian 5th graders performed on par with their Swedish and Finnish peers in mathematics and matched Swedish students in science, while Danish students lagged in both subjects. However, by 9th grade, Norwegian students fell behind both Swedish and Finnish students in science and mathematics. The Directorate attributes this to fewer instructional hours in science compared to neighboring countries.

In response, Høyre proposes adding an extra weekly science lesson in lower secondary schools to address these deficiencies.

Contrary to trends in Sweden and Denmark, where boys have shown more improvement, Norway exhibits minimal gender differences in performance at the lower secondary level. However, at the primary level, boys outperform girls in mathematics. Top performers in the TIMSS study include Singapore, Taiwan, and South Korea.

Norwegian students report feeling safe at school, with low instances of bullying. However, both students and teachers express less satisfaction compared to 2019. Consistent with other studies, TIMSS indicates that students who speak Norwegian at home and have parents with higher education and income levels tend to achieve better results.
