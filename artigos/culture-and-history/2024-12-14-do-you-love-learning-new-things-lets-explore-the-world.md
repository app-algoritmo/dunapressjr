## Transforming Education: Inspiring Students to Learn Beyond Grades

I am a science teacher with a deep passion for biology and years of experience in the field. Throughout my teaching career, I’ve observed that students often come to class with a genuine curiosity for learning. However, there’s a significant challenge in the learning process: many students lack commitment to their own education in the classroom.

Another pattern I’ve noticed in about 90% of my students is a fear of failing the school year. Their focus tends to revolve around grades, passing exams, achieving high scores, and outperforming their peers. This “immediate results-oriented” mindset reflects a broader trend in 21st-century life: an excessive focus on utilitarianism and materialism.

To clarify, I am not against striving for good grades or passing exams. My goal as a teacher is to inspire students to enjoy learning for its own sake. To achieve this, I demonstrate my enthusiasm for teaching and incorporate technical elements into the learning process.

In my lessons, I break down complex topics into simple, practical, and objective explanations. I aim to help students rethink the fundamental questions they often ask: Why do I go to school? What’s the purpose of learning this? Is school just about getting grades? Why is school so boring?

To answer these questions, I draw inspiration from Jesuit pedagogy and the teaching techniques of Nobel laureate Richard Feynman. The Jesuits successfully taught indigenous populations to speak Portuguese without prior knowledge of the language, while Feynman created an efficient learning methodology that made complex physics concepts understandable even to children.

Saint Thomas Aquinas emphasized the importance of a playful, informal approach to teaching, a method derived from the Latin term *ludus*—the root of the word “school.” Similarly, Alcuin of York in the 8th century used riddles and playful challenges, such as the famous puzzle involving a wolf, a goat, and a bundle of hay, to teach logic and ecological principles. This approach demonstrates how learning can be both engaging and culturally relevant.

I adopt similar techniques in my classroom. For example, I use games like Hangman to teach concepts such as types of rocks, trophic levels, celestial bodies, and parts of the digestive system. Every two weeks, I introduce dynamic activities to break the monotony of traditional lessons. One activity involves adapting a detective game to explain weathering and its impact on rocks—a lively way to blend scientific concepts with fun.

Feynman’s technique of simplifying concepts into small, digestible pieces is another cornerstone of my teaching. For instance, I explain the atom’s structure by comparing the nucleus to a small Earth and the electron cloud to its atmosphere. When students struggle, I use visual aids, such as crumpled paper, to clarify abstract ideas. This method ensures that even the most complex topics are accessible.

Combining these approaches, I contextualize learning within students’ cultural and daily experiences through debates, playful activities, and dynamic lessons. With additional resources, such as field trips or access to laboratories and libraries, this method could be even more effective.

In conclusion, the synergy of innovative techniques and diverse educational resources can transform the classroom, ensuring that 100% teaching results in 100% learning—driven by the active participation of students.
