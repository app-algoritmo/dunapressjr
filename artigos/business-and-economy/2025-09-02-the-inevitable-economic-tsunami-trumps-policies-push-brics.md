## 2025 Economic Tsunami: Trump’s Tariffs and BRICS Shift – Einar Tangen’s Insights

On September 2, 2025, as the world teeters on the edge of economic upheaval, a sobering conversation unfolded between Professor Glenn Diesen and Einar Tangen, a senior fellow at the Taihe Institute in Beijing and the Centre for International Governance Innovation (CIGI). Tangen, with his deep roots in investment banking and global economic analysis, painted a stark picture of an inevitable “economic tsunami” driven by U.S. President Donald Trump’s aggressive tariff policies. Over the past decade, the U.S. has increasingly alienated adversaries like Russia and Iran, pushing them into China’s orbit—a trend Trump once promised to reverse. Yet, in a surprising twist, his latest moves have doubled down, targeting not just foes but allies like India with 50% tariffs, inadvertently strengthening the BRICS bloc (Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa). As a father watching his children navigate an uncertain future, Tangen’s words resonate with a personal urgency, urging us to grasp how these policies are reshaping global alliances and economies.

Tangen traces this shift to a decade of U.S. missteps. The economic and military pressure on Russia and Iran has forged a strategic partnership with China, a dynamic Trump initially aimed to disrupt during his 2016 campaign, focusing solely on China. By 2024, however, his rhetoric shifted, branding BRICS a threat to the U.S. dollar. His plan to devalue the dollar by 40-60% to boost American manufacturing clashed with maintaining its status as the world’s trade currency—a contradiction Tangen deems unworkable. This tension escalated as BRICS nations, spooked by the weaponization of SWIFT and the seizure of Russian assets, began derisking from the U.S., seeking alternatives to dollar dominance.

The latest flashpoint is India. Trump’s threat of 50% tariffs, up from 25%, stunned a nation that saw itself as a U.S. ally. Tangen notes that Brazil and India, once hesitant to confront the U.S. during the recent BRICS meeting in Brazil, now face the fallout of their caution, alongside South Africa’s lingering humiliation from Trump’s treatment of President Ramaphosa. This aggression stems from domestic pressures: the Epstein case, dubbed “EPgate,” has fractured Trump’s MAGA base, with former allies like Steve Bannon and Laura Loomer turning critical. With markets faltering and his media profile tarnished, Trump’s tariff escalations are a desperate bid to shift headlines, a tactic Tangen sees as backfiring.

India’s response is telling. Prime Minister Narendra Modi, stung by perceived U.S. interference in his election and the Bangladesh “color revolution,” turned to Russia for support, embracing Putin. Trump’s claim of resolving the India-Pakistan conflict—repeated over 30 times and dismissed by Indian officials—adds insult to injury. The proposed U.S. energy deal with Pakistan, near insurgent-prone Balochistan, further alarms India, which relies less on Russian arms (down from 70% to 35%) and more on Western suppliers. Indian TV reflects public outrage, with commentators decrying U.S. humiliation and rejecting demands to open agricultural markets—a red line for Modi’s BJP, given farmers’ economic struggles and suicides.

Tangen frames Trump as an “irrational actor,” driven by domestic politics rather than geopolitical strategy. Mentored by Roy Cohn, Trump doubles down when cornered, a tactic that worked in New York real estate but falters in global diplomacy. His unpredictability—threatening tariffs one day, praising nations the next—erodes trust, a cornerstone of economics and politics. This approach, seen in demands to release Brazil’s Bolsonaro or force Russia into a ceasefire without settlement, ignores local realities, pushing nations toward diversification.

The economic stakes are dire. Tangen, drawing on his investment banking expertise, warns of a “tsunami” as stocks, bonds, and the dollar decline simultaneously—a rare sign of systemic instability. Trump’s withdrawal from the Trans-Pacific Partnership (TPP) and tariff barriers hurt Americans, especially the poor, while China’s $225 billion Belt and Road investment in green energy contrasts sharply. BRICS, representing 40% of global GDP, could counter with reciprocal tariffs on U.S. goods and services, forcing Trump to back down.

This push for diversification is BRICS’ core mission. India and Brazil’s initial reluctance to name the U.S. in trade disputes has shifted as tariffs hit home. Tangen envisions a united BRICS, potentially with Europe joining, challenging U.S. hegemony. Yet, the military card looms large—Trump’s nuclear submarine moves and rhetoric with Medvedev suggest desperation that could escalate conflicts.

China’s dilemma is poignant. As the U.S. sheds trust, China offers reliability, using rare earth leverage to deter tariffs. Its Phase One deal with Trump, now faltering, hints at a transactional Phase Two, keeping him in check. India’s oil purchases from Russia, tacitly allowed by the West, stabilized prices, a lesson in interdependence. The Nord Stream sabotage, mocked by U.S. officials, underscores this hypocrisy.

Tangen sees a wake-up call for India and others: diversify supply chains, push back on ultimatums, and unite. A united BRICS could force a U.S. attitude shift, though Trump’s crisis-driven change seems unlikely. As a global citizen, I feel the weight of this moment—nations seeking stability amid a superpower’s tantrum. Trump’s policies, meant to restore dominance, may instead hasten its decline, leaving us all to navigate the tsunami’s aftermath.
