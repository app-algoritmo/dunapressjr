## Norway's Screen Time Guidelines: Protecting Children's Digital Well-being

## Norway Charts a New Course for Childhood in the Digital Age

**A National Response to the Digital Dilemma Facing Modern Families** | **Screen time guidelines children**

In an unprecedented move, Norway has become one of the first nations to issue official, national guidelines on screen time and smartphone access for children and adolescents. Spearheaded by the Norwegian Directorate of Health and announced by Health Minister Jan Christian Vestre and Children and Family Minister Lene Vågslid, these evidence-based recommendations provide a clear, scientific framework for navigating the complex digital landscape that defines modern childhood. The guidelines, refined after receiving over 3,500 public consultations, represent a profound societal commitment to prioritizing children's long-term health and development over unchecked digital consumption.

## The Core of the Guidelines: From Infancy to Adolescence

The Norwegian Directorate of Health's guidelines are notable for their clarity and specificity, offering age-banded recommendations that provide concrete guardrails for parents.

For the youngest children, the advice is most stringent: **children under two years old should avoid screen time altogether**. The guidelines astutely note that adults caring for these children should also limit their own screen use in their presence, acknowledging the importance of undistracted, responsive caregiving. For preschoolers aged 2-5, leisure screen time is capped at a maximum of half an hour to one hour daily, with an explicit recommendation of "preferably less."

As children grow, the allotted time increases slightly but remains tightly bounded. Children aged 6-12 are advised to spend no more than one to one and a half hours on screens during their free time. Teenagers (13-18 years) have the most leeway, with a recommended limit of one and a half to three hours, yet the consistent mantra across all age groups is "preferably less." This graduated approach recognizes developmental differences while upholding the principle that **digital activities should not displace** essential pillars of childhood like physical play, face-to-face social interaction, and adequate sleep.

## A Landmark Recommendation on Smartphones and Internet Access

Perhaps the most groundbreaking and discussed aspect of the updated guidelines is the direct advice on smartphones. The government explicitly recommends that **parents delay and restrict children's access to internet-connected devices**, such as smartphones. The guidelines state that for safe use, adults should ensure children under 13 do not have unsupervised access to the internet, emphasizing the necessity of parental controls and active oversight.

!Screen time guidelines children

New national guidelines, shaped by extensive public consultation and scientific evidence, provide clear screen time limits and recommend delaying smartphone access to safeguard children's healthy development.

This recommendation directly addresses a core anxiety for many modern parents. Minister Vestre highlighted that the smartphone issue was a recurring theme in meetings with both parents and experts during the guideline development process. "Many are worried about the consequences screen time can have, especially when it comes to children's health," Vestre stated. This policy stance sends a powerful message: **childhood autonomy in the digital realm must be earned**, not assumed, and protection in early years is a parental and societal responsibility. As Children and Family Minister Lene Vågslid powerfully asserted, "Our children must not be left to algorithms and technology companies alone."

## The Foundational Principles of Healthy Digital Habits

Beyond time limits, the guidelines outline crucial qualitative principles for healthy screen use. These "rules of thumb" are designed to prevent digital devices from disrupting the natural rhythms of family life and child development. Key principles include:

- Not using screens as a tool to comfort or distract children from negative emotions.

- Keeping meals, including breastfeeding and bottle-feeding, screen-free to foster connection and healthy eating habits.

- Avoiding background TV or video, which can fragment a child's attention and reduce quality interaction.

- Implementing a "screen-free hour" before bedtime and not allowing phones or tablets in the bedroom, protecting critical sleep hygiene.

- Ensuring screen use never comes at the expense of physical activity, unstructured play, schoolwork, or social life.

The updated document also introduces a dedicated section on video games and gaming, acknowledging its unique role and potential risks, such as compulsive use and exposure to inappropriate content or interactions.

## The Science and Society Behind the Recommendations

These guidelines are not born of mere suspicion toward technology but are grounded in a growing body of empirical research. Studies consistently link **excessive screen time in young children** to delays in language acquisition, reduced attention spans, and poorer sleep quality. For adolescents, research points to correlations between high social media use and increased risks of anxiety, depression, and poor body image, particularly for girls.

The Norwegian government's process underscores its commitment to evidence-based policy. The initial guidelines, published in June of last year, were subjected to an extensive external consultation. The staggering response of over 3,500 submissions from the public, professionals, and organizations highlights how deeply this issue resonates. The final recommendations have been shaped by this national conversation and by new studies published since the first draft. This iterative, transparent process ensures the guidelines are not just top-down rules but a reflection of collective concern and evolving science.

## Implementing the Vision: A Collective Responsibility

!imagem

The publication of these guidelines is a starting point, not an endpoint. The real work lies in their implementation. The government frames this as a shared responsibility between parents, guardians, and authorities. The guidelines are intended as a supportive tool, not a punitive measure, to help adults make informed decisions in a commercial digital environment often designed to maximize engagement, not well-being.

For families, this means having more confident, guided conversations about digital house rules. For schools and municipalities, it provides a foundation for developing consistent policies. For technology companies, it serves as a clear signal about societal expectations regarding child safety and healthy product design. Norway's initiative provides a compelling, science-backed model for other nations grappling with the same universal challenge: how to harness the benefits of the digital world while firmly safeguarding the developmental needs of the next generation.
