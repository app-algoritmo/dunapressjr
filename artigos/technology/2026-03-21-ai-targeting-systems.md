## AI in Iran War 2026: How US-Israel Targeting Systems Like Maven & Claude Speed Up Strikes

In the dusty command centers of US Central Command and the high-tech war rooms of the Israel Defense Forces, a quiet revolution is unfolding. Officers no longer pore over stacks of printed intelligence reports for hours; instead, they query sophisticated AI systems that sift through terabytes of satellite imagery, intercepted communications, and drone footage in mere seconds. This is the reality of the 2026 US-Israel war against Iran—a conflict that began on February 28 with coordinated preemptive strikes codenamed Operation Epic Fury and Operation Roaring Lion. At its core lies artificial intelligence, not as a futuristic concept, but as a battle-tested tool that has already reshaped the tempo of warfare.

The human element remains at the heart of these operations. Behind every strike recommendation stands a team of analysts, ethicists, and commanders who bear the weight of life-and-death decisions. Yet the technology enabling them is unprecedented. The Pentagon’s Maven Smart System, integrated with Palantir’s AI platform, allows operators to process vast streams of battlefield data using large language models. Intelligence officers can ask natural questions—“Summarize recent movements near Natanz”—and receive instant insights drawn from classified satellite feeds and signals intelligence. US Central Command head Admiral Brad Cooper has publicly stated that these tools help warfighters “sift through vast amounts of data in seconds so our leaders can cut through the noise and make smarter decisions faster than the enemy can react.”

This acceleration of the “kill chain”—the sequence from target identification to strike—is no accident. In previous conflicts, such as the 1991 Gulf War or operations against mobile Scud launchers, analysts needed days or weeks to confirm targets. Today, AI compresses that timeline dramatically. Reports indicate that in the first 24 hours of the campaign, US and Israeli forces struck nearly 1,000 targets, a tempo impossible without automated assistance. Anthropic’s Claude AI model, despite the company’s public stance against fully autonomous lethal weapons, has reportedly been integrated into these workflows for optimizing target selection, analyzing imagery, and even simulating combat scenarios.

Israel’s contributions to this AI arsenal are equally significant. Systems like “The Gospel” (Habsora) rapidly generate targets for structural assets such as bunkers and command nodes by fusing drone footage with signals intelligence. “Lavender,” another proprietary platform, assigns suspicion scores to individuals based on digital footprints and behavioral patterns. These tools, honed during earlier operations in Gaza, have now been scaled for the Iranian theater. The result? Over 15,000 strikes since the conflict began, with an average exceeding 1,000 per day and remarkably low reported misidentification rates—only one public case to date.

Yet the human cost underscores the stakes. Iranian officials report over 1,300 deaths, including more than 400 women and children, with tragic incidents like the strike on an elementary school in Minab that claimed at least 168 young lives. US service members have also paid a price, with 13 confirmed fatalities. These numbers remind us that while AI enhances precision, the intelligence feeding it is only as accurate as the data it receives. In one documented case, outdated mapping failed to note that a school adjacent to a Revolutionary Guard base had been repurposed for civilian use years earlier. The AI recommended the strike; humans approved it. The fog of war persists, even in the age of algorithms.

On land, these systems shine brightest. Ground-based targeting in Iran’s rugged terrain relies heavily on AI-processed drone and satellite data. Israeli forces, operating under air superiority established early in the campaign, have deployed AI-equipped reconnaissance platforms to map underground facilities and mobile missile launchers. The technology’s ability to cross-reference behavioral data with real-time feeds has allowed for “decapitation” strikes, including the reported assassination of Supreme Leader Ayatollah Ali Khamenei in just 60 seconds—a feat a former Mossad operative described as operating at the “speed of thought.”

The broader implications extend beyond the battlefield. Logistics and maintenance, once labor-intensive, now benefit from AI predictive analytics. US forces use these models to schedule aircraft upkeep and optimize supply chains across the Persian Gulf, where bases in the UAE, Qatar, and Bahrain serve as critical hubs. Gulf nations themselves are not mere bystanders; their collaboration with US and Israeli tech firms has deepened through the Abraham Accords and subsequent agreements. The UAE and Saudi Arabia, investing billions in AI infrastructure, host data centers and R&D facilities that indirectly support allied operations—though this has made them targets for Iranian retaliation.

Critics, including ethicists and international law experts, worry about “decision compression.” When AI recommends strikes in seconds, human oversight risks becoming a rubber stamp. The US Department of Defense acknowledges this tension, maintaining policies that keep humans “always responsible over the use of force.” Yet the pace of operations—900 strikes in the first 12 hours—suggests the technology is pushing boundaries. As one expert noted in analyses of the campaign, speed wins wars, but at what cost to accountability?

Looking ahead, this conflict serves as a proving ground. Other nations, including those in the Gulf and beyond, are watching closely. China’s reported technical support to Iran highlights the global stakes, while US tech giants like Palantir and Anthropic navigate the delicate balance between innovation and ethics. For the soldiers on the ground, AI is not abstract code—it is the difference between mission success and ambush. For civilians in Tehran, Isfahan, or Bahrain, it represents a new era where algorithms influence survival.

The story of AI in this war is ultimately human: innovators in Silicon Valley and Tel Aviv crafting tools, commanders in CENTCOM weighing recommendations, and families on both sides living with the consequences. As the conflict enters its third week, one truth emerges clearly—artificial intelligence has not replaced human judgment, but it has irrevocably accelerated it. The world must now grapple with the moral and strategic ramifications of warfare at machine speed.
