## Ivermectin and Cancer: Can It Be a Cure? Scientific Insights and Challenges

Medical Consultation: Before considering any information from these investigations for health decisions regarding the off-label use of ivermectin, consult a qualified oncologist or medical professional.

Ivermectin, a well-established antiparasitic agent, has garnered attention for its potential anticancer effects based on preclinical studies. These investigations suggest mechanisms such as inhibition of cell proliferation, induction of programmed cell death, and reversal of drug resistance in various cancer types. However, clinical evidence remains limited, with no large-scale randomized controlled trials demonstrating efficacy in humans. This article reviews the scientific literature on ivermectin's anticancer potential, emphasizing the gap between laboratory findings and therapeutic application, while addressing controversies surrounding unsubstantiated claims of it being a "cure" for cancer.

Cancer remains a leading cause of mortality worldwide, prompting the exploration of repurposed drugs to augment existing treatments. Ivermectin, a macrocyclic lactone derived from avermectin, was originally developed for parasitic infections and earned its discoverers the 2015 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Beyond its antiparasitic role, emerging research indicates ivermectin may exert antitumor effects through multiple pathways. This review synthesizes scientific analyses from peer-reviewed studies, highlighting preclinical evidence, proposed mechanisms, and the paucity of human data. While promising, ivermectin is not currently approved or proven as a cancer treatment, and claims of it being a "cure" are unsupported by rigorous clinical evidence.

## Mechanisms of Action in Cancer

Ivermectin's antiparasitic activity stems from binding to glutamate-gated chloride channels in invertebrates, leading to paralysis and death of parasites. In mammals, it interacts with various targets, including the P-glycoprotein efflux pump and nuclear import proteins like importin α/β1. In cancer contexts, ivermectin modulates several oncogenic pathways:

- **Inhibition of Proliferation and Metastasis**: Ivermectin suppresses tumor growth by targeting the PAK1 kinase, which regulates pathways like Wnt/β-catenin, Akt/mTOR, and YAP1. This disrupts cell cycle progression, angiogenesis, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition.

- **Induction of Programmed Cell Death**: It promotes apoptosis via increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, Bax upregulation, and Bcl-2 downregulation. Autophagy is induced through PAK1/Akt inhibition, while pyroptosis may occur via the P2X4/P2X7/NLRP3 inflammasome pathway.

- **Reversal of Drug Resistance**: By inhibiting multidrug resistance proteins (e.g., P-glycoprotein), ivermectin sensitizes cancer cells to chemotherapy agents like doxorubicin or paclitaxel.

- **Immunomodulation**: Ivermectin induces immunogenic cell death (ICD), enhancing T-cell infiltration into tumors and shifting "cold" tumors to "hot" ones, potentially synergizing with immune checkpoint inhibitors like anti-PD-1.

These mechanisms have been observed primarily in cell lines and animal models, with effects varying by cancer type and dose.

## Clinical Evidence and Trials

Human data is sparse and inconclusive. A small observational study in Ecuador reported improved outcomes in cancer patients using ivermectin adjunctively, but lacked controls and randomization. Ongoing trials, such as NCT05318469 (ivermectin with pembrolizumab for metastatic triple-negative breast cancer), aim to evaluate safety and efficacy. A 2023 review of ivermectin in cancer patients found no safety concerns but emphasized the need for RCTs.

Anecdotal reports on social platforms, such as X (formerly Twitter), describe self-administration for cancer, often alongside fenbendazole, but these lack scientific validation and may promote misinformation. Experts caution against off-label use, noting risks like toxicity at high doses and delayed standard care.

## Controversies and Limitations

Claims that ivermectin "cures" cancer or is suppressed by authorities are unfounded and stem from misinformation amplified during the COVID-19 pandemic. While preclinical data is robust, translation to humans is challenged by:

- **Dose Discrepancies**: Effective in vitro doses (e.g., 2-10 μM) exceed safe human plasma levels (~0.05-0.2 μM).

- **Lack of RCTs**: No high-quality trials confirm benefits; most evidence is from cell/animal studies.

- **Bias and Misinformation**: Social media promotes unproven protocols, potentially harming patients by deterring evidence-based therapies.

- **Safety**: Generally well-tolerated, but high doses can cause neurotoxicity; interactions with other drugs are possible.

Regulatory bodies like the National Cancer Institute state there is no evidence ivermectin cures cancer in humans.

Ivermectin exhibits intriguing anticancer properties in preclinical models, warranting further investigation as an adjunct therapy. However, it is not a proven cure for cancer, and self-medication is inadvisable. Large-scale clinical trials are essential to validate its role, potentially integrating it with standard treatments like chemotherapy or immunotherapy. Until then, patients should rely on approved therapies and consult oncologists. The scientific community must combat misinformation to ensure evidence-based progress in oncology.
